# 1-5 Verb 101 & Present Tense Conjugation 대사 초안 v2

## Intro

We've used short replies to keep a conversation going. Now we're ready to build the present form of a Korean verb.

In "How Korean Works," we saw that the verb comes at the end and its ending carries several kinds of information. We also used a plant to picture those parts. Today, we'll use that picture to make the forms ourselves.

We'll start with actions and states. Then we'll find the stem, choose the ending, and see what happens when the sounds meet. After that, we'll look at 하다 and the formal present ending.

We'll use short conversations along the way. When an example has a part you haven't learned yet, I'll give you the meaning and point out the verb we're working on. You don't need to build every part of a long sentence today.

## Actions and states

Let's start with 공부하다, "to study," and 조용하다, "to be quiet."

Studying is an action. Being quiet is a state.

In English, think of "The cat is quiet" and "a quiet cat." The word "quiet" stays the same in those two expressions.

고양이 means "cat." In Korean, "The cat is quiet" is 고양이가 조용해요. "A quiet cat" is 조용한 고양이.

Listen to 조용해요 and 조용한. The part that describes the cat changes its form. We'll learn how to put a description before a noun later. For today, just hear those as two complete expressions.

At the end of a sentence, 조용하다 works as one verb meaning "to be quiet." That's why I'll call it a descriptive verb. 공부하다 is an action verb.

Here are their everyday polite present forms: 공부해요, "I study" or "I'm studying," and 조용해요, "It's quiet."

One describes an action, the other a state, and both end in 해요. We'll make that 하다 form later in this lesson. For now, notice that we can use the same kind of ending for both.

Imagine a classmate you've just met asks you in English, "Is the room quiet?"

You answer, "네, 조용해요."

The word describing the room has changed from 조용하다 to 조용해요. Let's work out how a dictionary form becomes something you can say.

## From the dictionary form to a reply

Look up 먹다, "to eat." Korean dictionary forms end in 다.

Remove 다 and you're left with 먹. That's the stem. It holds the basic meaning of eating.

In English, we have "I eat" and "she eats." Korean doesn't make that same change just because a different person does the action. We keep the stem and choose an ending for how we're using the verb.

Let's bring back my plant picture. "Stem" is a grammar term. The branch, leaf, and fruit are names I use to help you picture the ending.

The branch represents tense. For the basic present form we're making, we don't add a separate tense piece in that spot.

The leaf represents the context. The basic one we're learning is 아 or 어.

The fruit represents speech level. For everyday polite speech, we'll finish with 요.

So with 먹다, we find 먹, add 어, and then add 요: 먹어요.

Without that final 요, 먹어 is the casual form we're learning here.

A close friend you use 반말 with asks whether you're eating. You answer, "먹어."

Someone you're speaking politely with asks the same thing. You answer, "먹어요."

The idea of eating stays the same. The ending fits the way you're speaking to the other person.

This is different from the noun replies we learned before. With a verb, we first build the verb form. Then we can use 요 with that form.

Now, why do I call the leaf a context marker?

살다 means "to live." Its basic polite present form is 살아요. But you may also hear 살거든요 or 살잖아요.

Very roughly, 거든 can bring in a feeling like "just so you know," and 잖아 can bring in "you know." The exact feeling depends on the conversation.

You don't need to make those two forms today. I want you to see what that part of the ending can do. 아 and 어 give us the basic form we'll build on.

## Reading the present form in context

Before we work through the vowel choices, let's hear 먹어요 in a conversation.

Someone sees you eating and asks, "먹어요?"

That can mean "Are you eating?"

You reply, "네, 먹어요."

Now imagine you're talking about a food and whether you generally eat it. The person asks, "먹어요?"

You can still answer, "네, 먹어요."

This time, it can mean "Yes, I eat it."

The basic Korean form can describe an action happening now or something you do generally. The situation tells you which English wording fits.

Korean also has a separate form that focuses on an action in progress. We'll learn that later. Today, we're making this basic present form.

To choose 아 or 어, look at the last vowel in the stem.

If it's ㅏ or ㅗ, choose 아. For the other vowels in the basic pattern, choose 어.

Then check whether the stem has a final consonant, 받침. That helps you see whether the ending is added as another syllable or meets a vowel already there.

We'll use regular patterns and a few common special forms today. Other irregular forms have their own lesson.

## Adding 아 after a final consonant

닫다 means "to close."

Take off 다. The stem is 닫. Its vowel is ㅏ, so we choose 아.

닫 also has a final consonant. We add the 아 after it: 닫아. For polite speech, 닫아요.

Imagine you're by an open window. The person you're speaking politely with asks in English, "Are you closing it?"

You answer, "네, 닫아요."

Now, 놓다 means "to put" or "to put something down." Its stem is 놓. The vowel is ㅗ, so again, 아.

There is a final consonant, so we add 아: 놓아. With 요, 놓아요.

Someone sees you putting down a phone and asks, "놓아요?"

You answer, "네, 놓아요."

The vowel chose 아 in both examples. The 받침 is a separate thing we checked to see how that 아 joins the stem.

Keep those decisions separate: first the vowel, then the final consonant.

## When 아 meets another vowel

Let's change that second condition. What happens when there's no 받침?

가다 means "to go." Take off 다 and you get 가.

Its vowel is ㅏ, so the choice is still 아. But 가 already ends in that vowel sound. We don't need to repeat it as 가아 in the form we're using. We get 가, and with 요, 가요.

A classmate you've just met is getting ready to leave. You ask, "가요?" Are you going?

They answer, "네, 가요."

Now compare 보다, "to see" or "to watch."

The stem is 보. The last vowel is ㅗ, so we choose 아 again.

보 and 아 come together as 봐. With 요, 봐요.

You're talking about a film. One person asks, "봐요?" Are you watching it?

The other answers, "네, 봐요."

You chose 아 for both words. With 가, the repeated vowel stays as one. With 보, the vowels combine into 봐.

So 가요 and 봐요 aren't two new methods. They're the results of the same choice when the stem ends in a vowel.

## Choosing 어 and joining the sounds

Now let's return to 먹다. The stem is 먹, and its vowel is ㅓ. It isn't ㅏ or ㅗ, so we choose 어.

There's a final consonant, so we add it: 먹어. For polite speech, 먹어요.

Next, let's look at stems that end in a vowel.

서다 can mean "to stand" or "to stop." The stem 서 already ends in ㅓ. The everyday polite form is 서요.

켜다 means "to turn on." The stem ends in ㅕ, and the form is 켜요.

Here, adding another separate 어 would repeat the sound. Listen to the short forms: 서요, 켜요.

Now two forms to recognize with ㅐ and ㅔ.

보내다 means "to send." The stem is 보내, and we use 보내요.

세다 here means "to count." The stem is 세, and we use 세요.

You can hear those everyday forms without forcing a separate 어 into them: 보내요, 세요.

Imagine you're talking to a teacher about an email. The teacher asks, "보내요?" Are you sending it?

You answer, "네, 보내요."

The word "send" has the present form 보내요. We aren't changing the method because it looks shorter.

There are also vowels that combine with 어 into a different written shape.

고치다 means "to fix." Remove 다 and look at the last syllable, 치. Its vowel is ㅣ, so we choose 어.

고치 and 어 come together as 고쳐. Add 요: 고쳐요.

배우다 means "to learn." The last vowel in the stem 배우 is ㅜ. It also takes 어.

배우 and 어 come together as 배워. With 요, 배워요.

Listen to those sound combinations: 이어, 여. 우어, 워.

한국어 means "the Korean language." The complete sentence 한국어를 배워요 means "I learn Korean." That little 를 marks what you're learning. We'll study the marker itself separately. For now, focus on 배우다 becoming 배워요.

A classmate asks, "배워요?" Are you learning it?

You answer, "네, 배워요."

We've seen several different-looking results, but keep following the same route. Find the stem, choose 아 or 어 from its last vowel, and listen to how the sounds join.

## When the stem ends in ㅡ

Now there's a group where ㅡ gives way to the vowel we add.

바쁘다 means "to be busy." Its stem is 바쁘.

The ㅡ drops, and we look at the syllable before it. 바 has ㅏ, so we use 아. That gives us 바빠, and with 요, 바빠요.

예쁘다 means "to be pretty." Its stem is 예쁘.

Again, the ㅡ drops. The vowel in the previous syllable, 예, isn't ㅏ or ㅗ. So we use 어: 예뻐요.

Compare 바빠요 and 예뻐요. Both lose ㅡ. The earlier vowel helps us choose the new one.

Now take 끄다, "to turn off." Its stem is just 끄. There's no earlier syllable to look at.

For this form, ㅡ gives way to 어: 꺼. With 요, 꺼요.

A colleague asks in English, "Are you busy?"

You answer, "네, 바빠요."

Later, you're turning off a light. The colleague asks, "꺼요?"

You answer, "네, 꺼요."

In 바빠요, we looked back at 바. In 꺼요, there was no earlier syllable, and we used 어.

These examples give you the basic change to recognize. We'll handle other irregular patterns separately.

## 하다 and the forms that follow it

Now let's return to 하다, "to do."

하다 has its own common pattern. It uses 여 here: 하 and 여 give 하여. That usually shortens to 해 in the everyday form we're learning.

So the casual form is 해, and the polite form is 해요.

This also happens when 하다 is part of a longer verb.

공부하다, "to study," becomes 공부해요.

운동하다 means "to exercise." It becomes 운동해요.

And 조용하다, "to be quiet," becomes 조용해요.

That brings us back to our first comparison. Studying is an action; being quiet is a state. The 하다 part changes the same way in these present forms.

Listen to two classmates who use polite speech. One asks, "공부해요?" Are you studying?

The other answers, "네, 공부해요."

Later, they ask about the room: "조용해요?"

"네, 조용해요."

Now you can see how both answers were made.

You might wonder why 하다 uses this special form. It's natural to want a reason for every change. But you can learn to use a form before you know its history.

You already do that with English "go" and "went." You can use them without explaining why those two words belong together.

For 하다, understand the link from 하여 to 해, then get used to the present form 해요. That gives you something you can use across many words.

There's another common form to recognize: 되다, "to become," becomes 돼요.

We'll return to the different uses of 되다 in a later lesson. Here, just keep the dictionary word connected to its present form: 되다, 돼요.

## When you can separate a 하다 verb

With some action verbs, the part before 하다 is a noun.

공부 is "study" or "studying." In 공부하다, you're doing that activity.

You can keep it together as 공부해요, or separate it as 공부를 해요.

Imagine someone asks what you're doing. You can answer, "공부해요."

They ask again to check the activity. You can say, "공부를 해요."

Both tell them you're studying. The separated form gives that noun its own place in the sentence.

운동 is "exercise." So 운동해요 and 운동을 해요 work in the same kind of way.

The little 를 or 을 marks the activity as what you do. You can hear these as complete alternatives for now. We'll learn how those markers work in their own lessons.

The condition matters: the first part is a noun, and the whole verb describes an action.

친절하다 means "to be kind." Even though 친절 is "kindness," this verb describes a state. You don't make it 친절을 하다 to say someone is kind.

And 좋아하다, "to like," is built differently. The 좋아 part isn't a noun you can separate that way. You don't turn it into 좋아를 하다.

So when you meet a word ending in 하다, notice what kind of word it is before trying that separation.

## A short look ahead with 아 and 어

Let's look a little closer at 좋아하다.

좋다 means "to be good." In this explanation, think of something being likable.

The stem 좋 has ㅗ, so we get 좋아. Add 하다 and we have 좋아하다, "to like." Then the 하다 part takes the present form we just learned: 좋아해요.

싫다 describes something as undesirable or dislikable. With 어 and 하다, we get 싫어하다, "to dislike." Its polite present form is 싫어해요.

Imagine two people talking about a song. One asks, "좋아해요?" Do you like it?

The other answers, "네, 좋아해요."

If they don't like it, they might answer, "아니요, 싫어해요."

Today, you only need to see how the pieces connect in these examples. We'll come back to combined verbs in more detail.

아 and 어 also appear in forms that connect ideas. I'll give you the meaning of two complete sentences first, so you can listen for just the connecting part.

케이팝 means "K-pop," and you already heard 한국어, "the Korean language."

One person asks in English, "Why are you learning Korean?"

The other person wants to say, "I like K-pop, so I learn Korean."

In Korean, "저는 케이팝을 좋아해서 한국어를 배워요."

Listen to 좋아해서. The 서 after 좋아 helps link the reason to what follows.

Now the person is simply listing two things: "I like K-pop, and I learn Korean."

In Korean, "저는 케이팝을 좋아하고 한국어를 배워요."

Here, 고 attaches directly to the stem 좋아하. So these two connecting forms don't attach in exactly the same place.

You don't need to build connected sentences yet. This is a preview of why the basic forms you're learning will keep appearing. We'll learn the individual connectors later.

## Making a formal present statement

We've mainly used everyday polite 요 speech. Now let's use the same verbs for a more formal statement.

Remember the plant picture. This ending takes over the part where we had the basic 아 or 어 and the final 요.

Start from the stem again. For the examples we're using here, a stem with no 받침 takes ㅂ니다. A stem with 받침 takes 습니다.

Let's take 하다. The stem is 하, with no 받침. Add ㅂ니다: 합니다.

We start from 하, not from the everyday form 해요. That's why the formal form looks different.

Now compare 먹다. The stem is 먹, with a final consonant. Add 습니다: 먹습니다.

This time, the last vowel isn't choosing between 아 and 어. We're checking the final consonant of the stem to choose the formal ending.

Let's apply that to a few words we've already used.

보내다 has the stem 보내, with no 받침 at the end. It becomes 보냅니다.

세다 has the stem 세. It becomes 셉니다.

쓰다 means "to use" here. Its stem 쓰 also has no 받침, so the formal form is 씁니다.

And 고치다, "to fix," has the stem 고치. It becomes 고칩니다.

Now the stems with a final consonant.

We have 먹다, 먹습니다.

좋다, "to be good," becomes 좋습니다.

적다 here means "to write something down." Its stem 적 has a final consonant, so we get 적습니다. It's the write-down meaning we're using here.

Listen to 합니다 and 먹습니다 as whole words. The sounds run together when we speak. You don't need to stop between every written syllable.

Now let's hear why we'd choose one speech level over another.

A classmate asks, "공부해요?"

You answer, "네, 공부해요."

Later, you're giving a prepared update to a group. Someone asks in English what you're doing, and you answer, "공부합니다."

The studying hasn't changed. You're presenting it in a more formal style.

Or someone asks in English whether you eat breakfast. In an everyday polite exchange, you answer, "네, 먹어요."

In a formal exchange, that answer could be, "네, 먹습니다."

Both are polite. The setting helps you choose the style.

So even if the formal pattern looks easier to make, don't choose it only for that reason. The everyday 요 form will be useful in many conversations. You'll also hear people use both styles in the same setting.

And the formal ending is already complete. You don't need to add 요 after 합니다 or 먹습니다. The ending already gives you that formal polite style.

## Closure

Let's return to 먹다.

Take off 다 and you have the stem 먹. For the everyday present form, its vowel gives us 어: 먹어. Add 요 when you're speaking politely: 먹어요. For the formal statement, start from 먹 again and use 습니다: 먹습니다.

That's the main set of decisions we've been following. Find the stem, look at the sounds, and choose an ending that fits the conversation.

We used that same approach with actions and states: 공부해요 and 조용해요. We also saw how sounds join, and why 하다 gives us 해요.

You don't have to recall every example at once. Keep those connections clear as you review the forms. We'll keep building on them in the lessons ahead.
