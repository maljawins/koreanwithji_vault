# 1-5 Verb 101 & Present Tense Conjugation 대사 초안

## Intro

지난 레슨에서는 간단한 질문에 어떻게 대답을 하는지 배웠다. 이번 시간에는 드디어 한국어 동사에 대해 배워보는 시간을 갖도록 하겠다.

I talked about how Korean verbs work in pretty detailed fashion in the lesson "How Korean Works," so I highly recommend reviewing the verb part of that lesson before we get started.

You've seen how a Korean verb sits at the end of a sentence and changes its ending.

Until now, we've mostly looked at that shape from a distance. Today you'll make the present-tense form yourself. We'll find the stem, choose the sound that follows it, and finish with a speech level that fits the person you're talking to.

There's one difference from English to clear up first. If you hear “공부해요,” the person may be talking about studying in general, or about studying now. The situation tells you which English sentence fits. Korean also has a form that puts more attention on an action in progress. We'll learn that later. Today, stay with the basic present form.

We'll use the same method for doing words, like “study,” and describing words, like “be quiet.” Then we'll look at a few common sound changes, “하다,” and the more formal “ㅂ니다/습니다” ending. It is a lot of examples, but there is one route through them: find the stem, choose the ending, and choose your speech level.

Now, before we talk about how to conjugate verbs, there's something very important you should know.


## Adjectives are verbs

Adjectives are Verbs in Korean

That’s right. Adjectives ARE verbs in Korean.

Let me show you what I mean.

[Show - “The cat is quiet” VS “A quiet cat”]

In English, “quiet” doesn’t change no matter the usage. Now let’s look at Korean.

Show – 고양이: Cat / 조용하다: To be quiet

Show - 고양이가 조용해요 VS 조용한 고양이

As you can see, the part that corresponds to “quiet” changes its form in Korean.

This is why Korean adjectives are called “descriptive verbs” because they work exactly like verbs.

Let’s compare these two words.

[Show 공부하다: V. To study / 조용하다: Adj. To be quiet]

공부하다 means “To DO study”, it’s an action verb.

And 조용하다 means “To BE quiet”. And here, the state of being quiet itself is treated like one single verb.

But since both words end with 하다, they both conjugate exactly the same way in the present tense.

[Show 공부해요, I study / 조용해요, It is quiet.]

As you just saw, adjectives and verbs follow very similar conjugation rules in Korean. So I’m going to be using the term “descriptive verb” instead of “adjective” from here on out.


## Doing something and being a certain way

Let's start with two words. “공부하다” means “to study.” Someone is doing an action. “조용하다” means “to be quiet.” It describes a state. In English, “quiet” looks like an adjective. Korean treats that state as a kind of verb, so I'll call “조용하다” a descriptive verb. “공부하다” is an action verb.

Listen to these complete expressions. “고양이가 조용해요” means “The cat is quiet.” “조용한 고양이” means “a quiet cat.” The part that means “quiet” changes when it describes the cat directly. You don't need to make that form today. I just want you to see why I call it a verb: Korean changes its shape according to the job it does.

Now listen to “공부해요” and “조용해요.” The first is “I study” or “I'm studying,” depending on the situation. The second is “It's quiet.” One is an action, one is a state, and both use the same present-tense ending. That means you can learn one method and use it for both types.

Imagine two friends talking about a room. One asks, “조용해요?” Is it quiet? The other answers, “네, 조용해요.” You don't need to build the question yourself yet. Listen to the word “조용해요” in the answer. It describes the state of the room with the same ending that an action verb can use.

## The stem and the present-tense shape

Now let's build that ending. Find “먹다,” meaning “to eat,” in a dictionary. The “다” tells you you're looking at the dictionary form. Take “다” off, and you have “먹.” That's the stem. It carries the basic idea of eating.

I use a plant to picture what happens next. It's my way of organizing the parts, not a grammar term you need to memorize. The stem is already there. A branch can show tense. For this basic present form, that tense spot doesn't add a separate piece. Then comes a small ending, “아” or “어.” Finally, you choose how you speak to the other person. Add “요” for the everyday polite form. Without “요,” you have the casual form we're using here.

So “먹다” becomes “먹,” then “먹어,” then “먹어요.” To a close friend, “먹어.” To someone you're speaking to politely, “먹어요.” The meaning stays connected to eating. The ending makes it something you can say to a person.

I call that middle spot the context marker. “아/어” is the basic one we're learning. Other endings can add a feeling to what you say. For instance, you may hear “살아요,” “살거든요,” or “살잖아요,” all connected to “살다,” to live. The last two add a different conversational feeling. Don't try to make them yet. I want you to recognize why this basic “아/어” is the starting point.

Here's a small exchange. One person asks, “먹어요?” Are you eating? The other says, “네, 먹어요.” We're only listening for the verb in that reply. We will build Korean questions and the other parts of a full sentence later.

The key choice now is “아” or “어.” Look at the last vowel in the stem. If that vowel is “ㅏ” or “ㅗ,” start with “아.” For other vowels, start with “어.” Then check whether the stem ends in a final consonant, 받침. That tells you whether the new sound is simply added or joins the vowel already there. Let's take those steps one at a time.

## When the last vowel is ㅏ or ㅗ

“닫다” means “to close.” Remove “다”: “닫.” The vowel is “ㅏ,” and “닫” has a final consonant. Add “아,” and you get “닫아.” Add polite “요”: “닫아요.”

“놓다” means “to put something down.” Its stem is “놓.” The vowel is “ㅗ,” and there is a final consonant. So you add “아”: “놓아,” or “놓아요” when you're speaking politely. In both words, the vowel tells you to choose “아,” and the final consonant gives that “아” its own place.

Now compare “가다,” “to go.” The stem is “가.” Its vowel is “ㅏ,” so you choose “아.” But “가” already ends in that open vowel. Instead of saying “가아,” the spoken form is simply “가.” Add “요” and you get “가요.”

“보다” means “to see” or “to watch.” The stem “보” ends in “ㅗ.” Add “아,” and those vowels come together as “봐.” So the polite form is “봐요.” Notice the difference: “가” keeps one “ㅏ”; “보” and “아” meet in “봐.” The choice of “아” came from the same rule.

Let's use the words in a conversation. Two classmates who use polite speech are about to leave. One asks, “가요?” Are you going? The other answers, “네, 가요.” Later, one points to a film and asks, “봐요?” Are you watching it? The other answers, “네, 봐요.” Treat the questions as whole lines for now. Your job is to hear “가요” and “봐요” as the present forms of “가다” and “보다.”

## When the last vowel is something else

If the last vowel isn't “ㅏ” or “ㅗ,” start with “어.” We already did this with “먹다.” The stem “먹” has “ㅓ” and a final consonant, so “먹” plus “어” gives “먹어.” With “요,” “먹어요.”

Now let's see what happens when the stem ends in a vowel. “서다” can mean “to stand.” “서” already ends in “ㅓ.” The form you hear is “서요,” not “서어요.” “켜다” means “to turn on.” Its stem ends in “ㅕ,” and the spoken form is “켜요.” In these words, adding another separate “어” would repeat the sound, so the result stays short.

“보내다” means “to send.” Its stem is “보내.” You will usually hear “보내요.” “세다” can mean “to count.” You will usually hear “세요.” These are the everyday forms to practice here: “서요,” “켜요,” “보내요,” “세요.” Don't try to force an extra “어” between the stem and “요.”

Here's another exchange. Someone asks, “보내요?” Are you sending it? The other replies, “네, 보내요.” You can hear the whole answer without knowing how every part of a longer Korean sentence would work. Today the point is the verb “보내다” becoming “보내요.”

Two other vowels join “어” in a more visible way. “고치다” means “to fix.” Its stem ends in “ㅣ.” “고치” plus “어” comes together as “고쳐,” so you say “고쳐요.” “배우다” means “to learn.” The stem ends in “ㅜ.” With “어,” you get “배워,” then “배워요.”

Imagine a student says, “한국어를 배워요.” That means “I learn Korean.” The word “한국어” is Korean language. You don't need to sort out the object marker in that sentence today. Look at “배우다” turning into “배워요.” If a friend asks, “배워요?” the student can answer, “네, 배워요.”

Let's pause on the method. First look at the last vowel of the stem. “ㅏ” or “ㅗ” takes “아.” Everything else here starts with “어.” Then listen to what happens when two vowels meet. You may see “가요” rather than “가아요,” or “배워요” rather than “배우어요.” The written result can change, but you haven't changed the route you used to choose the ending.

## The ㅡ words

There's a group where the stem ends in “ㅡ.” Listen to three examples. “바쁘다,” to be busy, becomes “바빠요.” “예쁘다,” to be pretty, becomes “예뻐요.” “끄다,” to turn off, becomes “꺼요.” The “ㅡ” disappears as the new vowel comes in.

For these examples, look one syllable back when there is one. In “바쁘다,” “바” has “ㅏ,” so you hear “바빠요.” In “예쁘다,” “예” doesn't have “ㅏ” or “ㅗ,” so you hear “예뻐요.” With the short word “끄다,” the everyday form is “꺼요.” You don't need an entire catalog of “ㅡ” verbs right now. Learn to recognize this change in the words we just used.

A colleague asks in English, “Are you busy?” You answer, “네, 바빠요.” Later, the same colleague points to a light and asks, “꺼요?” Are you turning it off? You answer, “네, 꺼요.” In the first answer, “바쁘다” becomes “바빠요.” In the second, “끄다” becomes “꺼요.” Both answers fit the polite way you're speaking to this colleague.

## 하다 and words built with it

Now back to “하다,” to do. It is everywhere in Korean, so its present form is worth knowing well. “하다” becomes “해요” in everyday polite speech, and “해” in casual speech. You can think of “해” as the form you need here. You don't have to solve its history before you use it. One other common word, “되다,” can mean “to become.” Its polite present form is “돼요.” We'll come back to the different jobs “되다” can do later; for now, recognize that form when you hear it.

“공부하다,” to study, follows it: “공부해요.” “운동하다,” to exercise, becomes “운동해요.” A descriptive verb does it too. “조용하다,” to be quiet, becomes “조용해요.” This is why we began with action and state. The meaning differs, but the “하다” ending changes the same way.

Listen to two students. One asks, “공부해요?” Are you studying? The other answers, “네, 공부해요.” Later they enter a quiet room. One asks, “조용해요?” Is it quiet? The other says, “네, 조용해요.” The same “해요” appears in an action and a state.

With some action words, the part before “하다” is a noun. “공부” is study. You can say “공부해요,” and you can also say “공부를 해요.” Both talk about studying. “운동해요” and “운동을 해요” work in a similar way. For now, hear those as complete alternatives. The “를/을” in the separated version marks what someone does; we'll study those small markers in their own lesson.

Be careful with that shortcut. “친절하다” means “to be kind.” “친절” is kindness, but “친절을 하다” is not the normal way to say “be kind.” “좋아하다,” to like, also shouldn't be split into “좋아를 하다.” The action-noun pattern helps with words such as “공부하다” and “운동하다.” It isn't a rule for every word that ends in “하다.”

There is one more reason to notice “아/어.” It can help form longer verbs. “좋다,” to be good, leads to “좋아하다,” to like, and the present form is “좋아해요.” “싫어하다,” to dislike, becomes “싫어해요.” You only need to recognize those whole words today. We'll return to how combined verbs work later.

You can hear it in a short exchange about music. One person asks, “좋아해요?” Do you like it? The other answers, “네, 좋아해요.” Another person says, “아니요, 싫어해요.” No, I don't like it. Keep your ear on “좋아해요” and “싫어해요”; we aren't building every kind of combined verb in this lesson.

That same “아/어” shape can also appear before a piece that connects one thought to another. For example, “좋아해서” can help link a reason to what follows, while “좋아하고” uses a different connector for “and.” Hear those as two complete forms for now. We will learn how to build connected sentences later. Today they show you why the small ending you've been practicing will keep coming back.

## The formal present form

So far we've mainly used the everyday polite “요” form. Korean also has a more formal way to end a statement. You may hear it in an announcement or a prepared introduction. To make that form, the vowel doesn't choose “아” or “어.” Look at whether the stem has a final consonant, 받침.

If there is no 받침, add “ㅂ니다.” “하다” becomes “합니다.” “보내다” becomes “보냅니다.” “세다,” to count, becomes “셉니다.” “쓰다,” to use, becomes “씁니다.” “고치다,” to fix, becomes “고칩니다.” Listen to the stem: “하,” “보내,” “세,” “쓰,” “고치.” None ends in a final consonant, so they take “ㅂ니다.”

If the stem does have 받침, add “습니다.” “먹다” becomes “먹습니다.” “좋다,” to be good, becomes “좋습니다.” “적다,” to write something down, becomes “적습니다.” The last vowel isn't making the choice here. The final consonant is.

Imagine the same person speaking first to a classmate, then giving a prepared update to a group. The classmate asks, “공부해요?” The person answers, “네, 공부해요.” In the update, the person says, “공부합니다.” Both talk about studying. The second sounds more formal. In another exchange, a classmate asks, “먹어요?” The answer is “네, 먹어요.” A formal announcement might use “먹습니다.” These are different speech levels, so choose them for the setting rather than because one looks easier to form.

The formal ending is already complete. Don't tack “요” onto “합니다” or “먹습니다.” And don't assume every polite conversation needs to sound like an announcement. Everyday “요” speech is useful in many situations. You may also hear a speaker move between “요” and “ㅂ니다/습니다” in a formal setting. Listen to the relationship and the setting, then choose the tone you need.

## Closure

Let's return to that plant picture. Take the dictionary “다” off to find the stem. In this basic present form, the tense spot doesn't add a separate piece. The last vowel helps you choose “아” or “어,” and the sounds may join. Then your relationship with the listener gives you “요” or a casual form. For a formal statement, you use “ㅂ니다” or “습니다” according to 받침.

You can now see the same route in “먹어요,” “공부해요,” and “조용해요.” An action and a state can follow the same ending pattern. We've met a lot of forms today. Keep the order of decisions in mind: stem, ending, speech level. That will help you recognize the next verb you hear.
