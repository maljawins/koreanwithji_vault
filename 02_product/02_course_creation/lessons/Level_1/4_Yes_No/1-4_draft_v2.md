# 1-4 Yes & No 대사 초안 v2

## Intro

Last time, we learned to keep the right speech level even in a short reply. Today, we'll use that idea with yes and no.

You may already know 네 means yes. But imagine a teacher calls your name, explains something, and then says a word you don't catch.

You could respond with 네 in all three moments. You answer when you're called, show that you're listening, and ask the teacher to repeat. If you translate every one as English "yes," you'll miss what the student is doing.

We'll start with the basic forms. Then we'll follow those small words through a conversation and see how the situation changes their meaning.

As before, I'll give you the longer questions and situations in English.

## Choosing the form for the person

In 존댓말, a polite yes is 네. You can also say 예. Both are already polite, so you don't need to add 요.

예 can feel a little more formal. You'll hear 네 a lot more in everyday conversations, so that's the one I'll mainly use here.

With a close friend who uses 반말 with you, yes can be 응 or 어. They're both casual.

응 feels a little softer than 어. But the tone matters.

Let's keep the offer the same and change the person making it.

Someone you've just met offers you coffee: "Coffee?"

You want some, so you answer, "네."

Now your close friend makes the same offer. You answer, "응."

Both replies accept the coffee. The relationship changes the form you choose.

For no, use 아니요 in a polite conversation and 아니 in casual speech.

The person you've just met offers coffee. You don't want any, so you say, "아니요."

Your close friend asks, "Coffee?" You say, "아니."

Keep those pairs together: 네 and 아니요 for the polite conversation, 응 or 어 and 아니 for the casual one.

You may also have heard 아니에요. That is a different form, closer to "it isn't." We'll learn how it works in our lesson on 아니다, "to not be." For the basic no we're learning here, use 아니요.

One quick listening tip before we move on. A fast 네 can sound a little like 데 to an English-speaking ear. You're still hearing the word 네. Listen to the Korean sound and keep the Hangul connected to it.

## Following 네 through a conversation

Let's return to the teacher.

The teacher calls your name. You answer, "네."

In English, you might say "Here." This 네 tells the teacher you're there and you've heard them call you.

Then the teacher starts explaining something. At a pause, you respond, "네. 네."

Now you're showing that you're following. Think of "uh-huh" or "right" when you're listening in English.

That doesn't mean you agree with every idea the teacher says. You're letting them know you're still with them.

This is especially useful on the phone. The other person can't see you nodding. If you stay completely silent, they may wonder whether you're still listening or whether the call has cut out.

So when there's a natural gap, you can give a little 네. With a close friend, 응 can do the same job.

Responding as someone speaks can also sound attentive and respectful. You don't need to count how many times you say it. Follow the points they're making and the pauses in their speech.

You may hear a quick "네, 네" that feels like "Go ahead" or "Yes, carry on." And when something becomes clear, "아, 네" can feel like "Ah, I see."

The voice and the moment help you understand these. Two 네s don't always mean one fixed thing.

Now the teacher asks you to check something. You understand the request and answer, "네."

Here it means something like "Okay" or "Got it." A short 네 can work as a complete response to a simple request. Later, when we have more Korean, we'll also build longer answers for questions that ask for an opinion or an explanation.

Next, the teacher says something you don't hear clearly. You ask, "네?"

Your voice rises because you're asking them to repeat. In English, "Sorry?" or "Pardon?"

The teacher says it again, and you understand.

But suppose you heard the words perfectly and the news was surprising. You can also respond, "네?"

This time, it feels more like "What?" or "Really?" You heard them. You want to check that they really mean it.

You can also hear 네 when someone answers a call from a person they recognize. And near the end of a call, after they've finished talking, a longer 네 can help close the exchange.

Imagine someone you use polite speech with calls you. You pick up with "네?" During their explanation, you say, "네, 네." After they've finished and you're ready to hang up, you both say a final "네."

Same word, at different points in the conversation. Listen to what happened before it and what happens next.

## When 아니 opens the next thought

With no, there's a similar thing to notice.

When your friend offers coffee and you don't want any, "아니" is a straightforward no.

But 아니 can also come at the beginning of a thought to bring in a feeling. It might sound surprised, doubtful, frustrated, shy, or defensive. Sometimes it opens an explanation or gets the other person's attention.

We'll keep the Korean short. You don't need to work out the grammar of a whole sentence here. Listen to what 아니 is doing in the situation.

You know 이거, "this one," from the last lesson.

A friend points to an object you weren't expecting and asks, "This one?"

You react, "아니, 이거?"

It feels like "Wait, this one?" Your surprise comes through before you say much else.

Now, 지금 means "now." A friend tells you, "We have to leave now."

You weren't expecting that. You say, "아니, 지금?"

Again, there's that surprised "Wait, now?"

Keep the same little phrase, but change the situation. You've been waiting for your friend, and they finally say they're leaving home now.

"아니, 지금?"

This time, it can carry annoyance or frustration. You aren't learning a separate word for every feeling. The same 아니 takes on the feeling of the conversation.

Let's look at another short start: "아니, 그게..."

In these situations, 그게... leads into something like "the thing is..." We'll use it as a whole little phrase.

Your friend assumes you did something wrong. You want to explain what actually happened.

You begin, "아니, 그게..."

It can feel like "No, the thing is..." You're opening your explanation and pushing back on the assumption.

Now change the situation. You have an extra movie ticket and feel shy about asking your friend to come. They ask why you wanted to know their plans.

You hesitate: "아니, 그게..."

Same words, but you aren't defending yourself this time. You're trying to bring yourself to say something.

Or your friend asks why you didn't call. You need to explain what happened, so you start, "아니, 그게..."

That can lead into an excuse or some background. The words after it would tell your friend the reason. We don't need to build that longer Korean sentence yet.

There's one more small example. 잠깐 means "a moment" or "a second."

Your friend is about to leave, but you still have something to tell them. You say, "아니, 잠깐."

It's like "Wait, a second." You want their attention before you continue.

So when you hear 아니 at the beginning of someone's thought, listen past the English word "no." They may be showing how they feel, preparing an explanation, or asking you to listen to what comes next.

These uses will become more familiar as you hear them in context. For now, recognize the difference between refusing the coffee and opening a thought with a feeling.

## What you're agreeing with

There's one more situation where an English translation can get in the way: a negative question.

Imagine a Korean conversation. I'll give you the question in English: "Aren't you hungry?"

The idea inside that question is "You are not hungry."

First, suppose you're not hungry. That idea is right, so you answer, "네."

Here you're agreeing with "not hungry." Your answer means, "That's right, I'm not hungry."

Now keep the question the same, but change how you feel. You are hungry.

You answer, "아니요."

You're disagreeing with "You are not hungry." In this situation, your reply means, "No, I am hungry."

Let's hear those two exchanges with their meanings.

"Aren't you hungry?" "네." That's right, I'm not hungry.

"Aren't you hungry?" "아니요." No, I am hungry.

If numbers help you remember, rejecting a negative is like two negatives giving a positive. But start by understanding the idea in the question.

You're deciding whether you agree with "not hungry." That is why 네 doesn't always give you the same English answer you expected.

## Closure

Let's bring this back to our first conversations.

When someone offers coffee, your relationship helps you choose 네 or 응, 아니요 or 아니.

When the teacher calls, explains, or says something you miss, 네 changes its job with the moment: answering, listening, or asking them to repeat.

And 아니 can open a feeling or an explanation, as we heard in 아니, 지금? and 아니, 그게...

So keep the situation connected to the word. With a negative question, that includes the exact idea you're agreeing with or rejecting.

As you hear these small words in longer conversations, listen for what each person is doing with them.
