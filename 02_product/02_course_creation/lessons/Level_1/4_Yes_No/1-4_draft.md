# 1-4 Yes & No 대사 초안

## Intro

Last time, we looked at short answers. Even one word tells the other person how you're speaking to them. Today we need two words you will hear all the time: yes and no. The words are simple. What they do in a conversation is more interesting.

Imagine you're talking to someone you just met. They call your name. You say, “네?” They start explaining something. You say, “네, 네.” Then you realize you missed a word. You say, “네?” again. Same word, three different jobs. We'll start with the basic yes and no, then listen to what they can do in a real exchange.



## Yes and no for the person you're talking to

In polite speech, “yes” is “네.” You may also hear “예.” They both work as polite yes, though “예” can sound a little more formal. Most of the time in this lesson, I'll use “네.” It's already polite. You don't have to add “요.”

If you're talking to a close friend and you both use casual speech, you can say “응” or “어.” “응” can feel a bit softer. The relationship decides which set you use.

Let's hear the difference. Someone you just met asks in English, “Coffee?” You want some. You answer, “네.” Now your close friend asks the same thing. You answer, “응.” Both are yes. The way you speak to the other person has changed.

For “no” in a polite conversation, say “아니요.” With a close friend in casual speech, say “아니.” So the same coffee question could go like this. A person you just met asks, “Coffee?” You answer, “아니요.” Your friend asks, “Coffee?” You answer, “아니.”

You might recognize “아니에요” from somewhere else. It means something closer to “it isn't,” so don't use it as your basic word for no here. We'll build that form when we learn “아니다” later.

One small listening tip. When people say “네” quickly, it may sound a bit like “데” to an English-speaking ear. The word is still “네.” Trust the Hangul and listen again, rather than trying to spell that impression as a new word.

## What “네” can do

Now let's go back to the person calling your name. If a teacher is taking attendance and calls you, “Ji?” you can answer, “네.” In English you might say “Here.” Here, “네” tells the teacher you're there.

Suppose a coworker is explaining a plan to you. The coworker talks; you listen and say, “네. 네.” You're not saying yes to every fact in the plan. You're showing that you're following. With a friend, you might give the same listening signal with “응. 응.”

Then the coworker asks you to check something. You understand and answer, “네.” Here it feels like “Okay, got it.” A short “네” is a natural answer to a clear yes-or-no question or a simple request. If someone asks for your opinion or an explanation, you can add more to your answer. We aren't learning those longer answers yet.

You may hear “네” at the start or end of a phone call too, especially when the people know each other. Imagine a friend calls and you already know it's them. You pick up: “응?” A little later, as you finish the call, “응, 응.” With someone you're speaking to politely, that could be “네” and “네, 네.” The voice and the moment tell you whether it's greeting, listening, or closing.

Now one of the most useful versions. The coworker says something, but you miss it. You respond with a rising “네?” That means “Sorry?” or “Could you say that again?” If what you heard surprises you, the same rising “네?” may sound more like “Really?” You don't need a new word. Listen to the situation and the voice.

Let's put those three moments together. The teacher calls your name. You answer, “네.” The teacher explains something. You answer, “네, 네.” Then you miss a word. You ask, “네?” The first is a response to being called, the second shows you're listening, and the last asks the teacher to repeat. That's why translating every “네” as English “yes” will sound strange.

## When “아니” starts the conversation

“아니요” and “아니” can give a straightforward no. Someone offers coffee; you don't want any. To someone you're speaking to politely: “아니요.” To a close friend: “아니.”

But you will also hear “아니” at the beginning of a thought when the person is not simply answering a yes-or-no question. It can carry surprise, doubt, frustration, or the feeling of “Wait.” It can also lead into an explanation, like “I mean,” or get the listener's attention before a new point. The feeling depends on the situation and the voice. There isn't one English word that fits every time.

Let's keep the Korean very small. You know “이거요?” from the last lesson: “This one?” With a close friend, the short casual version is “이거?” You don't need to work out the grammar of the words around “아니” here. Just listen to its feeling.

A friend points to the wrong item and asks in English, “This one?” You're surprised. You point elsewhere and say, “아니, 이거?” Here “아니” gives the little “Wait, no” feeling before you point to the one you mean.

Now a friend says in English, “We need to leave now.” You weren't expecting that. “지금” means “now.” You say, “아니, 지금?” It sounds like “Wait, now?” The surprise is the point.

One more. A friend asks why you changed your mind. You want to explain, but need a second to find the words. You start, “아니, 그게...” It feels like “No, I mean...” The next part would be your explanation. You don't need to build it in Korean yet. Hear how “아니” opens the explanation instead of closing the conversation with a no.

Now imagine that same friend is about to walk away while you still have something to say. “잠깐” means “a moment.” You say, “아니, 잠깐.” It gets their attention: “Wait, a second.” This use can sound surprised or urgent, depending on your voice. We aren't assigning a fixed English translation to “아니” in every scene.

You will hear other shades of this in real conversations. Sometimes the person is surprised. Sometimes they're defending an idea. Sometimes they just want you to listen to what comes next. Don't try to memorize a separate translation for every mood. Listen for what happened right before “아니” and what follows it.

## A yes that seems to mean no

There's one situation that can catch English speakers. Imagine someone asks, in English, “Aren't you hungry?” We're keeping the question in English because you haven't learned to build that Korean question yet.

What idea is inside the question? “You are not hungry.” In Korean, if you agree with that idea, you can answer “네.” In this situation, “네” means, “That's right, I'm not hungry.”

If you disagree, answer “아니요.” Here it means, “No, I am hungry.” Let me say those two answers with their meanings again. “Aren't you hungry?” “네.” I'm not hungry. “Aren't you hungry?” “아니요.” I am hungry.

This can sound backwards if you're waiting for the English yes or no to tell you whether the person is hungry. Listen to the statement in the question. Are you agreeing with “not hungry,” or rejecting it? In a real conversation, you can add an explanation so nobody has to guess. We don't need the Korean sentence for that explanation yet.

## Closure

You now have a small set of answers for two kinds of relationship: “네/예” and “아니요” for polite speech, “응/어” and “아니” for casual speech. “네” also tells someone you're listening, answers when they call you, or asks them to repeat when your voice rises. “아니” can start a thought with surprise or frustration. And with a negative question, listen to the idea you're agreeing with before you answer.

As you learn longer Korean answers, keep listening for these small words. They'll tell you whether the other person agrees, is listening, or wants you to say something again.
