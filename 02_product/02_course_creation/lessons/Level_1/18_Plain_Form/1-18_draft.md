# 1-18 Plain Form · Sample Draft

> 샘플 v1 · Ji 검수 전. 영어 설명과 한국어 예문이 발화 대사다. 굵은 괄호의 CLEAR·4MAT 주석과 마지막 제작 메모는 읽지 않는다.

## Intro

**(4MAT Why + What / CLEAR Calibrate: 사전형처럼 들리는 실제 발화에서 출발해, 형태와 쓰임을 함께 배울 이유를 연다.)**

You might hear someone say, “아, 배고프다,” meaning “Ah, I’m hungry,” and think, “Wait. Isn’t that the form I’d find in a dictionary?”

For 배고프다, yes. But what you’re hearing is a complete sentence in the plain form.

And this is where it can get confusing. Some words look exactly like their dictionary forms. Others change. And knowing how to make the form doesn’t automatically tell you when to use it.

So, in this lesson, we’re going to connect those two things: how the plain form is used, and how to make it in the present and past tense.

Let’s start with when you’d actually use it.

## Talking to yourself

**(4MAT How + Why / CLEAR Link: 영어로도 혼자 “Oh, that’s good”이라고 말하는 익숙한 경험을 한국어 혼잣말에 연결한다.)**

Imagine you take a bite of something, and it’s really good.

Before you even think about telling anyone, you say:

아, 맛있다.

“Oh, this is good.”

맛있다 means “to taste good.” Here, you’re just reacting out loud. You’re not addressing anyone, so you don’t need an ending that shows politeness to a listener.

That’s one of the most common ways you’ll hear the plain form: talking to yourself.

It’s similar to saying “Oh, that’s good” in English, even when nobody’s there to hear you.

**(4MAT What if + Why: 같은 음식·같은 평가를 유지하고, 발화 대상을 혼자에서 식당 직원으로 바꾼다.)**

Now imagine a server comes over and asks:

맛있어요?

“Does it taste good?”

This time, you’re answering the server, so you say:

네, 정말 맛있어요.

“Yes, it’s really good.”

That 요 shows politeness to the server. You didn’t need it when you were just reacting to the taste yourself.

So, hearing someone say 맛있다 doesn’t mean you can start saying it to anyone you meet.

And you don’t have to be completely alone to talk to yourself. You could be walking outside and mutter, “아, 춥다,” meaning “Ah, it’s cold,” while other people happen to be nearby.

But it needs to be clear that you’re talking to yourself. If you’re making a comment TO someone, use the speech level that fits your relationship with them.

## Letting a friend know

**(4MAT What + How + Why: 혼잣말과 달리 상대가 있는 사용을 따로 연다. 반말이 가능한 관계와 알리는 느낌을 함께 설명한다.)**

The plain form can also be directed at someone. When that happens, it’s 반말.

A common example is letting a close friend know that you’re leaving.

You’ve been hanging out together. You pick up your bag and say:

나 간다.

“I’m leaving.”

And your friend says:

응, 잘 가!

“Okay, bye!”

나 means “I.” 간다 comes from 가다, “to go.” We’ll look at how that change works in a moment.

Here, 나 간다 has the feeling of an announcement. You’re letting your friend know, “Okay, I’m heading off.”

Think of it as a little “Just so you know” to the people around you. You don’t have to shout or make a formal announcement.

But it still counts as 반말. So, this is for someone you already speak casually with.

You can also say 나 가 to a friend. The plain form gives you another way to deliver that announcement. It doesn’t replace all your usual casual endings.

And for the plain-form sentences we’re making, use 나 for “I,” rather than the humble 저.

## Plain form in writing

**(4MAT What + Why / CLEAR Link: 상대에게 답하는 말과 일기·일반 서술의 차이로 쓰기 용법을 연결한다.)**

So far, we’ve looked at speaking. But the plain form is also very common in writing.

You’ll see it in things like diary entries, news articles, and essays.

When you write “I’m hungry” in your own diary, you’re not having a polite conversation with a reader. You can simply write:

배고프다.

“I’m hungry.”

In an article, the same kind of ending lets the writer present information without speaking directly to one particular person.

That doesn’t mean every piece of Korean writing has to use the plain form. A message to your teacher, for example, is still a message TO your teacher. You’d choose an appropriate speech level.

This also helps explain why you may see the plain form labeled “Formal Low.”

“Formal” here does not mean “polite.” When you use this form toward another person, it belongs on the 반말 side. In writing such as a news article, you’re presenting information, so it doesn’t read like you’re being rude to each reader.

For now, focus on those uses rather than the label: writing, talking to yourself, and making a casual announcement.

Now let’s look at how to make the form.

## Present tense: action verbs

**(4MAT What + How / CLEAR Envision 제안: 사전형 → 어간 → Plain form의 변화를 나란히 보여준다. 대사에서도 각 변화를 설명한다.)**

First, we need to separate the dictionary form from the plain form.

The dictionary form is how a word is listed. For example, 가다 means “to go,” and 먹다 means “to eat.”

To make a present-tense statement in the plain form, an action verb needs a different ending.

Take 가다. Remove 다, and you have the stem, 가.

Because 가 ends in a vowel, attach 니은 as the 받침, then add 다.

가 becomes 간, and then 간다.

That’s the form we just heard in 나 간다, “I’m leaving.”

하다 works the same way. Its stem is 하. Add the 니은 받침 and 다, and you get 한다.

**(4MAT What if + How: 동사 종류와 시제는 유지하고, 어간 끝이 모음에서 자음으로 바뀔 때의 적용을 비교한다.)**

With 먹다, the stem is 먹. It already ends in a consonant, so this time you attach 는다.

먹, 는다. 먹는다.

So, for action verbs in the present tense, look at the end of the stem.

After a vowel, ㄴ다. After a consonant, 는다.

There’s one adjustment for stems ending in 리을, ㄹ.

Take 살다, “to live.” Its stem is 살. Remove the 리을, and you’re left with 사. Then add the 니은 받침 and 다.

살다 becomes 산다.

You’ll see the same change in 놀다, “to play”: 놀다 becomes 논다.

So, don’t put 는다 after that 리을. Remove it first.

## Present tense: descriptive verbs

**(4MAT What + How + Why / CLEAR Link: 영어의 adjective에 대응하는 descriptive verb를 짧게 상기하고, 종류 구분이 실제 활용 규칙을 바꾼다는 점을 보여준다.)**

Now, this is where the difference between action verbs and descriptive verbs matters.

Action verbs describe doing something, like eating or going. Descriptive verbs describe a state, like being good, cold, or tired.

These are the words we’d usually call adjectives in English. But in Korean, they work as verbs, so they can carry endings and finish a sentence.

In the present plain form, a descriptive verb looks exactly like its dictionary form.

좋다, “to be good,” stays 좋다.

춥다, “to be cold,” stays 춥다.

And 맛있다, “to taste good,” stays 맛있다.

That’s why the reaction we started with was simply:

아, 맛있다.

There’s no 니은 to add. The stem 맛있 takes 다, and you get 맛있다.

**(4MAT What if + How: 하다 또는 ㄹ이라는 겉모양만 보고 규칙을 적용하지 않도록 동사 종류를 바꿔 비교한다.)**

Here’s something to watch out for. Ending in 하다 doesn’t automatically make a word an action verb.

공부하다 means “to study.” That’s an action, so its plain form is 공부한다.

피곤하다 means “to be tired.” That’s a state, so it stays 피곤하다.

Both end in 하다 in the dictionary, but they don’t follow the same present-tense rule.

The same distinction matters with 리을.

살다, “to live,” is an action verb, so it becomes 산다.

멀다, “to be far,” is descriptive, so it stays 멀다. We don’t remove its 리을.

So, check the type of verb FIRST. Then choose the ending.

This is why action verb and descriptive verb are useful categories. They tell you how the word behaves when you start changing its form.

## Present tense: 이다

**(4MAT How + What if: 명사+이다를 동사 활용과 구분하고, 명사 끝의 받침 유무에 따른 모양을 비교한다.)**

There’s one more case: 이다, the form we use to say that something IS a noun.

책 means “book.” Add 이다, and you get 책이다, “It’s a book.”

커피 means “coffee.” You can say 커피이다, but after a vowel, 이다 can shorten to 다. So you’ll commonly hear 커피다.

Imagine you pick up a cup, expecting tea, then realize what’s inside:

아, 커피다.

“Oh, it’s coffee.”

You’re identifying the drink to yourself.

After a consonant, keep 이다: 책이다.

After a vowel, you can shorten it: 커피다.

So we now have our three present-tense patterns.

Action verbs change: 가다 becomes 간다, and 먹다 becomes 먹는다.

Descriptive verbs keep the same form: 좋다 stays 좋다.

And with a noun, use 이다, which can shorten to 다 after a vowel.

## Past tense

**(4MAT How / CLEAR Calibrate + Link: 과거 어간에 남는 ㅆ을 짚고 끝을 바꾸는 과정을 설명한다. 과거 활용 전체를 새로 강의하지 않는다.)**

For the past tense, we don’t need that separate ㄴ다 or 는다 rule for action verbs.

Instead, we keep the past-tense stem and add 다.

Let’s use 먹었어요, “I ate.”

먹었 is the past-tense stem. Notice the 쌍시옷, ㅆ, in 었. That part tells us we’re talking about the past.

Take off 어요 and put 다 in its place:

먹었어요 becomes 먹었다.

하다 works the same way. 했어요, “I did,” becomes 했다.

And 가다: 갔어요, “I went,” becomes 갔다.

Be careful here: we’re not just removing 요. 먹었어요 without 요 would be 먹었어, the usual casual form.

For the plain form, we take 먹었 and add 다: 먹었다.

**(4MAT What if + How: 동작에서 상태로 바꿔도 과거 어간+다라는 적용 방식이 유지됨을 확인한다.)**

Descriptive verbs follow the same pattern in the past.

좋았어요, “It was good,” becomes 좋았다.

피곤했어요, “I was tired,” becomes 피곤했다.

So, compare these two:

피곤하다. “I’m tired.”

피곤했다. “I was tired.”

The present descriptive form stays the same as the dictionary form. But when we talk about the past, we still need the past-tense part.

With nouns, you’ll use 이었다 or 였다.

책이었다 means “It was a book.”

커피였다 means “It was coffee.”

These come from 책이었어요 and 커피였어요. Again, keep the past-tense stem and change the ending to 다.

**(4MAT How / CLEAR Link: 앞에서 설명한 일기라는 쓰임으로 돌아와 과거형을 실제 문장에 적용한다.)**

Now we can use that in a short diary entry.

만나다 means “to meet.” Its past form is 만났어요, so the plain form is 만났다.

어제 친구를 만났다.

“I met a friend yesterday.”

어제 means “yesterday,” and 친구를 is “a friend” as the person you met.

The ending 만났다 records what happened. You’re writing down your experience, rather than answering someone politely.

## Starting a story

**(4MAT What if + Why: 바로 앞의 과거 문장을 유지하고, 일기에서 친구에게 이야기를 여는 말로 바꾼다. 올라가는 억양과 실제 질문을 구분한다.)**

And there’s one more spoken use I want to show you with that same sentence.

Imagine you’re talking to a close friend and you have a story to tell.

You could start with:

나 어제 친구를 만났다?

“So, I met a friend yesterday, right?”

Your friend responds:

어, 그래?

“Oh, really?”

Then you continue:

같이 커피 마셨어.

“We had coffee together.”

And you go on with the rest of the story.

Notice the rising tone on 만났다?

You’re not asking your friend whether you met someone. You already know that you did. You’re getting their attention before you tell them what happened.

English does something similar with “So, I met a friend yesterday, right?” That little “right?” invites the other person to follow the story.

This is a way you may hear the plain form used in casual conversation. It doesn’t mean you can turn every plain-form statement into an ordinary question by using a rising tone.

And notice that the next sentence, 같이 커피 마셨어, uses the usual casual ending. You don’t have to keep every sentence in the plain form once you’ve used it.

## Closure

**(CLEAR Abstraction + Repeat: 형태 선택과 사용 상황을 다시 묶고, 도입의 사전형 혼동과 혼잣말 장면을 회수한다.)**

All right, let’s bring this back together.

The plain form is something you’ll see in writing and hear in speech, especially in self-talk, casual announcements, and the start of a story.

When you’re speaking TO someone, remember that it’s 반말. Knowing the ending doesn’t replace knowing who you’re speaking to.

To make present-tense statements, check the type of word.

For action verbs, use ㄴ다 or 는다, with the 리을 adjustment we practiced.

For descriptive verbs, keep the dictionary form.

For nouns, use 이다, or 다 after a vowel.

In the past tense, keep the past-tense stem and add 다: 먹었다, 좋았다, 커피였다.

So, when you hear “아, 맛있다,” you can now recognize both the form and what the speaker is doing with it.

They’re expressing a reaction to themselves. When they turn to answer the server, it becomes 맛있어요.

And these plain forms will come up again when we learn how to report what someone said or express a thought. So, keep the action and descriptive patterns clear. We’ll be building on them.

I’ll see you in the next lesson.
