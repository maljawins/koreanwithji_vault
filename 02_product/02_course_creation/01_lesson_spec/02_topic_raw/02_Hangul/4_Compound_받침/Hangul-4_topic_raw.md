**Recap**

So far, we've learned how 한글 works, the sounds of consonants, vowels, and compound vowels.

We covered basic consonants, aspirated consonants, and double consonants—which are the amplified sounds of ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ, ㅅ, ㅈ. Right?

And then we learned consonant assimilation—how sounds change with amplifiers and nasal consonants.

**Now Compound 받침 is** the final part of learning Hangul. After this, you'll be done with Hangul!

**Compound 받침 인트로**

There are **11 compound 받침**, but don't worry—they're easier than they look.

ㄳ ㄵ ㄶ ㄺ ㄽ ㄾ ㅀ ㅄ ㄻ ㄼ ㄿ

First, you can ignore **ㄽ** because it’s only used in old Korean. I can't even think of any word that has this 받침, so you can forget about it. That leaves us with **10 compound 받침**:

ㄳ ㄵ ㄶ ㄼ ㄾ ㅀ ㅄ ㄺ ㄻ ㄿ

I’ll make something clear. These only appear in **받침** positions, meaning you’ll never see them at the beginning of a syllable.

**Dominant letter of compound 받침**

Every compound 받침 has one **dominant letter**.

Except for **ㄺ, ㄻ, and ㄿ**, the **first letter** is the dominant one. And when there’s only one syllable, you only pronounce the dominant letter.

Here are some examples:

**First Letter Dominant:**

* ㄳ → 넋 is pronounced 넉
* ㄵ → 앉 is pronounced 안
* ㄶ → 않 is pronounced 안
* ㄼ → 넓 is pronounced 널
* ㄾ → 핥 is pronounced 할
* ㅀ → 잃 is pronounced 일
* ㅄ → 값 is pronounced 갑

**Second Letter Dominant:**

For ㄺ, ㄻ, and ㄿ, the second letter is dominant:

 ㄺ → 읽 is pronounced 익

 ㄻ → 삶 is pronounced 삼

 ㄿ → 읊 is pronounced 읍

Remember? ㅍ 받침 makes a ㅂ sound.

**Consonant assimilation of compound 받침**

When these 받침 are followed by other consonants, the same consonant assimilation rules apply.

The only difference is that, no matter which letter is dominant, all compound 받침 act as either amplifiers or aspirators.

1. **When followed by ‘ㅇ’ silent letter**

Let’s first look at the easiest rule. If the next syllable starts with ‘ㅇ’, the second letter in the compound 받침 moves to the next syllable.

넋이 > 넉시
앉아 > 안자
않아 > 아나 – (ㅎ goes silent when followed by ㅇ, remember?)
넓어 > 널버
핥아 > 할타
잃어 > 이러 – Again, ‘ㅎ’ followed by ‘ㅇ’ turns silent
값이 > 갑시

1. **When followed by non-silent consonants**

When followed by other consonants, all you need to do is apply the usual consonant assimilation rules to the dominant letter.

However, compound 받침 always act as either amplifiers or aspirators.

**넋만 → 넉만 → 넝만**

The dominant letter here is ㄱ.

Remember how ㄱ 받침, when it meets a nasal consonant, becomes ㅇ (ng sound)?

So this is pronounced 넝만.

**앉다 → 안따**

The dominant letter of ㄵ 받침 is ㄴ.

But although ㄴ by itself is not an amplifier 받침, the ㄵ compound 받침 amplifies 다 to 따.

So this is pronounced 안따.

**않고 → 안코**

Remember ㅎ, the aspirator? The ㅎ in ㄶ aspirates 고 to 코.

**넓다 → 널따**

Again, ㄹ is the dominant letter, but it amplifies 다 to 따.

**넓혀 → 널펴**

The ㅂ sound combined with the aspirator ㅎ in the following consonant makes a ㅍ sound.

So this is pronounced 널펴.

**핥다 → 할따**

Again, 다 is amplified to 따.

**잃지 → 일치** The ㅎ in ㅀ aspirates 지 to 치. So this is pronounced 일치.

**값도 → 갑또** 도 is amplified to 또.

**값만 → 갑만 → 감만**

Remember when ㅂ 받침 is followed by ㅁ, it naturally turns into ㅁ? So this is pronounced 감만.

**읽는 → 익는 → 잉는**

Here, the dominant letter ㄱ is followed by ㄴ—a nasal consonant. So this is pronounced 잉는.

**Examples with Second Letter Dominant:**

Now let's look at the compound 받침 where the second letter is dominant:

**읽다 → 익따**

The dominant letter is ㄱ, and it amplifies 다 to 따.
**삶다 → 삼따**

The dominant letter is ㅁ, but again, 다 is amplified to 따.

**읊다 → 읍따**

ㅍ is the dominant letter, and ㅍ in 받침 position turns into ㅂ sound.

So this is pronounced 읍따. On a side note: This is the only word that has ㄿ 받침.

**The Exception: 닭 (Chicken)**

There's one exception you need to know. In the word 닭, the ㄹ sound is completely silent.

**Congratulations**

Now you learned everything you need to know about Hangul and pronunciation rules.

In our next lesson, I’ll show you the most effective method to memorize Hangul.

### Constructing Syllables with Four Letters
Korean syllables can contain up to four letters: one initial consonant, one vowel, and two final consonants (known as a compound 받침). The instructor explains that the layout of these blocks depends entirely on whether the vowel is vertical or horizontal.

For vertical vowels like **아, 야, 어, 여**, the initial consonant is placed on the left, the vowel on the right, and the two final consonants are placed side-by-side at the very bottom. This creates a square-like arrangement. An example shown is **앉 (from 앉다, to sit)**, where **ㄴ** and **ㅈ** sit together at the base.

For horizontal vowels like **오, 요, 우, 유, 으**, the initial consonant sits on top, the vowel is in the middle, and the two final consonants are placed side-by-side at the bottom. The instructor demonstrates this with the block **긁 (from 긁다, to scratch)**, showing the **ㄹ** and **ㄱ** next to each other under the vowel **으**.

---

### Pronouncing Compound Final Consonants
When a syllable with two final consonants is followed by a syllable starting with a consonant (not the empty ㅇ), usually only one of the two consonants is pronounced. The instructor focuses on a common pattern involving the letter **ㄹ (L/R)**. In many compound combinations like **ㄹㅁ, ㄹㄱ, ㄹㅂ, ㄹㅍ**, the **ㄹ** is silenced if it is not followed by an empty ㅇ.

The instructor provides specific examples of this "disappearing ㄹ" rule:
- **삶 (Life)** is pronounced as **삼**
- **흙 (Soil/Dirt)** is pronounced as **흑**
- **읽 (Read)** is pronounced as **익**
- **밟 (Step on)** is pronounced as **밥**
- **옮겨 (Move)** is pronounced as **옴겨**

However, the instructor notes a very common exception to this rule: the word **여덟 (Eight)**. In this specific case, the **ㄹ** is actually pronounced, resulting in **여덜**. He concludes by reminding students that if these words were followed by an empty ㅇ (like **삶이**), both consonants would be heard because the second one would link forward (sounding like **살미**).

---

### The Two Groups of Friend Consonants

The instructor categorizes the nine common compound consonants based on which letter is typically pronounced. Group 1 consists of seven combinations where the first consonant is the boss: ㄳ, ㄵ, ㄶ, ㄼ, ㄾ, ㅀ, and ㅄ. For example, in the word 넋 (soul), the first consonant ㄱ is pronounced while the ㅅ is silent, resulting in the sound 녹. Similarly, in 앉다 (to sit), the ㄴ is the boss, so it is read as 안.

Group 2 consists of two combinations where the second consonant is the boss: ㄺ and ㄻ. In these cases, the sound follows the second letter. For example, 닭 (chicken) is pronounced 닥, following the ㄱ sound. The word 삶 (life) is pronounced 삼, following the ㅁ sound. The instructor notes that while the "non-boss" consonant might seem useless when the word is alone, it plays a critical role in pronunciation when the word is followed by other letters.

````md
The same principle applies to ㄻ. In the word 삶 (life), the second consonant ㅁ is the boss when the word is alone, sounding like 삼. When followed by a consonant, as in 삶한번 (one life), it remains 삼한번. But when followed by a vowel, as in 삶은 (life is...), the ㄹ becomes the boss of the first syllable and the ㅁ moves over, resulting in 살믄.

---

### Group 1 Dynamics: First Consonant as Boss

For Group 1, the first consonant is the primary sound, but the second consonant often affects the following syllable. In the case of ㄵ, as in 앉다 (to sit), the ㄴ is the boss. When followed by a consonant like 다, the silent ㅈ puts "pressure" on the next sound, making it a double consonant sound: 안따. If followed by a vowel, as in 앉아 (sit down), the ㅈ moves over to become the initial of the next syllable: 안자.

The instructor highlights a special rule for nasal sounds like ㄴ. Because nasal sounds are very strong, the pressure from a "friend consonant" cannot change them. For example, 앉네 (sitting) is pronounced 안네 because the ㄴ in 네 is resistant to the pressure of the ㅈ.

---

### Special Cases: ㄶ and ㅀ

The combinations ㄶ and ㅀ involve the letter ㅎ, which behaves uniquely. When followed by a consonant like ㄱ, ㄷ, or ㅈ, the ㅎ combines with it to create an aspirated sound (ㅋ, ㅌ, ㅊ). For example, 많다 (to be many) becomes 만타, and 앉히다 (to seat someone) becomes 안치다. 

When ㄶ or ㅀ is followed by a vowel, the ㅎ sound typically disappears or blends in. The word 많아 (many) is pronounced 마나, and 싫어 (to dislike) is pronounced 시러. The instructor notes that the ㅎ effectively vanishes, allowing the first consonant to link directly to the following vowel.

---

### Group 1 Continued: ㄼ, ㄾ, and ㅄ

The instructor demonstrates ㄼ with the word 넓다 (to be wide). The ㄹ is the boss, sounding like 널. When followed by a consonant, the ㅂ adds pressure, resulting in 넓게 being pronounced 널께. When followed by a vowel, as in 넓어, the ㅂ moves over: 널버.

For ㄾ, as in 핥다 (to lick), the ㄹ is the boss. When followed by a vowel, as in 핥아, the ㅌ moves over: 할타. For ㅄ, as in 없다 (to not have), the ㅂ is the boss. When followed by a consonant, the ㅅ adds pressure, making 없지 sound like 업찌. When followed by a vowel, as in 없어, the ㅅ moves over and is pronounced as a double consonant sound: 업써.

### Compound Batchim (받침)
Ji explains that compound batchim consist of two different consonants at the bottom of a syllable. While there are 11 possible combinations, one (ㄹㅅ) is only used in Old Korean and can be ignored.
*   **Dominant Letter Rule:** In most pairs, the first letter is dominant. In three specific pairs (ㄹㄱ, ㄹㅁ, ㄹㅍ), the second letter is dominant.
*   **Pronunciation in Isolation:** Only the dominant sound is pronounced.
    *   **넋** (spirit): dominant is ㄱ → pronounced [넉].
    *   **앉다** (to sit): dominant is ㄴ → pronounced [안].
    *   **읽다** (to read): dominant is ㄱ → pronounced [익].
    *   **삶** (life): dominant is ㅁ → pronounced [삼].
    *   **읊다** (to recite): dominant is ㅍ (which becomes ㅂ) → pronounced [읍].
*   **Followed by Silent Consonant (ㅇ):** The second letter of the batchim moves over to the next syllable.
    *   **넋이** → [넉시]
    *   **앉아** → [안자]
    *   **읽어** → [일거]
*   **Followed by Non-Silent Consonants:** The dominant letter interacts with the following consonant using standard assimilation rules (amplification, aspiration, or nasalization).
    *   **Exception:** **닭** (chicken). Usually pronounced [닥], but when followed by a particle like **이**, it becomes **다기** [da-gi] rather than the expected [달기].
````
