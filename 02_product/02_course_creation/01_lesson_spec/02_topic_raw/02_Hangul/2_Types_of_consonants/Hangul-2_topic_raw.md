````md
### The Tissue Paper Test: Airflow and Aspiration

A primary way to distinguish Korean consonants is by the amount of air expelled during pronunciation. The instructor demonstrates this using a piece of tissue paper held in front of his mouth. When pronouncing the single consonant ㅅ, as in the word 사랑 (love), there is a significant burst of air that causes the tissue to move visibly. In contrast, the double consonant ㅆ is a "tense" or "strong" sound that involves suppressing the air. When the instructor says words like 싸다 (to be cheap), the tissue paper barely moves, even when he increases his volume.

This principle applies to other consonant pairs as well. The instructor briefly compares 가 and 까 to show that the single consonant (가) produces a noticeable puff of air, while the double consonant (까) produces almost none. To practice this, learners are encouraged to say the sounds loudly; yelling the sounds makes the presence or absence of airflow even more obvious.

---

### Mouth Positioning and the "Hiss" Sound

The instructor describes ㅆ as having a distinct "hissing" quality. He suggests imagining taking an extra breath of air just before making the sound to create that sharp, tensed hiss. Visually, he uses a diagram of the mouth to explain the physical placement of these sounds. The single ㅅ is pronounced toward the front of the mouth, similar to a regular English "s." However, because ㅆ requires more tension and less air, it feels as though it is being pronounced slightly further back in the mouth, almost coming from the throat. This tensing of the vocal apparatus is what prevents the air from escaping as freely as it does with the single consonant.

---

### Practice Tips: Syllable Placement and Exaggeration

To better hear and feel the difference, the instructor recommends practicing these consonants in the middle of a word rather than in isolation. By placing a vowel sound before the consonant, the transition becomes more apparent. He provides comparison pairs for practice:

- **아사** vs **아싸**
- **해서** vs **했어**

In the second pair, the instructor notes that saying 했어 with too much air (like 해서) would be incorrect. The goal is to maintain that tensed, airless quality even in the middle of a sentence.

Finally, for learners who are struggling to be understood, the instructor suggests over-exaggerating the tension of the double consonant. He uses the example of 살 (flesh/meat) versus 쌀 (uncooked rice). If a learner is in a situation where they must be absolutely clear, making the ㅆ sound extremely strong and tensed will signal to the listener exactly which word is intended. While this level of exaggeration isn't necessary for natural speech, it serves as a useful training tool.
````

````md
### Double Consonants (쌍자음)

Double consonants are formed by writing two of the same basic consonant side-by-side within the same space normally occupied by a single character. The instructor explains that there are five double consonants to learn: **ㄲ**, **ㄸ**, **ㅃ**, **ㅆ**, and **ㅉ**. The stroke order is straightforward: you simply draw the base consonant twice, moving from left to right. It is important to ensure that the two characters are squeezed together so they do not take up double the width of a standard syllable block.

The pronunciation of double consonants is characterized by "tensing." While the basic mouth position remains the same as the regular version, the speaker must tense the mouth and throat slightly before releasing the sound. This results in a "harder" or more "compact" sound with virtually no air being released. The instructor demonstrates this by comparing pairs of syllables:

- **가 (ga)** vs. **까 (kka)**
- **다 (da)** vs. **따 (tta)**
- **바 (ba)** vs. **빠 (ppa)**
- **사 (sa)** vs. **싸 (ssa)**
- **자 (ja)** vs. **짜 (jja)**

To master these, the instructor suggests practicing them within words rather than in isolation. For example, comparing **아가 (baby)** with **아까 (a moment ago)** makes the tensing of the **ㄲ** sound more apparent.

---

### Strong Consonants (격음)

Strong consonants, also known as aspirated consonants, are versions of basic consonants pronounced with a strong burst of air. There are four strong consonants: **ㅋ**, **ㅌ**, **ㅍ**, and **ㅊ**. Each is visually related to a basic consonant but includes an additional stroke to signal the stronger pronunciation.

The instructor details the stroke order for each: **ㅋ** is derived from **ㄱ** by adding a middle horizontal stroke; **ㅌ** is derived from **ㄷ** by adding a middle horizontal line (written as three strokes total); **ㅍ** is written with four strokes, resembling a Roman numeral II with a top and bottom bar; and **ㅊ** is derived from **ㅈ** by adding a small mark at the top.

The pronunciation difference is purely based on the force of the breath. The instructor compares them to their regular counterparts:

- **가 (ga)** vs. **카 (ka)**
- **다 (da)** vs. **타 (ta)**
- **바 (ba)** vs. **파 (pa)**
- **자 (ja)** vs. **차 (cha)**

The strong consonants sound much closer to the aspirated "k," "t," "p," and "ch" sounds found at the beginning of English words like "kite" or "top."

---

### The Tissue Paper Trick and Phonetic Distinction

To help students visualize the difference between regular, double, and strong consonants, the instructor demonstrates the "tissue paper trick." By holding a thin piece of tissue in front of the mouth while speaking, the student can see the amount of air released. For a regular consonant like **가**, the tissue moves slightly. For a double consonant like **까**, the tissue should not move at all because the sound is tensed and held back. For a strong consonant like **카**, the tissue should move significantly due to the forceful burst of air.

This distinction is vital because changing the consonant type changes the word's meaning. The instructor provides a clear example using the following words:

- **불** / fire (Regular consonant **ㅂ**)
- **뿔** / horns (Double consonant **ㅃ**)
- **풀** / grass or glue (Strong consonant **ㅍ**)

While context often helps, incorrect pronunciation of these consonant types can lead to significant confusion in conversation.
````

#### 격음·ㅅ의 sh·ㅎ·쌍자음·받침 기초

````md
### Slide 54~55 — Aspirated Consonants
- **On screen:** ASPIRATED; WITH A PUFF OF AIR; ㅋ ㅌ ㅍ ㅎ ㅊ.
- **Ji's teaching:** Aspirated consonants (ㅋ, ㅌ, ㅍ, ㅎ, ㅊ) are all about that puff of air. Compare 가 (ga) to 카 (ka), or 다 (da) to 타 (ta). You should feel the air hit your hand if you hold it in front of your mouth.

### Slide 56~59 — The "Sh" Sound Exception for ㅅ
- **On screen:** Slide 57: "Sh sound Depending on the vowel." Slide 58-59 show tables. ㅅ paired with ㅏ, ㅓ, ㅗ, ㅜ, ㅡ stays "Smooth" (사, 서, 소, 수, 스). ㅅ paired with ㅑ, ㅕ, ㅛ, ㅠ, ㅣ becomes "Aspirated (Sh)" (샤, 셔, 쇼, 슈, 시).
- **Ji's teaching:** Even though ㅅ doesn't have a separate aspirated letter, it *does* have an aspirated sound: "Sh." This happens depending on which vowel it is paired with. When ㅅ is paired with vowels that have two strokes (ㅑ, ㅕ, ㅛ, ㅠ) or the vowel ㅣ, it changes from an "s" sound to an "sh" sound. So 사 is "sa," but 시 is "shi." Note that 싀 doesn't actually exist in the language.

### Slide 60~61 — ㅎ (The Aspirator)
- **On screen:** ㅎ 히읗 (hieut). Labeled "Aspirated ㅇ" and "Aspirates other consonants." Example: 좋다 (jo h + da) becomes 조타 (jo ta).
- **Ji's teaching:** Think of ㅎ as the "Aspirator Consonant." Its role is to aspirate other consonants when they are paired together. For example, in the word 좋다 (to be good), the ㅎ at the bottom of the first syllable combines with the ㄷ at the start of the next syllable. ㅎ + ㄷ = ㅌ. So you pronounce it as 조타, not "joh-da." ㅎ aspirates every letter except ㅅ, because ㅅ is the only sound that doesn't have its own dedicated aspirated letter.

### Slide 62~65 — Double Consonants
- **On screen:** ㄲ ㄸ ㅃ ㅆ ㅉ. Labeled "STRENGTHENED" and "WITH NO PUFF OF AIR." Slide 65 shows "Sound 싸운드 VS Smooth 스무스."
- **Ji's teaching:** Double consonants are the opposite of aspirated ones. They are strong and emphasized, but no air should come out. Let's focus on ㅆ. Remember my pun: ㅅ is for "Smooth," but ㅆ is for "Sound." ㅆ is a much more "sound" sound—it's sharper and more tense.

### Slide 66~67 — 받침 Affecting Next Syllables
- **On screen:** 좋다 = 조타; 잃다 (to lose); 읽다 (to read). Text: "the 받침 in the first syllable can affect the pronunciation of the second syllable."
- **Ji's teaching:** This is a key concept: the 받침 at the end of one syllable often changes how the *next* syllable is pronounced. We saw this with 좋다 becoming 조타. It also happens in words like 잃다 and 읽다.

### Slide 68~72 — Single Syllable 받침 Rules
- **On screen:** ㄱ ㄴ ㄷ ㄹ ㅁ ㅂ ㅇ. Slide 69 explains that many letters collapse into 7 base sounds. ㄱ, ㅋ, ㄲ all sound like ㄱ (g/k). ㄷ, ㅌ, ㅅ, ㅆ, ㅈ, ㅊ all sound like ㄷ (d). ㅂ, ㅍ all sound like ㅂ (b).
- **Ji's teaching:** When a consonant is in the 받침 position at the end of a single syllable, there are only 7 possible sounds. ㄴ, ㄹ, ㅁ, and ㅇ are self-explanatory—they sound like themselves. However, many other consonants simplify. For example, whether you see ㄱ, ㅋ, or ㄲ at the bottom, they all just make a "k" sound (like in 낙, 낰, 낚). Similarly, a whole group of letters (ㄷ, ㅌ, ㅅ, ㅆ, ㅈ, ㅊ) all simplify to a "d" sound when they are at the bottom.

### Slide 73~74 — Tension Practice
- **On screen:** TRY READING: 악 닫 압 숫 낮. Labeled with their simplified sounds: ak, dad, ab, sud, nad. Slide 74: "Key to pronouncing double consonants: Feel the tension in your mouth / throat."
- **Ji's teaching:** Let's practice reading these: 악, 닫, 압, 숫, 낮. To get these right, you need to feel the tension forming in your mouth and throat. You aren't fully releasing the sound; you are catching it. This tension is the key to mastering double consonants later.

### Slide 75~80 — Double Consonant Practice with Real Words
- **On screen:** Words with double consonant sounds: 악기 (Musical Instrument), 닫다 (To close), 압박 (Pressure), 숫소 (Male Cow), 낮잠 (Nap). Slide 80: "REMEMBER THE FEELING OF THE TENSION IN YOUR MOUTH AND THROAT."
- **Ji's teaching:** We can use that 받침 tension to help us pronounce double consonants in the middle of words. In 악기, the tension from the ㄱ in 악 makes the next syllable sound like 끼. So it's like saying "ak-(tension)-gi" which results in 아끼. The same happens for 닫다 (pronounced 다따), 압박 (아빡), 숫소 (수쏘), and 낮잠 (나짬).
````
