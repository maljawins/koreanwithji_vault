````md
### The Fundamental Rule: Words and Particles
The most basic rule of Korean spacing is to place a space between every individual word. However, a critical exception exists for particles and markers. In Korean, grammatical markers (like subject, object, or topic markers) are never separated from the word they follow. For example, in the sentence 저는 한국을 좋아해요 (I like Korea), the topic marker 는 is attached directly to 저 (I), and the object marker 을 is attached directly to 한국 (Korea). There is a space after the particle, but never before it. This rule also applies to modifiers; adjectives and nouns, as well as adverbs and verbs, are considered separate words and must be spaced accordingly.

---

### Verbs and Spacing Exceptions
Spacing rules for verbs depend heavily on the specific verb type and the presence of markers. For 하다 verbs derived from nouns, such as 공부하다 (to study), the spacing changes based on whether an object marker is used. If you say "to do study" using the marker, it is written as 공부를 하다 with a space. However, if the marker is removed, the space must also be removed, resulting in the single word 공부하다.

The verb 이다 (to be) is another unique case; it is always attached directly to the preceding noun without a space, as seen in 저는 빌리예요 (I am Billy). Conversely, the negative verb 아니다 (to not be) follows standard rules and requires a space after the subject marker. Additionally, the future tense construction 갈 거예요 (will go) requires spaces because 갈 (from 가다) and 거 (thing/fact) are treated as separate words.

---

### Auxiliary Verbs and Optional Spacing
Auxiliary verbs—verbs used to add grammatical nuance rather than literal meaning—occupy a "gray area" in spacing. Common examples include 보다 (to try/see) and 주다 (to do for someone). In forms like 먹어 보다 (try eating) or 해 주다 (do as a favor), spacing is technically optional. While adding a space is considered slightly more formal and "proper" for essays, writing them as single words (먹어보다, 해주다) is perfectly acceptable in most contexts. However, some specific combinations have become fixed through usage; for instance, 물어보다 (to ask) is always written without a space, regardless of the general auxiliary rule.

---

### Names, Titles, and Compound Words
When addressing or referring to someone by name and title, a space is required between the two. The instructor demonstrates this with the name 현우 (Hyun-woo) and the honorific title 씨; it must be written as 현우 씨. This applies to all titles, including 선생님 (teacher) or 회장님 (president).

Compound words, which are two nouns combined to create a new meaning, are written without spaces. A prime example is 눈물 (tears), formed from 눈 (eye) and 물 (water). The instructor advises that the best way to identify a compound word is to check a dictionary; if the combination has its own entry, it should not be spaced. Proper nouns, such as government organizations like 국립국어원 (National Institute of the Korean Language), often follow their own specific branding or historical spacing preferences which must simply be memorized or looked up.

---

### Numbers, Counters, and Pronouns
Official Korean spacing rules dictate that there should be a space between a number and its counter. For example, "2:10 PM" should technically be written as 2시 10분 with spaces between the digits and the units. However, the instructor notes that this often looks awkward even to natives. Consequently, official rules allow for these to be combined (2시 10분), which is the preferred style for most writers. When writing large numbers entirely in Hangeul, a space is required at every 10,000 (만) unit, though this is rarely seen outside of textbooks as most people use Arabic numerals.

Certain pronouns and demonstratives are always written as single words without spaces. This includes terms for "this/that person" (이분, 그분, 저분) and "this/that thing" (이것, 그것, 저것).

---

### Meaning Shifts and Practical Tools
In some cases, spacing changes the meaning of a phrase. The instructor highlights the difference between 한 번 (with a space) and 한번 (without a space). 한 번 literally means "one time" (counting frequency), whereas 한번 is used as an adverb meaning "sometime" or "once" in the context of a suggestion, such as "Let's eat together sometime."

To master spacing, the instructor suggests paying attention to how words are spaced when first learning them rather than memorizing rules later. For checking work, he recommends using online AI spacing checkers (linked in the video description), which are highly accurate but should be used with a degree of critical thinking. Ultimately, the most important takeaway is that while spacing matters for clarity, learners should focus on doing their best rather than fearing mistakes.
````

#### 사이시옷의 기능·발음·조건

- 원문 줄: 24~63

````md
### The Function and Meaning of 사이시옷
The instructor demonstrates how 사이시옷 functions by comparing it to the "apostrophe S" in English. Just as "'s" can indicate possession or a relationship (like "the stomach's fat"), the ㅅ in a Korean compound word indicates that the first word modifies the second. The instructor provides several common examples to illustrate this compounding process:

- **뱃살 (stomach fat):** Combined from `배 (stomach)` and `살 (flesh/skin)`.
- **햇빛 (sunlight):** Combined from `해 (sun)` and `빛 (light)`.
- **찻집 (tea house):** Combined from `차 (tea)` and `집 (house)`.
- **고춧가루 (red pepper powder):** Combined from `고추 (red pepper)` and `가루 (powder/flour)`.

In each case, the ㅅ is inserted at the bottom of the first word's final syllable to bind the two concepts together into a new, specific noun.

---

### Impact on Pronunciation
A critical aspect of 사이시옷 is how it transforms the sound of the resulting compound word. When this letter is present, the first consonant of the second word is pronounced as a double consonant (tense sound). The instructor provides the phonetic realizations for the previous examples:

- **뱃살** is pronounced as `배쌀` (with a double ㅅ sound).
- **햇빛** is pronounced with a double ㅂ sound (`해삗`).
- **찻집** is pronounced with a double ㅉ sound (`차찝`).
- **고춧가루** is pronounced with a double ㄱ sound (`고추까루`).

This change in sound is not random; it reflects how native speakers naturally adjusted their pronunciation over time when merging words. The spelling was eventually standardized to reflect this natural phonetic shift.

---

### Rules and Exceptions for Usage
The instructor outlines the specific conditions required for 사이시옷 to appear in spelling. First, it is generally only used when at least one of the words being combined is a native (pure) Korean word. It is typically not used for compounds made entirely of two Sino-Korean (Chinese-derived) words, though a few rare exceptions exist.

Second, the first word must end in a vowel for the ㅅ to be physically written. If the first word already ends in a consonant (받침), the ㅅ cannot be added to the spelling, even if the pronunciation still changes. The instructor illustrates this with two examples:

- **콩가루 (bean powder):** Combined from `콩 (bean)` and `가루 (powder)`. Because `콩` ends in a consonant, no ㅅ is added, even though it is pronounced like `콩까루`.
- **물고기 (fish):** Combined from `물 (water)` and `고기 (meat)`. It is pronounced as `물꼬기`, but because `물` ends in a consonant, the ㅅ is omitted from the spelling.

Historically, some of these words might have been written with a ㅅ, but modern standardized spelling has removed them for words ending in consonants.

---

### Advice for Learners: Deconstruction vs. Creation
The instructor concludes with a warning: learners should not use the 사이시옷 rule to invent their own compound words. The presence of 사이시옷 is based on established usage and historical evolution, not a universal formula. For example, combining `피부 (skin)` and `색깔 (color)` does not result in `피붓색깔`; it remains `피부 색깔` because that is the established form. Similarly, while `새집` means "birdhouse," attempting to say `샛집` would be incorrect and unnatural as it does not exist in the lexicon.

The best approach for a student is to use this knowledge to "break apart" and understand the components of words they encounter. Recognizing the 사이시옷 allows a learner to identify the two base words and understand the correct tense pronunciation of the compound.
````

````md
### The History of Korean Spacing: The John Ross Legacy

For over 500 years after the creation of Hangul in 1443, the Korean language was written without any spaces, much like classical Chinese. Characters were packed together in tight blocks. The introduction of spacing is credited to John Ross, a Scottish missionary living in China who was trilingual in Gaelic, English, and Chinese. After meeting Korean traders, Ross decided to translate the New Testament into Korean, a project that required him to first master the language. In 1877, he published *Korean Primer*, a lesson book for foreigners. In this book, Ross introduced two revolutionary concepts: the first widely used romanization system (the Ross system) and the first use of spaces in Korean text, inspired by English punctuation.

The concept moved into the mainstream in 1896 when *Donglipsunmun* (The Independent), the first newspaper published in Korean, implemented spacing in its very first edition. The practice was eventually formalized in 1933 when the Korean Language Society published the *Unified Draft for Hangul Orthography*, establishing the standardized rules for spelling and punctuation used today.

---

### Why Korean Needs Spaces (Comparison with English, Chinese, and Japanese)

While it is possible to read Korean without spaces—as seen in casual text messages—it is significantly more difficult and prone to ambiguity. The instructor compares this to English, noting that while English without spaces is nearly unreadable, Korean is slightly easier because each block-shaped character contains more information and provides a visual break. However, spacing remains critical for clarity.

The reason Chinese and Japanese do not require spaces lies in the density of information within their characters. Chinese characters (Hanzi) are logograms where each character carries a specific meaning. When Korean simplified thousands of Chinese characters into a 24-letter alphabet, much of that inherent meaning was lost at the character level. For example, the Korean character 가 can represent many different Chinese characters with distinct meanings, such as those found in 가장 (head of household), 가수 (singer), 가속 (acceleration), or 가능 (possible). Without spaces or Hanja (Chinese characters), words like 화장 can mean either "makeup" or "cremation" depending entirely on context.

Japanese avoids the need for spaces by using a mix of three writing systems: Hiragana, Katakana, and Kanji. The visual transition between these systems naturally breaks the sentence into chunks. Korean, being written almost exclusively in Hangul, lacks these natural visual anchors, making artificial spacing necessary.

---

### Semantic Ambiguity and Spacing Examples

The instructor demonstrates how a single space can completely change the meaning of a sentence. These differences are not just visual; native speakers indicate the correct meaning through pauses and stresses when speaking.

> **나물 좀 다오.** / Please give me some herbs.
> **나 물 좀 다오.** / Please give me some water.

In the first sentence, `나물` means "herbs." In the second, `나` is the pronoun "me" and `물` is "water."

> **걔밖에 없다.** / There is no one but him/her. (Only he/she is there.)
> **걔 밖에 없다.** / He/she is not outside.

Here, `밖에` as a particle means "only/nothing but," while `밖` as a noun means "outside" followed by the location particle `에`.

> **친구가 자꾸만 져요.** / My friend keeps losing.
> **친구가 자꾸 만져요.** / My friend keeps touching (me).

The verb `지다 (to lose)` vs. `만지다 (to touch)` creates a significant difference in meaning based on where the space is placed.

---

### Spacing in Modern Usage

The instructor acknowledges that spacing rules are notoriously difficult, even for native Koreans. Nuances often allow for multiple "correct" versions. For instance, long phrases can sometimes be written with or without spaces depending on whether they are treated as a single compound noun.

- **한국 대학교 사범 대학** (Technically correct)
- **한국대학교사범대학** (Allowed/Common)

Common compound terms like `주민등록번호 (Social Security Number)` or `사회적거리두기 (Social Distancing)` are often seen without spaces. The instructor concludes that while perfect spacing is rare in everyday life, the system exists to prevent "eternal confusion," even if it occasionally causes "eternal frustration" for learners.
````

#### 사이시옷 합성어 조건

- 원문 줄: 10~125

````md
사이시옷: Putting ㅅ Within Compound Words

Often two individual words get combined to make a compound word. For example:

아침 = morning
밥 = food, rice
아침밥 = breakfast, or the food/rice that one eats for breakfast

Although 아침밥 is one word, it is composed of two words that are words on their own. We have created a “compound word,” and in effect, the first half of the compound word (아침) is describing the second half (밥). What type of “rice/food” do we have? We have *morning* “rice/food.”

Notice in 아침밥 that the final letter of the first half of the word is a consonant. That is, 아침 ends in a consonant. In other words, the final syllable has a 받침.

*A*받침*is used to refer to the type of plate that one would rest a hot pan/pot onto instead of resting it directly onto the table. In language, it is used to refer to the final consonant of a syllable. Some words have a*받침*(like “*집*”) and some words do not have a*받침 *(like “*자*”)*

Sometimes you will see compound words where the first half of the word originally doesn’t end in a consonant. That is, where the final syllable of the first half of the word doesn’t have a 받침. For example, I can combine “어제” (yesterday), and “밤” (night) to refer to yesterday’s night (last night). Notice that 어제, which would be the first half of our compound word, doesn’t end in a consonant. In other words, its final syllable doesn’t have a 받침.

In these situations, you will often see ㅅ inserted into the place where a final consonant would go. For example, if we combine 어제 and 밤, the compound word is not 어제밤, but instead it is 어젯밤.

Again, although this is one word, in effect, the first half of the compound word (어제) is describing the second half (밤). What “night” are we talking about? We are talking about *yesterday’s*“night.”

The addition of a ㅅ into a compound word is called “사이시옷.” 사이 means “in between” and [“시옷” is the name of the letter ㅅ](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/unit0/letter-names/). There are rules that indicate when a 사이시옷 should be included. This is a very complex topic, and is something that many Korean people find difficult as well. In fact, this is the kind of thing that Korean students learn in their Korean language classes in high school. Where a Canadian high school student will learn how to use a semicolon in his high school English classes, a Korean high school student will learn how to use the 사이시옷 in his Korean classes.

I would like to use this lesson to explain the rules of 사이시옷 to you.

Rules

Rule 1: I’ve already explained this, but I want to state it specifically as a rule. The 사이시옷 is only added between the individual constituents of a compound word. It is not added within a regular word.

For example, 가방 is a word on its own. It is not a compound word. Even though “가” doesn’t have a 받침, 사이시옷 would not be added to it. 갓방 is not a word.

However, 어제 and 밤 are words on their own. They can come together to make a compound word – 어젯밤. In this case, 사이시옷 is used.

—————————-

Rule 2: I’ve already explained this as well. The 사이시옷 is only added when the final syllable of the first part of the compound word does not have a 받침.

For example, the word for “leaf” is 잎. What kind of leaf are we talking about? I can create a compound word using many types of plants/trees to specifically describe the type of leaf I am referring to. For example:

감잎 = persimmon leaf
호박잎 = pumpkin leaf
마늘잎 = garlic leaf

The three words above are compound words. However, the first half of each compound word (감, 호박 and 마늘), ends in a consonant. 사이시옷 is not added to these words because, well, it can’t fit!

However, I can create a compound word using other types of plants/trees:

깻잎 = sesame leaf
나뭇잎 = tree leaf
배춧잎 = Korean cabbage leaf

The three words above are compound words. The first half of each compound word (깨, 나무, 배추) ends in a vowel. As you can see, 사이시옷 is added to these words.

—————————-

Rule 3: You never add 사이시옷 when one of the constituents of the compound word is a foreign loan-word (excluding Hanja). These foreign words typically come from English.

For example, the word for “house” is 집.  What kind of house are we talking about? I can create a compound word to specifically describe the type of house I am referring to:

꽃집 = flower house
찻집 = tea house
고깃집 = meat house (a restaurant that serves meat)
전셋집 = Jeon-sae house (a house that is rented using the 전세 method)

Using Rule 1 and Rule 2, you can understand the four examples above.

However, I can use a foreign loan-word to describe a house. For example:

피자집 = pizza house (a restaurant that serves pizza)

피자 is a word borrowed from English. If there is one of these “foreign” words in a compound word, even if the final syllable of the first word ends in a vowel, 사이시옷 is not used.

—————————-

Rule 4: This one is hard to know, especially if you don’t know Chinese or aren’t keeping up with your Hanja studies. At least one of the constituents of the compound word must be of pure Korean origin. As you know, the other half of the compound word cannot be a foreign load-word. However, the other half *can*be a Hanja-derived word.

Let’s go through this step-by-step:

The word for “road/path” is 길.  What kind of road/path are we talking about? I can create a compound word to specifically describe the type of road/path I am referring to:

뱃길 = a path for boats to follow
빗길 = a road that is being rained on

In both of the words above, both constituents of the compound words are of pure Korean origin. 배 (boat), 비 (rain) and 길 (road/path) are all original Korean words. There is no Hanja equivalent for any of those words. For a Korean learner, it is very hard to know this unless you have been exposed to Korean and Hanja for many, many years.

For me, even without looking in the dictionary, I assumed that 배, 비 and 길 were pure Korean words. I honestly don’t know how I knew this, I just did. Years of exposure to these types of words have given me experience with dealing with them, and this experience gave me the assumption. If you don’t know this right now – you probably don’t have to. Your brain, with time, will most likely be able to recognize Hanja-derived words from pure Korean words eventually.

The word for “going/walking to school” is 등교. 등교 is of Hanja origin and therefore has a Hanja equivalent -登校. I can create a compound word using 등교 and 길:

등굣길 = road/path that is used to walk to school

Remember the rule. At least one of the constituents of the compound word must be of pure Korean origin. The other half *can*be a Hanja-derived word. 등굣길 meets the requirements of this rule (and all the other rules discussed so far), so a 사이시옷 is used.

Let’s do it again.

The word for “light” is 빛.  What kind of light are we talking about? I can create a compound word to specifically describe the type of light I am referring to:

햇빛 = sunlight
무지갯빛 = rainbow colors (iridescence)

In both of the words above, both constituents of the compound words are of pure Korean origin. 해 (sun), 무지개 (rainbow) and 빛 (light) are all original Korean words. There is no Hanja equivalent for any of those words.

The word for “rose” is 장미. 장미 is of Hanja origin and therefore has a Hanja equivalent -薔薇. I can create a compound word using 장미 and 빛:

장밋빛 = the light (color) of a rose

Remember the rule. At least one of the constituents of the compound word must be of pure Korean origin. The other half *can*be a Hanja-derived word. 장밋빛 meets the requirements of this rule (and all the other rules discussed so far), so a 사이시옷 is used.

—————————-

Rule 5: Rule 4 states that at least one of the constituents of the compound words must be of pure Korean origin. There are six exceptions to this, where both constituents are of Hanja origin, and there is no Korean originated word:

숫자(數字) = a numeral
횟수(回數) = the number of times something is done
찻간(車間) = the inside of a train car
곳간(庫間) = a storehouse
셋방(貰房) = a room to be rented
````
