````md
### The Nature of ㅎ (히읗)

The letter ㅎ is named 히읗. A critical starting point for learners is realizing that it is not a direct equivalent to the English "H." In English, an "H" sound typically involves a distinct and forceful expulsion of air. In contrast, the Korean ㅎ is much softer. For example, when saying 하하 (haha), a Korean speaker does not use the same amount of breathy force as an English speaker saying "haha." Because ㅎ is so soft to begin with, it is prone to being dropped or modified when it interacts with other sounds. This "softness" is the underlying reason for almost all sound change rules involving this letter.

---

### ㅎ Disappearing Before Empty Consonants (ㅇ)

When ㅎ appears as a batchim (bottom consonant) and is followed by the empty consonant ㅇ, it typically disappears in pronunciation. The instructor demonstrates this with several common words:

- **좋아요 (to be good/to like):** Although written with ㅎ, it is pronounced as 조아요.
- **싫어요 (to dislike):** The ㅎ in the double batchim disappears, resulting in a pronunciation like 실어요.
- **많이 (many/a lot):** This is pronounced as 마니, not 만히.

The instructor explains that if you think of ㅎ as a strong "H," these rules seem confusing. However, if you view ㅎ as a very weak sound, it makes sense that it simply "bails" or gets dropped when the mouth moves quickly to the next vowel sound.

---

### ㅎ and Aspiration (Strong Consonants)

Another major rule involves ㅎ combining with regular consonants (ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ, ㅈ) to create their strong, aspirated counterparts (ㅋ, ㅌ, ㅍ, ㅊ). This happens whether the ㅎ comes before or after the consonant. Since the primary difference between a regular consonant and a strong one is the addition of extra air, the ㅎ acts as that "extra air" source.

- **좋다 (to be good):** The ㅎ batchim meets ㄷ. The ㅎ disappears, and its air is added to the ㄷ, making it ㅌ. The pronunciation is 조타.
- **급하다 (to be urgent):** Here, the ㅂ batchim is followed by ㅎ. The air from ㅎ is added back to the ㅂ, turning it into ㅍ. The pronunciation is 그파다.

In both cases, the letter ㅎ itself vanishes from the spoken sound, leaving only its "air" behind in the neighboring consonant.

---

### The "Weakening" of ㅎ in Regular Speech

Beyond formal sound change rules, ㅎ often weakens or disappears in the middle of words during natural, fast-paced conversation. This specifically occurs when ㅎ follows the "sonorant" consonants: 미음 (ㅁ), 니은 (ㄴ), 이응 (ㅇ), or 리을 (ㄹ).

The instructor provides several real-world examples where the ㅎ is 90% to 100% silent:
- **안녕하세요 (Hello):** While beginners might say 안-녕-하-세-요 syllable by syllable, native speakers usually pronounce it as 안녕아세요.
- **결혼 (Marriage):** Pronounced as 겨론 rather than 결-혼.
- **전화 (Telephone):** Pronounced as 저놔 rather than 전-화.
- **올해 (This year):** Pronounced as 오래.
- **조회수 (View count):** Pronounced as 조외수.
- **감히 (How dare...):** Pronounced as 가미.

The instructor notes that while these are not "official" dictionary rules (the dictionary may still list the version with the ㅎ sound), they represent how 99% of people actually speak. Attempting to pronounce every ㅎ clearly in these positions can actually make a learner sound unnatural or like they are putting in too much effort.

---

### Summary of ㅎ Pronunciation

The only time ㅎ is consistently and clearly heard is at the very beginning of a word where no other sounds are affecting it, such as in 하하. In almost every other environment—whether meeting a vowel, a regular consonant, or following a soft consonant—ㅎ tends to disappear or merge. It is a "shy" letter that bails whenever it faces phonological difficulty.
````

````md
**What Is Consonant Assimilation?**

Consonant assimilation refers to the sound change where a 받침 sound is affected by the next consonant, resulting in a change in pronunciation.

This happens in English too.

**Here’s some more examples.**

**Kindness → Kain nis**

**I don’t know → I don no**

**Did you → D’ Jew**

It’s pretty much **the same concept.**

Now, the idea that the pronunciation changes might sound intimidating at first but you should know that it’s there to help you pronounce things easier and more naturally.

You’ll see what I mean by this as we go.

**1. When followed by ‘ㅇ’**

Let’s start with the **easiest rule**: When the next syllable starts with **ㅇ**, the silent letter, the 받침 sound *moves* over to the beginning of the next syllable.

Here are some examples.

받아 날아 음악 잡아 앗아가다

Of course, there are a few exceptions, but there are only a handful of words like this. You'll pick them up naturally over time.

So, these are just for your reference:

맛없다, 멋없다, 겉옷

**2. Types of 받침 sounds.**

Reminder. In the previous lesson, you learned that there can only be 7 sounds in the 받침 position which are ㄱ ㄷ ㅂ ㄴ ㄹ ㅁ ㅇ.

And now we have to add ㅎ on the list because this guy needs a bit of extra attention, which we’ll talk about at the end.

Consonant assimilation is basically about how these 8 sounds interact with each other to make pronunciation EASIER on your mouth.

To understand this better, we first need to divide these 8 letters into three categories:

**1. Amplifiers – ㄱ ㄷ ㅂ**
These create tension in your mouth. We covered this when we were learning double consonants. So, when they meet certain sounds, they amplify the next consonant.

**2. Nasal 받침 – ㄴ ㄹ ㅁ ㅇ**
These all form a nasal sound. And what these guys do is that when these guys are met with certain consonants, the sound often changes to flow smoother and more natural.

**3. ‘ㅎ’ - a Wild card**

And finally we have ‘ㅎ’. Remember how we focused on ㅅ last time?

Yeah, in this lesson, we're going to pay special attention to ㅎ in the end

**1. When the next syllable becomes a double consonant**

**2. When pronunciation naturally shifts to an easier sound**
This isn’t really a "rule" per se and it’s just something that happens naturally and in most cases, nasal consonants (ㄴ, ㄹ, ㅁ, ㅇ) trigger this change.

Notice they all have their **double consonant counterpart.**

So when they come after ㄱ, ㄷ, or ㅂ 받침, the tension built up in the mouth naturally amplifies them into stronger sounds.

**2. When pronunciation naturally shifts to an easier sound**

Now, let's look at the second case—when sounds shift naturally to make pronunciation smoother.

When an **Amplifier consonant** (ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ) meets a **Nasal consonant** (ㄴ, ㄹ, ㅁ, ㅇ), the tension created by the Amplifier consonant weakens due to the nasal sound. This makes the pronunciation **softer and more fluid**.

**When ㄱ Meets ㄴ, ㄹ, or ㅁ → It Becomes ㅇ (ng sound)**

Okay, now it's time to look at how ㅎ changes things.

There are three main ways:

**Way 1: ㅎ Goes Silent**

First, ㅎ goes silent when it's followed by ㅇ:

Also, when it’s followed by ㄴ ㄹ ㅁ ㅇ, Nasal 받침, ㅎ goes silent.

Some people do pronounce ㅎ, but you can barely hear the difference.

**Way 2: ㅎ Aspirates Other Letters**

Let me remind you of these 4 consonants:

* ㄱ becomes ㅋ
* ㄷ becomes ㅌ
* ㅂ becomes ㅍ
* ㅈ becomes ㅊ

One thing to note: ㅅ doesn't follow this rule because it doesn't have its own aspirated counterpart.

**Amplifier 받침 (ㄱ/ㄷ/ㅂ) + ㅎ**

And the same thing happens when it's the other way around:

So it’s 박하 (바카) 맏형 (마텽) 입학 (이팍) 젖혀 (저쳐)

**Way 3: ㅎ Becomes ㄷ**

ㅎ becomes ㄷ only when it's followed by ㄴ:

쌓네 > 싿네 > 싼네

좋네 > 졷네 > 존네

And remember how ㄷ, when it meets ㄴ or ㅁ, becomes ㄴ?

That's what's happening in the second step here.

**Bonus: Palatalization (구개음화)**

Before we move on to compound 받침, there's one final quirky rule. We call this palatalization, or 구개음화 in Korean.

You can think of it like this:

In English, D is often pronounced like J—like in "Did you" (D'jew).

And there are times when T is pronounced like CH, especially in British English—like "Tuesday" (Chewsday).

Here’s how it works.

**1. ㄷ + 이/여 → ㄷ becomes ㅈ**

If a syllable ends with ㄷ and the next one starts with 이 or 여, ㄷ becomes ㅈ.

굳이 > 구디 X 굳이 > 구지 O

**2. ㅌ + 이/여 → ㅌ becomes ㅊ**

If it's ㅌ (the aspirated version of ㄷ) followed by 이 or 여, it becomes ㅊ.

같이 > 가티 X 같이 > 가치 O
붙여 > 부텨 X 붙여 > 부쳐 O

**3. ㄷ + 히/혀 → becomes ㅊ (with ㅎ aspiration)**

When ㄷ meets 히 or 혀, where ㅎ acts as an aspirator, it becomes ㅊ.

묻히다 > 뭊히다 > 무치다

The truth is, Korean consonant assimilation rules are extensive.

Think of it like English spelling and pronunciation.

Words like "comb," "tomb," and "bomb" all sound different, right?

I recommend learning pronunciation as you learn actual words.
````

````md
### The Concept of Re-syllabification (Flowing Sounds)

The most basic sound change occurs when a syllable ending in a consonant is followed by a syllable starting with the empty consonant ㅇ. In this case, the final consonant of the first syllable "flows" into the empty space of the next. The instructor demonstrates this with the word 한국어 (Korean language). While written as `한-국-어`, it is pronounced as `한구거` because the ㄱ moves to the empty ㅇ position. Similarly, 미국인 (American person) is written as `미-국-인` but pronounced as `미구긴`. This eliminates the need for a glottal stop or a pause between syllables, making the word much easier to say in one continuous breath.

---

### Understanding 받침 (Batchim) and Base Consonants

받침 refers to any consonant or pair of consonants located at the bottom of a Korean syllable. While many consonants can be written in the 받침 position, only seven sounds are actually possible to pronounce at the end of a syllable without a following vowel: ㄱ, ㄴ, ㄷ, ㄹ, ㅁ, ㅂ, and ㅇ. If a syllable ends in a letter like ㅅ, ㅈ, or ㅊ, and there is no following vowel to pull the sound forward, the sound must "collapse" into one of these seven sounds.

To simplify the many rules, the instructor introduces five "base consonants": ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ, ㅅ, and ㅈ. These are the plain versions of consonants that often undergo changes—specifically tensing or aspiration—when they interact with other letters. Learning how these five specific sounds react to their environment allows a student to predict most sound changes without memorizing dozens of individual exceptions.

---

### Tensing and Aspiration Rules

Tensing occurs when a base consonant follows another consonant. The instructor uses the word 학교 (school) to illustrate this. Written as `학-교`, the ㄱ at the bottom of the first syllable causes the following ㄱ to become tensed, resulting in the pronunciation `학꾜`. This happens naturally because the tongue must pause to finish the first ㄱ, creating pressure that releases as a double consonant sound. Other examples include 찾다 (to find), which is pronounced `찻따`, and 먹자 (let's eat), pronounced `먹짜`.

Aspiration happens when a base consonant meets the letter ㅎ. Whether the ㅎ comes before or after the base consonant, the two sounds combine to form a "strong" or aspirated version of that consonant. For instance, the word 많다 (to be many) contains a ㅎ in the batchim that interacts with the following ㄷ, resulting in the pronunciation `만타`. The instructor also shows how ㅂ and ㅎ combine to form ㅍ, as seen in the transition from `반-파` to a strong `ㅍ` sound.

---

### Nasalization and Liquid Consonant Interactions

Nasalization occurs when the consonants ㅂ, ㄱ, or ㄷ (and their equivalents) appear before the nasal consonants ㄴ or ㅁ. The instructor highlights the common verb ending ~습니다. Although written with a ㅂ, it is pronounced as `~슴니다` because the ㅂ softens into a ㅁ sound when it hits the ㄴ. This also applies across word boundaries in a sentence if the words are spoken quickly together, such as 밥 먹어 (eat rice) sounding like `밤머거`.

The liquid consonant ㄹ has unique interactions. When ㄹ meets ㄴ, or vice versa, the ㄴ almost always transforms into an ㄹ sound. The word 혼란 (confusion) is written as `혼-란` but pronounced `홀란`. Similarly, the number twenty-four, 스물 넷, is pronounced `스물렛`. Another specific rule involves the combination of ㄱ or ㅇ followed by ㄹ; in these cases, the ㄹ often changes to an ㄴ sound to make the transition smoother, such as 강릉 (Gangneung) being pronounced `강능`.

---

### Palatalization and Special Cases

Palatalization occurs with the consonants ㄷ and ㅌ when they are followed by the vowel 이. Instead of a literal "di" or "ti" sound, they transform into ㅈ and ㅊ respectively. The instructor provides the common examples 같이 (together), pronounced `가치`, and 굳이 (stubbornly/pointlessly), pronounced `구지`.

Finally, the instructor touches on a few miscellaneous rules. The diphthong 의 is pronounced as `에` when used as a possessive marker, but as `이` when it follows a consonant (e.g., 희망 is pronounced `히망`). When a double batchim is present, usually only one of the two consonants is pronounced, such as 읽다 (to read) being pronounced `익따`. Lastly, a ㅎ in the batchim position often disappears entirely when followed by a vowel, as seen in 많아요 (there are many), which is pronounced `마나요`.
````

````md
## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the concept of sound change rules in Hangul, explaining that these rules exist to make the language easier, faster, and more natural to speak. Rather than being arbitrary hurdles, these rules reflect the physical reality of how sounds flow into one another in rapid speech. The lesson emphasizes that while the list of rules may seem daunting, many of them occur naturally for learners through regular exposure and practice.

The learning arc begins with a demonstration of why certain syllables cannot be pronounced as written without adding extra vowels. From there, the instructor categorizes the most common sound changes, including bottom consonant (받침) simplifications, tensification (making sounds "double"), and nasalization. The goal is not for students to memorize every technical name for these rules, but to become aware of their existence so they can refer to them when they encounter words that sound different from their spelling.

---

### Why Sound Change Rules Exist
The instructor explains that sound change rules make Hangul possible to read in a way that sounds natural. For example, the word `맛이` is pronounced as `마시` because the final consonant flows into the empty circle (이응). However, if the syllable `맛` stands alone, it cannot be pronounced as "ma-sh" or "ma-s" without adding an extra vowel like `으` (resulting in `마스`). To avoid adding non-existent vowels, Korean uses sound change rules to simplify the final consonant sound. In this case, `맛` is pronounced as `맏`, and `낮` is pronounced as `낟`. These rules ensure that speech remains smooth and efficient.

---

### Tips for Learning These Rules
Before diving into the specific rules, the instructor offers several disclaimers to ease student anxiety. First, sound change rules are meant to make Korean easier, not harder. Second, most of these changes will occur naturally as you try to speak at a normal pace; your mouth will often take the path of least resistance. Third, there is no need to stress about memorizing every rule immediately. Even native speakers often cannot name these rules; they simply use them because they sound better. If a learner is ever unsure of a word's pronunciation, they are encouraged to check a dictionary like Naver, which provides the phonetic spelling for every entry.

### Rule 1: Simplifying the Bottom Consonant (받침)
When a syllable ends with a consonant that cannot be pronounced as-is in the final position, it simplifies to a more basic sound. Specifically, the consonants `ㅅ, ㅆ, ㅈ, ㅊ, ㅌ, ㅎ` all become pronounced as a `ㄷ` sound when they are at the end of a syllable and not followed by a vowel. The instructor provides several examples:
- `맛` becomes `맏`
- `밎` becomes `믿`
- `갖` becomes `갇`
- `빛` becomes `빋`
- `밭` becomes `받`
- `낯` becomes `낟`

Conversely, consonants that can be pronounced normally at the end of a syllable do not change. These include `ㄱ, ㄴ, ㄷ, ㄹ, ㅁ, ㅂ, ㅇ`.

---

### Rule 2: Pronouncing ㄲ, ㅋ, and ㅍ
Similar to the first rule, double or strong consonants at the end of a syllable simplify to their basic versions. The double consonant `ㄲ` and the strong consonant `ㅋ` both become a regular `ㄱ` sound. The strong consonant `ㅍ` becomes a regular `ㅂ` sound. Examples shown on screen include:
- `꺾` becomes `꺽`
- `부엌` becomes `부억`
- `잎` becomes `입`

---

### Rule 3: Tensification Between Two Syllables
This is an extremely common rule where a consonant becomes a "double" (tense) consonant when it follows another consonant. When the first syllable ends in any consonant sound except `ㅁ, ㄴ, ㄹ`, and the second syllable begins with `ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ, ㅅ, ㅈ`, that second consonant is pronounced as its double version (`ㄲ, ㄸ, ㅃ, ㅆ, ㅉ`). The instructor demonstrates this with `학교 (school)`, noting that it naturally sounds like `학꾜` because the `ㄱ` at the end of the first syllable adds stress to the next sound. Other examples include:
- `받다` becomes `받따`
- `어떤` becomes `어떤` (Note: the `ㄴ` here is an exception mentioned later)
- `백보` becomes `백뽀`
- `국수` becomes `국쑤`
- `먹자` becomes `먹짜`
- `없다` becomes `업따`

---

### Rule 4: The Disappearing and Transforming ㅎ
The consonant `ㅎ` is relatively weak and often changes based on its surroundings. When `ㅎ` appears between two syllables and is followed by the empty consonant (이응), the `ㅎ` sound simply disappears. For example:
- `싫어` becomes `시러`
- `많아` becomes `마나`
- `좋아` becomes `조아`

However, if `ㅎ` comes before or after a regular consonant (`ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ, ㅈ`), it combines with that consonant to make it a strong version (`ㅋ, ㅌ, ㅍ, ㅊ`). This is shown in:
- `착하다` becomes `차카다`
- `많다` becomes `만타`
- `좋다` becomes `조타`
- `어떻게` becomes `어떠케`
- `잃다` becomes `일타`
- `못하다` becomes `모타다` (Note: `못` first becomes `몯`, then the `ㄷ` combines with `ㅎ` to become `ㅌ`)

---

### Rule 5: Tensification After ㄹ
Similar to Rule 3, the letter `ㄹ` at the end of a syllable often causes the following consonant (`ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ, ㅅ, ㅈ`) to become double. This happens in words like `밀당 (밀땅)`, `일자리 (일짜리)`, and `물고기 (물꼬기)`. However, the instructor notes this is not universal. Common exceptions where tensification does *not* occur after `ㄹ` include `얼굴`, `웰빙`, and `알바`.

---

### Rule 6: Nasalization of ㅂ
When a syllable ends with a `ㅂ` sound (including `ㅂ, ㅃ, ㅍ`) and the next syllable begins with `ㄴ` or `ㅁ`, the `ㅂ` sound changes to a `ㅁ` sound. This makes the transition between syllables much smoother. The most common example is the polite ending `합니다`, which is pronounced as `함니다`. Other examples include:
- `입냄새` becomes `임냄새`
- `밥 먹어` becomes `밤 머거`
- `겁나` becomes `검나`
- `잡는` becomes `잠는`
- `입 모양` becomes `임 모양`

---

### Rule 7: Nasalization of ㄱ
Similar to the `ㅂ` rule, if a syllable ends with a `ㄱ` sound and the next syllable starts with `ㄴ` or `ㅁ`, the `ㄱ` changes to an `ㅇ` (ng) sound. This is famously heard in the word for the Korean language: `한국말`, which is pronounced `한궁말`. Other examples include:
- `백만` becomes `뱅만`
- `국물` becomes `궁물`
- `목마르다` becomes `몽마르다`
- `학년` becomes `항년`
- `볶는` becomes `봉는`
- `부엌 문` becomes `부엉 문`

---

### Rule 8: Nasalization of ㄷ and Others
Consonants that simplify to a `ㄷ` sound at the end of a syllable (`ㄷ, ㅅ, ㅈ, ㅎ`) will change to a `ㄴ` sound if the following syllable begins with `ㄴ`. This is demonstrated in:
- `몇 년` becomes `면 년`
- `맞나` becomes `만나`
- `믿는` becomes `민는`
- `닫는` becomes `단는`
- `있나` becomes `인나`
- `잇몸` becomes `인몸`

---

### Rules 9–12: Complex Changes Involving ㄹ
The instructor groups several rules involving the letter `ㄹ`, which often forces neighboring consonants to become nasal or lateral sounds:
- **Rule 9:** If `ㅂ` is followed by `ㄹ`, the `ㅂ` becomes `ㅁ` and the `ㄹ` becomes `ㄴ`. Example: `합량` becomes `함냥`.
- **Rule 10:** If `ㄱ` is followed by `ㄹ`, the `ㄱ` becomes `ㅇ` and the `ㄹ` becomes `ㄴ`. Example: `백리` becomes `뱅니`.
- **Rule 11:** If `ㅇ` or `ㅁ` is followed by `ㄹ`, the `ㄹ` becomes `ㄴ`. Example: `음료` becomes `음뇨`. An exception is the loanword `장르 (genre)`, which stays `장르`.
- **Rule 12:** If `ㄴ` comes before or after `ㄹ`, the `ㄴ` changes to an `ㄹ` sound. Example: `신라` becomes `실라` and `일년` becomes `일련`.

---

### Rule 13: Palatalization (ㄷ/ㅌ + ㅣ)
When a syllable ends in `ㄷ` or `ㅌ` and is followed by a syllable starting with the vowel `ㅣ`, the `ㄷ` becomes `ㅈ` and the `ㅌ` becomes `ㅊ`. This is a very specific but common rule:
- `맏이` becomes `마지`
- `같이` becomes `가치`
- `굳이` becomes `구지`
- `붙여` becomes `부쳐`

---

### Rule 14: Irregulars and Final Thoughts
The instructor concludes by noting that there are always irregular words that do not follow these standard rules. These should be learned on a case-by-case basis as they are encountered. Examples include `십육 (심뉵)`, `깻잎 (깬닙)`, and `여권 (여꿘)`. Having completed these sound change rules, the student has now finished the foundational study of the Hangul writing system and is ready to move on to grammar and vocabulary.
````

````md
### The Basic Rule of ㄴ Addition
The ㄴ 첨가 rule primarily occurs in compound words—words formed by sticking two independent words together to create a new meaning. For the rule to trigger, two specific conditions must be met. First, the first word in the compound must end in a consonant (a 받침). Second, the second word must begin with the vowel sound `이` or a y-vowel sound such as `야`, `여`, `요`, or `유`.

When these conditions are met, a `ㄴ` sound is inserted at the beginning of the second word. The instructor illustrates this by showing how the placeholder consonant `ㅇ` (which has no sound value at the start of a syllable) is effectively replaced by a `ㄴ` sound in speech. For example, in the word `끝말잇기`, the first part `끝말` ends in a consonant, and the second part `잇기` starts with `이`. This results in the `이` sound transforming into a `니` sound during pronunciation.

---

### Distinguishing Compound Words from Particles
A crucial distinction made in the lesson is that ㄴ 첨가 only applies to compound words, not to words followed by grammatical particles. The instructor provides the example `사람이` (a person + subject marker). Although `사람` ends in a consonant and `이` is the following vowel, this is not a compound word; `이` is simply a particle. Therefore, the standard liaison rule applies, and it is pronounced as `사라미`, never as `사람니`.

Similarly, the rule does not apply within a single, non-compound word. The word `높이` (height) is a single unit. Even though it contains a consonant followed by the `이` sound, it is pronounced as `노피` through regular liaison. The rule is strictly reserved for instances where two distinct words are combined, such as `끝` (end) and `말` (word) forming `끝말`, which then combines with `잇기`.

---

### Vowel Triggers: The "Y" Sounds
While `이` is the most common trigger, the instructor explains that the rule applies to any variation of the `이` sound, specifically the y-vowels. These include:
- **야** (y + a)
- **여** (y + eo)
- **요** (y + o)
- **유** (y + u)

The instructor demonstrates that if you say these sounds quickly, they all begin with an "ee" (`이`) mouth position. Therefore, any second word starting with these sounds will trigger the addition of a `ㄴ`. Examples provided include `깻잎` (perilla leaf), which is a combination of `깨` and `잎`. Although it contains a `사이시옷` (middle `ㅅ`), the pronunciation becomes `깬닙`. Another example is `앞일` (future work/things to come), which combines `앞` (front) and `일` (work), resulting in the pronunciation `암닐`.

---

### Interaction with Other Sound Change Rules
The ㄴ 첨가 rule often acts as a catalyst for further sound changes. The instructor uses `서울역` (Seoul Station) to show this "domino effect."
1. **Step 1:** Combine `서울` (Seoul) and `역` (station).
2. **Step 2:** Apply ㄴ 첨가 because `서울` ends in a consonant and `역` starts with `여`. This creates a theoretical pronunciation of `서울녁`.
3. **Step 3:** Apply the Lateralization rule. When `ㄹ` meets `ㄴ`, the `ㄴ` typically changes to a `ㄹ` sound.
4. **Result:** The final pronunciation is `서울력`.

A similar process occurs with `담요` (blanket). The word `담` (fur/fleece) and `요` (mattress) combine. The `요` becomes `뇨` due to ㄴ 첨가, resulting in the pronunciation `담뇨`.

While the rule is technically for compound words, the instructor notes that it can sometimes occur between two separate words in a sentence if they are spoken very quickly as a single unit. He uses the phrase `옷 입다` (to wear clothes) as an example.
- **Standard pronunciation:** `오딥따` (following regular liaison and fortis rules).
- **Compressed pronunciation:** If spoken rapidly, `입` can take on the `ㄴ`, becoming `닙`. This then causes the `ㅅ` in `옷` to nasalize into a `ㄴ` sound.
- **Result:** `온닙따`.
Both pronunciations are considered acceptable, though the standard version is more common.

The instructor concludes by noting that like many linguistic rules, there are exceptions. The most notable exceptions are the days of the week, such as `일요일` (Sunday) and `월요일` (Monday). Despite meeting the criteria, these are never pronounced as `일뇨일` or `월뇨일`.

Because it can be difficult to predict every instance of this rule, the instructor recommends using the Naver Dictionary. If the pronunciation shows an added `ㄴ` or `ㄹ` that isn't in the spelling, it is likely due to the ㄴ 첨가 rule.
````

````md
# Go Billy | What happened to the ㄴ/ㄹ? Simplifying 두음법칙 | Korean FAQ

## Metadata
- **Channel:** Go Billy
- **URL:** https://youtu.be/I4yOtmvhJ8c

## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the concept of 두음법칙, which literally translates to the "rule of initial sounds" (두 meaning head or initial, 음 meaning sound, and 법칙 meaning law or rule). This linguistic rule describes how certain words derived from Chinese characters (한자) are written and pronounced in Korean when specific consonants appear at the very beginning of a word. Specifically, the rule dictates changes for words that would originally start with the letters ㄹ or ㄴ.

The instructor emphasizes that while the complete academic rules for 두음법칙 are complex, they can be condensed into a few manageable patterns. Crucially, this rule only applies to 한자어 (Sino-Korean words) and only when the target sound is the "initial sound" of the entire word. It is presented as a natural phenomenon of language—a "path of least resistance"—where sounds were historically removed or altered to make them easier for Korean speakers to pronounce.

---

### The First Rule: ㄹ or ㄴ Changing to ㅇ
The first major rule states that whenever a Sino-Korean word originally starts with the letter ㄹ or ㄴ, and that letter is followed by one of six specific vowels, the initial consonant changes into ㅇ (which has no sound in the initial position). The six vowels that trigger this change are 이, 야, 여, 요, 유, and 예.

The instructor provides several common examples to illustrate this transformation. The word for woman, 여자, originally comes from characters that would be read as 녀자; however, because it starts with ㄴ followed by 여, the ㄴ is dropped to become 여자. Similarly, the word for age, 연세, comes from the character 연 (year), and the word for element, 요소, is written with ㅇ instead of its original initial sound. The word for dragon, 용, originally stems from the sound 룡. 

A significant point of confusion for learners is the surname Lee. While often romanized as "Lee" or "Rhee," it is written in South Korea as 이. The instructor notes that the famous actress 이다혜 (Lee Da-hae) writes her name with 이 even though the original 한자 reading is 리. Another example is the word 양심 (conscience), which follows this same pattern of dropping the initial consonant, as does 예의 (etiquette/manners).

---

### The Second Rule: ㄹ Changing to ㄴ
The second rule applies specifically to the letter ㄹ. When ㄹ appears at the beginning of a Sino-Korean word before vowels other than the six mentioned in the first rule, it does not disappear but instead changes into a ㄴ. The vowels that typically trigger this change include 아, 오, 우, 어, 에, and 외.

Examples of this rule include the word for tomorrow, 내일, which originates from the sound 네일. The word for senior citizen, 노인, would originally be read as 로인. Paradise is written as 낙원 instead of the original 락원, and the word for thunder, 뇌성, comes from 뢰성. The instructor also mentions 루각 (a pavilion) coming from 루각 and 능 (royal tombs) coming from 릉. In all these cases, the "harder" ㄹ sound at the start of the word is softened to a ㄴ for easier pronunciation.

---

### The "Initial Sound" Constraint and Exceptions
The instructor stresses that 두음법칙 is strictly an "initial sound" rule. If the same 한자 character appears anywhere else in a word besides the very first syllable, the rule does not apply, and the original sound is preserved. For instance, while 녀자 becomes 여자 at the start of a word, the ㄴ remains in the word 소녀 (young girl) because it is the second syllable. Similarly, while 연세 starts with ㅇ, the word 학년 (school year) keeps the ㄴ because it is not at the beginning.

There is a very specific exception regarding the sounds 열 and 율. When these sounds follow either a vowel or the consonant ㄴ, they change to 열 and 율 (dropping the initial ㄹ). For example, 비율 (ratio) is written this way because it follows a vowel, and 진열 (display) is written with ㅇ because it follows ㄴ. However, if they follow any other consonant, they remain as is, such as in the word 법률 (law), which keeps the 률 sound.

---

### Non-Applicability and Regional Differences
It is important to remember that 두음법칙 only applies to Sino-Korean words. It does not apply to native Korean words, such as the names of the letters themselves (리을, 니은) or the word 녀석 (fellow/guy). It also does not apply to loanwords from other languages, which is why words like 라면 (ramen), 뉴스 (news), and 레몬 (lemon) retain their initial ㄹ or ㄴ sounds. Furthermore, the rule only affects the start of a word; a word like 진리 (truth) is written as is and does not become "진이."

Finally, the instructor points out a major geopolitical distinction: these rules only apply to the standard language of South Korea. In North Korea, 두음법칙 is not followed. Therefore, words like 룡 (dragon) and 녀자 (woman/girl) are written and pronounced with their original initial consonants in the North, whereas they are 용 and 여자 in the South.
````

````md
### The Concept of Unreleased Sounds: ㄱ, ㄴ, ㄷ

The instructor begins with the basic rule of Korean final consonants: they are not extended or fully released like in English. For the letter ㄱ (giyeok), she uses the word `약 (medicine)`. While a romanization might suggest "yag," it is actually pronounced `약`. The speaker prepares to make the "g" sound but stops the airflow immediately. She demonstrates this with `미국 (USA)`, which is not "mi-gook" with a trailing "k" sound, but a clean `미국`. Another example is `책 (book)`, which is `책`, not "chaek."

For ㄴ (nieun), the instructor notes that this is relatively easy for English speakers because the "n" sound isn't typically extended in English either. She provides the example `한국 (Korea)`, where the ㄴ in `한` is a short, clipped sound. Other examples include `펜 (pen)`, `핸드폰 (cell phone)`, and `편지 (letter)`.

The consonant ㄷ (digeut) is described as trickier. In the word `곧 (soon)`, the final sound is not a full "d." The instructor explains that the tongue should touch the area underneath the front teeth, and the sound is cut off before the "d" is released: `곧`. She provides further examples like `옷 (clothes)`—noting that ㅅ often sounds like ㄷ in the final position—`얻다 (to earn)`, and `믿다 (to believe)`.

---

### Liquid and Nasal Consonants: ㄹ, ㅁ, ㅂ

The consonant ㄹ (rieul) in the final position sounds like an "l." In the word `물 (water)`, the sound is `물`, not an extended "mull." The instructor demonstrates with `선물 (gift)`, where the first syllable `선` uses the ㄴ rule and the second syllable `물` uses the ㄹ rule. She also shows `달력 (calendar)`, pointing out the final ㄱ in `력`.

For ㅁ (mieum), the instructor emphasizes that the lips must press together and stay closed. In English, a word like "jam" might have a trailing "m" sound, but the Korean word `잼 (jam)` ends abruptly with the lips sealed: `잼`. She also uses `냄새 (smell)` and `엄마 (mom)` to illustrate the nasal ㅁ sound.

The consonant ㅂ (bieup) follows the unreleased rule as well. In `입 (mouth)`, the lips touch softly at the end but do not release a "p" or "b" puff of air. This is also seen in `밥 (rice/meal)`, where the lips gently close to finish the syllable: `밥`.

---

### The "ㄷ-Sound" Group: ㅅ, ㅈ, ㅊ

A major point of confusion for learners is that many different consonants sound like a ㄷ (digeut) when they are in the final position. The instructor explains that ㅅ (siot) in `옷 (clothes)` is pronounced as `옫`. Similarly, `곧 (soon)` and `곳 (place)` are pronounced exactly the same way despite having different final consonants (ㄷ and ㅅ).

She introduces ㅈ (jieut) and ㅊ (chieut) as "sisters" to the ㄷ sound. In the word `낮 (daytime)`, the ㅈ sounds like a clipped ㄷ: `낟`. The word `낯 (face)` with a ㅊ final consonant also sounds like `낟`. The instructor demonstrates this with `민낯 (bare face)`, where the ㅊ is unreleased and sounds like a ㄷ. She reminds students that for all these sounds, the tongue must touch behind the front teeth to stop the air.

---

### The Velar Group: ㄱ, ㅋ, ㄲ

Just as several letters sound like ㄷ, the letters ㅋ (kieuk) and ㄲ (ssang-giyeok) sound like ㄱ (giyeok) when they are in the final position. The instructor calls these "sisters." For example, `부엌 (kitchen)` ends in ㅋ, but it is pronounced as `부억`. The word `밖 (outside)` ends in ㄲ, but it is pronounced as `박`. She also provides the example `해질녘 (sunset)`, where the final ㅋ is pronounced as a ㄱ. On their own, `박`, `밖`, `밬` would all sound identical.

---

### The Unique Case of ㅇ and ㅎ

The instructor explains ㅇ (ieung) using a "nothing" analogy. When ㅇ is at the beginning of a syllable, it has no sound (nothing). However, when it is a 받침, it produces an "ng" sound. She demonstrates this with `고양이 (cat)`, where both the first and second syllables end in ㅇ, and `성공 (success)`. She also mentions `영어 (English)` and explains that `한국어 (Korean language)` comes from `한국 (Korea)` + `어 (language)`.

The final consonant ㅎ (hieut) is the most complex because it rarely has a sound of its own; instead, it changes the sound of the following consonant. This is the introduction to "Consonant Assimilation." The instructor outlines five specific rules for ㅎ:
1. **ㅎ + vowel:** The ㅎ sound is deleted. `좋아요` is pronounced `조아요`.
2. **ㅎ + ㄴ:** The ㅎ changes to a ㄴ sound. `좋네` is pronounced `존네`.
3. **ㅎ + ㄱ:** The sounds combine to make ㅋ. `좋게` is pronounced `조케`.
4. **ㅎ + ㄷ:** The sounds combine to make ㅌ. `좋다` is pronounced `조타`.
5. **ㅎ + ㅈ:** The sounds combine to make ㅊ. `좋지` is pronounced `조치`.

The instructor notes that while you can technically follow these rules for any word, some combinations (like ㅎ + ㄹ) don't actually exist in the Korean language, so students only need to focus on these five common patterns.

---

### Introduction to Consonant Assimilation

To conclude, the instructor gives a "glimpse" of broader consonant assimilation rules to be covered in Part 2. She uses the word `국물 (soup)`. While a beginner might read it syllable-by-syllable as "guk-mul," it is naturally pronounced as `궁물`. The final ㄱ changes to an ㅇ sound because it is followed by the nasal ㅁ. She explains that these rules aren't meant to make Korean harder; they actually exist to make the language easier and more "effortless" to speak by reducing the physical pressure required to move between certain sounds.
````

````md
### Linking Rules with 이응 (ㅇ)
When a syllable ending in a 받침 is followed by a syllable starting with the silent consonant ㅇ, the final consonant sound typically "slides" up to occupy the empty consonant position of the next syllable. The instructor demonstrates this with several examples: `석유 (oil)` is pronounced as 서규, and `기차역에 (at the train station)` is read as 기차여게.

However, there are notable exceptions to this general linking rule. The word `색연필 (colored pencil)` is not read as 재견필 as one might expect, but rather as 생년필. Similarly, when the ㄷ 받침 is followed specifically by the vowel 이, it undergoes palatalization, changing to a ㅈ sound. For example, `해돋이 (sunrise)` is read as 해도지, and `마디 (joint/knot)` is read as 마지. This is contrasted with `받은 (received)`, which follows the typical rule and is read as 바든 because the following vowel is 은, not 이.

A similar palatalization occurs with the ㅌ 받침 followed by 이, where it transforms into a ㅊ sound. The word `같이 (together)` is pronounced as 가치. The instructor provides a comparison: `밭에 (in the field)` follows the standard rule (바테), but `밭이 (the field [subject])` becomes 바치.

---

### Nasalization Rules (ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ + ㄴ, ㅁ)
When the "stop" consonants (ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ) are followed by nasal consonants (ㄴ, ㅁ), the stop consonants transform into their corresponding nasal sounds (ㅇ, ㄴ, ㅁ) to allow the air to flow continuously through the nose.

For the ㄱ family (ㄱ, ㅋ, ㄲ), the sound changes to ㅇ. The instructor illustrates this with `각 나라 (each country)`, which is pronounced 강나라, and `부엌 내 (inside the kitchen)`, which becomes 부엉내. The common phrase `한국말 (Korean language)` is read as 한궁말, and `악마 (devil)` is read as 앙마.

For the ㄷ family (ㄷ, ㅅ, ㅆ, ㅈ, ㅊ, ㅌ, ㅎ), the sound changes to ㄴ. For example, `닿나요 (does it touch?)` is read as 단나요, and `걷는다 (walking)` becomes 건는다. The instructor notes that even ㅅ changes to ㄴ in this context, such as `갓난아기 (newborn baby)` being read as 간난아기 and `잇몸 (gums)` as 인몸.

For the ㅂ family (ㅂ, ㅍ), the sound changes to ㅁ. This is most famously seen in the formal polite ending `-습니다`, where `감사합니다 (thank you)` is pronounced 감사함니다. Other examples include `덥니 (is it hot?)` pronounced 덤니 and `집만 (only the house)` read as 짐만.

---

### Lateralization and Liquid Consonant Rules (ㄴ + ㄹ)
When ㄴ and ㄹ meet, the ㄴ typically transforms into an ㄹ sound, resulting in a double "L" sound. This can happen whether the ㄴ is the 받침 or the following consonant. The instructor provides examples like `한류 (Hallyu/Korean wave)` read as 할류, `선릉 (Seolleung)` read as 설릉, and `분리 (separation)` read as 불리.

Conversely, in some specific combinations, an ㄹ following a nasal consonant (ㅇ, ㅁ) might change into an ㄴ sound. The instructor demonstrates this with the subway station `청량리`, which is pronounced 청량니. Similarly, `심리 (psychology)` is read as 심니, and `삼류 (third-rate)` is read as 삼뉴.

---

### Aspiration Rules with ㅎ
When the consonants ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ, or ㅈ meet the consonant ㅎ (either as a 받침 or the following consonant), they combine to form the aspirated versions of those sounds: ㅋ, ㅌ, ㅍ, and ㅊ.

- ㄱ + ㅎ = ㅋ: `북한 (North Korea)` → 부칸, `식혜 (sweet rice drink)` → 시케.
- ㄷ + ㅎ = ㅌ: `맏형 (eldest brother)` → 마텽. (Note: In some cases like `갇혀서`, it becomes ㅊ sound: 가쳐서).
- ㅂ + ㅎ = ㅍ: `집회 (assembly/meeting)` → 지퓌.
- ㅅ + ㅎ = ㅌ: `못해 (cannot do)` → 모태.
- ㅈ + ㅎ = ㅊ: `좋지 (good, right?)` → 조치.

When ㅎ is followed by ㅇ, the ㅎ sound often disappears entirely, as in `좋아 (good/like)` being read as 조아.
````

- 원문 줄: 23~43

````md
### Rule 1: Pronunciation as ㅅ [s]

The final consonant ㅅ is pronounced as its original "s" sound when it and the following ㅇ belong to the same word or the same grammatical unit. This occurs most frequently when a noun is followed by a particle (postposition), as a noun and its particle are considered a single grammatical element in Korean.

The instructor provides several examples to illustrate this. First, the combination of the noun 옷 (clothes) and the topic marker 은 results in the pronunciation `[오슨]`. Because the noun and particle are one unit, the ㅅ simply slides over to the next syllable. Similarly, the noun 빛 (comb) combined with the particle 으로 (by/with) is pronounced as `[비스로]`. This rule also applies within a single word, such as the adverb 반드시 (straight/properly), which is pronounced exactly as written: `[반드시]`. In all these cases, the ㅅ sound is preserved because there is no grammatical boundary between the consonant and the vowel.

---

### Rule 2: Pronunciation as ㄷ [d]

When the ㅅ final consonant and the following ㅇ belong to separate grammatical elements, the ㅅ does not link directly to the following vowel. Instead, it follows the default final consonant pronunciation rule before connecting to the next sound. In Korean phonology, the final consonants ㄷ, ㅅ, ㅈ, ㅊ, ㅌ, and ㅎ are all neutralized to a ㄷ [d] sound when they are at the end of a syllable and not immediately followed by a suffix or particle.

The instructor demonstrates this with the phrase 못 읽어요. Here, 못 is an adverb meaning "cannot" and 읽어요 is a verb meaning "read." Since they are separate words, the ㅅ in 못 is first realized as its underlying ㄷ sound. When this ㄷ sound then meets the vowel in 읽어요, it links over, resulting in the pronunciation `[모딜거요]`. Another example is 맛없어요 (to be not delicious), composed of the noun 맛 (taste) and the verb 없어요 (to not have). Because these are separate grammatical elements, the ㅅ in 맛 becomes a ㄷ, leading to the pronunciation `[마덥써요]`.

---

### Important Exceptions: 맛있어요 and 멋있어요

The instructor highlights a significant exception to Rule 2 involving the words 맛있어요 (to be delicious) and 멋있어요 (to be cool/stylish). Technically, since 맛 (taste) and 멋 (coolness) are separate from the verb 있어요, they should follow Rule 2 and be pronounced as `[마디써요]` and `[머디써요]`. While these are technically the original correct pronunciations, the vast majority of Korean speakers overwhelmingly pronounce them as `[마시써요]` and `[머시써요]`. Because this usage became so common, the "s" sound pronunciation was eventually accepted as standard. Learners are encouraged to use the "s" sound for these two specific words as it sounds more natural to native speakers.
````

````md
The lesson concludes by noting that this rule is not limited to ㅅ; it applies to all final consonants that share the underlying ㄷ sound, including ㅈ, ㅊ, ㅌ, and ㅎ. The instructor compares two examples:
- **낮 아니에요** (It's not daytime): 낮 and 아니에요 are separate, so the ㅈ becomes ㄷ, pronounced as `[나다니에요]`.
- **낮에 갔어요** (I went in the daytime): 낮 and the particle 에 are one unit, so the ㅈ links normally, pronounced as `[나제 가써요]`.
- **밑 안 봤어요** (I didn't look underneath): 밑 and 안 are separate, so the ㅌ becomes ㄷ, pronounced as `[미단 봐써요]`.
- **밑으로 가요** (Go underneath): 밑 and the particle 으로 are one unit, so the ㅌ links normally, pronounced as `[미트로 가요]`.
````

````md
### ㄱ (Giyeok) Final Consonant Rules
When the final consonant is ㄱ, it undergoes several types of assimilation depending on what follows. If followed by ㄴ (Nieun), the ㄱ sound softens to an ㅇ (Ieung) sound. This is compared to the English word "ignore," where the 'g' sound naturally transitions into the 'n' sound. For example, `작년 (last year)` is pronounced as 장년, and `미국 날씨 (U.S. weather)` is pronounced as 미궁 날씨.

If ㄱ is followed by ㄹ (Rieul), a double transformation often occurs: the ㄱ becomes ㅇ and the ㄹ becomes ㄴ. This is seen in `자가격리 (self-quarantine)`, pronounced as 자가격니, and `기억력 (memory power)`, pronounced as 기엉녁. When ㄱ meets ㅁ (Mieum), it also softens to ㅇ, such as in `백만원 (one million won)` pronounced as 뱅만원, and `목말라요 (I'm thirsty)` pronounced as 몽말라요.

A special rule applies when ㄱ meets ㅎ (Hieut): the two combine to form a strong ㅋ (Kieuk) sound. This is demonstrated in `행복해요 (I'm happy)`, pronounced as 행보캐요, and `어떻게 생각해요? (What do you think?)`, where the ㄱ in 생각 and the ㅎ in 해요 combine to sound like 생가캐요.

---

### ㄴ (Nieun) and ㄹ (Rieul) Interaction
The interaction between ㄴ and ㄹ is one of the most common assimilation patterns. When ㄴ is the final consonant and is followed by ㄹ, the ㄴ typically changes to an ㄹ sound to create a "double ㄹ" sound, which is easier to pronounce. For example, `연락하다 (to contact)` is pronounced as 열락하다, and `온라인 수업 (online class)` is pronounced as 올라인 수업.

Conversely, if ㄹ is the final consonant and is followed by ㄴ, the ㄴ often changes to an ㄹ sound. `설날 (Lunar New Year)` is pronounced as 설랄, and `발냄새 (foot smell)` is pronounced as 발램새. The instructor notes that the "double ㄹ" sound is much more comfortable for Korean speakers than the ㄴ-ㄹ or ㄹ-ㄴ combination.

---

### ㄷ (Digeut) and Related Sound Rules
Final consonants that share the ㄷ sound (including ㅅ, ㅈ, ㅊ, ㅌ, ㅎ in certain positions) follow similar patterns. When these meet ㄴ, they soften to an ㄴ sound. For example, `걷는 중이에요 (I am walking)` is pronounced as 건는 중이에요, and `못 나가요 (I can't go out)` is pronounced as 몬 나가요.

When ㄷ or ㅌ is followed by the vowel 이 (i), a process called palatalization occurs, changing the sound to ㅈ or ㅊ. `같이 (together)` is pronounced as 가치, and `굳이 (obstinately)` is pronounced as 구지.

If ㄷ meets ㅎ, they combine to form a ㅌ sound, as in `닫히다 (to be closed)`, pronounced as 다치다 (note: here the combination with 'i' further shifts it to 'chi'). However, in words like `맏형 (eldest brother)`, it is pronounced as 마텽.

---

### ㅂ (Bieup) Final Consonant Rules
When the final consonant ㅂ is followed by ㄴ or ㅁ, it softens to an ㅁ sound. This is most famously heard in the polite ending `~습니다`, where the ㅂ is pronounced as ㅁ (습니다). Other examples include `감사합니다 (thank you)`, pronounced as 감사함니다, and `입냄새 (breath smell)`, pronounced as 임냄새.

If ㅂ is followed by ㄹ, it follows a pattern similar to ㄱ: the ㅂ becomes ㅁ and the ㄹ becomes ㄴ. `합리적 (reasonable)` is pronounced as 함니적. When ㅂ meets ㅎ, they combine to form the aspirated ㅍ (Pieup) sound, as in `복잡하다 (to be complicated)`, pronounced as 복자파다.

---

### ㅎ (Hieut) and Special Cases
The final consonant ㅎ is unique because it often becomes silent when followed by a vowel or ㅇ, as in `좋아요 (I like it)`, pronounced as 조아요. However, when ㅎ meets plain consonants like ㄱ, ㄷ, or ㅈ, it transforms them into their aspirated counterparts: ㅋ, ㅌ, and ㅊ.
- `좋고 (good and...)` → 조코
- `좋다 (to be good)` → 조타
- `좋죠 (It's good, right?)` → 조쵸

The instructor also covers "irregular" cases where the standard rules are bypassed by specific vocabulary, such as `한국 요리 (Korean cooking)` being pronounced as 한국 뇨리 and `색연필 (colored pencil)` as 생년필, rather than following the standard linking rules.
````

````md
## Teaching Points

Ji explains that Korean pronunciation is not always a literal reading of the letters on the page. He introduces several key concepts:

*   **The Linking Rule (Liaison):** When a syllable ending in a *받침* is followed by a syllable starting with the silent consonant *ㅇ*, the *받침* sound "moves over" to the next syllable. Ji illustrates this with examples like `잡아` (pronounced as `자바`) and `날아` (pronounced as `나라`).
*   **Consonant Assimilation:** This is the process where two consonants meeting at a syllable boundary influence each other's sounds. Ji breaks this down into three main categories:
    *   **Amplifiers (ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ):** These consonants create tension in the mouth. When followed by `ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ, ㅅ, ㅈ`, they "amplify" the next sound into a double consonant (e.g., `악기` becomes `악끼`).
    *   **Nasalization (Natural Sounds):** When an "Amplifier" meets a "Nasal" consonant (`ㄴ, ㄹ, ㅁ`), the tension is neutralized, and the sound becomes softer. For example, `ㄱ` becomes `ㅇ` (ng) before a nasal, as seen in `학년` (pronounced `항년`) and `한국말` (pronounced `한궁말`).
    *   **Nasal-to-Nasal Shifts:** Nasal consonants also influence each other. Ji teaches that `ㄴ` often changes to `ㄹ` when it meets another `ㄹ` (e.g., `한류` becomes `할류`), and `ㄴ` can shift toward `ㅁ` before another `ㅁ` (e.g., `눈물` becoming `눔물`).
*   **The "Wild Card" ㅎ:** Ji explains that `ㅎ` is unique because it can become silent when followed by `ㅇ` (e.g., `좋아` becomes `조아`) or when it follows a nasal consonant. It also acts as an "aspirator," turning plain consonants into their aspirated counterparts (e.g., `ㄱ + ㅎ = ㅋ`).
*   **Palatalization:** Ji introduces the rule where `ㄷ` or `ㅌ` followed by `이` or `여` changes to `ㅈ` or `ㅊ`. He uses the English example "Did you" (pronounced like "Did-jew") to explain why `같이` is pronounced `가치`.
````

````md
## Teaching Points

Ji explains that consonant assimilation is the process where the sound of a final consonant (받침) changes depending on the consonant that follows it. He categorizes these into several key behaviors:

*   **The "ㅎ" (h) Rules:** Ji describes ㅎ as a "wild card" with three main behaviors:
    *   **Silent ㅎ:** When ㅎ is followed by the silent consonant ㅇ, it becomes silent. For example, **좋아** is pronounced as **조아** (jo-a). It also becomes silent or nearly silent when it follows nasal consonants (ㄴ, ㄹ, ㅁ, ㅇ), such as **안해** becoming **아내** (a-nae).
    *   **Aspiration:** When ㅎ meets the "amplifier" consonants (ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ, ㅈ), it combines with them to create their aspirated counterparts (ㅋ, ㅌ, ㅍ, ㅊ). This works both ways (받침 ㅎ + consonant, or 받침 consonant + ㅎ). Examples include **놓고** → **노코** (no-ko) and **입학** → **이팍** (i-pak).
    *   **Shift to ㄷ/ㄴ:** In specific cases, like when followed by ㄴ, the ㅎ sound shifts to a ㄷ sound and then assimilates further into a ㄴ sound. **좋네** is explained as shifting from **졷네** to **존네** (jon-ne).

*   **Nasal Assimilation:** Ji teaches that nasal consonants (ㄴ, ㅁ) often "take over" the preceding sound to make it flow better.
    *   **ㅇ (ng) + ㄹ (l) = ㄴ (n):** On screen, Ji shows **정리** (organize) and explains it is pronounced **정니** (jung-ni).
    *   **ㅁ (m) + ㄹ (l) = ㄴ (n):** Similarly, **금리** becomes **금니**. Ji notes that while some natives enunciate clearly, many follow this "natural flow" rule.

*   **Palatalization:** This is a "quirky" rule where ㄷ or ㅌ sounds shift when followed by the vowels 이 (i) or 여 (yuh).
    *   **ㄷ + 이 → 지:** **굳이** is pronounced **구지** (gu-ji).
    *   **ㅌ + 이 → 치:** **같이** (together) is pronounced **가치** (ga-chi).
    *   **ㄷ + 히 → 치:** Through a combination of palatalization and aspiration, **닫히다** becomes **다치다** (da-chi-da).

## Lesson Flow

The lesson begins with a discussion on the optional nature of some pronunciation rules. Ji uses English examples like "data" vs. "day-ta" to explain that while rules exist, native speakers often vary in how strictly they follow them. Amira asks for a list of practice words for each rule, which Ji agrees to provide.

Ji then moves into the "ㅎ" rules. He explains the "Silent ㅎ" rule using **나아** and **좋아**. Amira practices repeating **좋아** after him. Next, they tackle aspiration. Ji shows how **놓고** becomes **노코**. Amira struggles slightly with the distinction between the plain and aspirated sounds but corrects herself with Ji's guidance. The final major topic is palatalization. Ji uses the English "Tuesday" (chews-day) comparison. He introduces **같이** (together) and **붙여** (to stick), explaining how the ㅌ shifts to a ㅊ sound. Amira follows the logic well, noting that the "natural" feel of the pronunciation helps her remember the rule.
````
