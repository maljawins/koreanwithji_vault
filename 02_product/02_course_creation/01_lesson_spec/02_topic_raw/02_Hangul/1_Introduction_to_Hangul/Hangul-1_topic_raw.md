````md
**1. The Importance of 한글**

Before you learn anything else, the very first thing you need to learn is the Korean alphabet—한글.

Now, some people seem to think they can learn Korean without knowing 한글 and if you're one of them, I strongly urge you to think again. Because I guarantee you'll end up nowhere.

Imagine trying to learn English without knowing ABCs.

한글 is extremely easy to read. In fact, it was **purposefully** **DESIGNED** to be easy to replace the Chinese writing system that was prevalent back in the day by King Sejong.

But as a result of that long history of Chinese influence, even today, approximately 60% of the Korean language is made up of words of Chinese origin. We call them Sino-Korean, or 한자 in Korean.

At this point, you might be wondering “Should I learn Chinese too?”

And my answer to that is “Absolutely not”

Yes, knowing 한자 can really help with your vocabulary, but most Korean speakers, including myself, use Sino-Korean words in our day to day life without knowing how to write in Chinese.

You can think of it like how you don’t have to learn Latin in order to speak English.

**2. Romanization**

한글 is a phonetic alphabet, meaning each character has its own sound. At first, you obviously have no other option but to learn each sound through romanization—which is essentially Korean written in English.

But once you learn 한글, you should immediately ditch romanization and forget it even existed.

Why? Because romanization only represents "similar" sounds. Not exact sounds.

Think of it this way: Romanization to 한글 is what Edge or Safari is to Chrome—you only use them **once** to download Chrome. Which is why I won't be using romanization for any lessons after 한글.

But don't worry. I'll also teach you how to memorize 한글 FAST—and more importantly, how to make it STICK to your brain so you'll never forget.

That's coming up in the lesson "How to Memorize 한글."

**3. How 한글 Works**

So first, let's learn the structure of 한글.

한글 works based on syllable blocks made up of consonants and a vowel. It consists of individual phonetic symbols that are arranged together to create syllable blocks.

So each syllable block like these - 간, 곤, 관 represents one syllable.

One thing to note: There always needs to be at least one consonant and one vowel in each syllable block.

Unlike English, where you can cram a bunch of consonants together in one syllable.

So, THIS doesn’t happen in Korean [Show the word: strength]

Each syllable always has a consonant at the top, a vowel either on the right, the bottom, or both, and can also have up to two consonants at the bottom.

These consonants at the bottom are called 받침 in Korean, which means "support."

So, the key take away would be that there can be top or bottom consonants and horizontal and/or vertical vowels.

And we have 14 consonants and 10 vowels and each character has its own name and sound.
````

````md
### The Problem with Romanization
The instructor strongly advises against using Romanization—the practice of writing Korean sounds using the English alphabet. He demonstrates its inconsistency by showing how a single word like 한글 can be written in multiple ways, such as Han-gul, Han-geul, or Hahn-gul. More complex examples like 독립문 (Independence Gate) illustrate how Romanization fails to capture actual pronunciation, as it might be written as Dongnimmum, Toklipmun, or Dog-Rib-Moon. Because Romanization cannot accurately represent Korean sounds, meanings, or spellings, the instructor insists that students commit to learning the native script immediately to avoid hitting a progress wall later.

---

### Syllable Structure and the Syllable Block
In Korean, individual letters are not written in a linear string like in English. Instead, they are grouped into "syllable blocks." A syllable is defined as any sound made with a single mouth movement. The instructor explains that there are six types of syllable blocks, but this lesson focuses on the most basic one: a consonant paired with a vertical vowel.

A critical rule of Hangul is that a consonant or a vowel cannot stand alone to produce a sound; every syllable must have at least one of each. For syllables using a vertical vowel (one that is taller than it is wide), the consonant is placed on the left and the vowel is placed on the right. This is visually represented as a "CV" (Consonant-Vowel) block.

---

### Stroke Order Principles
Stroke order is the specific sequence and direction used to draw the lines of each letter. The instructor explains that following the correct order is essential for writing quickly, developing a natural "flow" similar to English cursive, and ensuring the handwriting remains legible to others. The universal rule for Korean stroke order is twofold: strokes move from left to right and from top to bottom. Even if a letter looks the same when drawn "incorrectly," the instructor notes that the subtle differences in line quality become apparent when letters are combined, affecting the overall balance of the syllable block.
````

````md
### First Letters and Syllables
The lesson introduces one vowel and three consonants to demonstrate how syllables are formed and pronounced.

The first vowel is **아**, which sounds like the "a" in "father." On screen, the instructor demonstrates the two-stroke order: first, a long vertical line drawn from top to bottom; second, a short horizontal line drawn from the middle of the first line to the right.

The first consonant is **ㄱ**. It has a sound that falls between a "k" and a "g." It is written in a single stroke: moving to the right and then curving down. When combined with the vowel 아, it forms the syllable **가**.

The second consonant is **ㄴ**, which is similar to the English "n" sound. It is written in one stroke: starting at the top, moving down, and then turning to the right. When combined with 아, it forms the syllable **나**. The instructor notes that the Korean "n" is slightly different from the English "n," sounding more like "na" than a hard English "N."

The third consonant is **ㄷ**. This sound is between a "t" and a "d." It is written with two strokes: first, a horizontal line from left to right; second, a single line that starts at the left of the first stroke, moves down, and then turns to the right. When combined with 아, it forms the syllable **다**. The instructor emphasizes that this is not exactly a "T" or a "D," but a distinct sound: **타** (ta).

The lesson concludes with a practice drill of the three syllables:
- **가** (ga/ka)
- **나** (na)
- **다** (da/ta)
````

````md
### The Horizontal Vowel 오
The instructor introduces the vowel **오**, which carries a sound similar to the "o" in "oak tree." This vowel is categorized as a horizontal vowel because its primary shape is wider than it is tall. The stroke order consists of two steps: first, a short vertical stroke going downward, followed by a longer horizontal stroke drawn from left to right underneath the first stroke.

Because **오** is a horizontal vowel, it requires a different syllable block structure than the vertical vowels learned previously (like **아**). When combining a consonant with a horizontal vowel, the consonant is placed directly above the vowel. For example, combining the consonant **ㄱ** with **오** results in the syllable **고**. This contrasts with vertical vowels where the consonant is placed to the left.

---

### The Placeholder Consonant ㅇ
The consonant **ㅇ** is unique because it has two distinct roles. When it appears at the beginning of a syllable (before a vowel), it has no sound at all. It serves as a silent placeholder, allowing vowels to be written as complete syllable blocks, as Korean rules forbid starting a syllable block with a vowel alone. When placed at the end of a syllable, however, it creates an "ng" sound, similar to the end of the English word "song."

Visually, **ㅇ** is written as a single counter-clockwise circular stroke. The instructor warns learners not to confuse it with the box-shaped consonant **ㅁ**. By using this silent placeholder, we can now correctly write and pronounce vowels as full syllables:
- **아** (pronounced "a")
- **오** (pronounced "o")

---

### Three-Letter Syllable Blocks
The lesson introduces syllable blocks containing three letters: two consonants and one vowel. The third letter (the final consonant) is always placed at the very bottom of the block. The internal arrangement of the top portion depends on whether the vowel is vertical or horizontal.

For a vertical vowel like **아**, the first consonant is on the top left, the vowel is on the top right, and the final consonant is centered at the bottom. An example is **간** (ㄱ + ㅏ + ㄴ). For a horizontal vowel like **오**, the first consonant is on the top, the vowel is in the middle, and the final consonant is at the bottom. An example is **곤** (ㄱ + ㅗ + ㄴ).
````

````md
### Basic Vowels: 어, 우, 으, and 이
The vowel **어** is pronounced similarly to the "u" in "up" or the "o" in "done." It is written with two strokes: a short horizontal stroke from left to right, followed by a longer vertical stroke from top to bottom. When combined with the empty consonant ㅇ, it forms the syllable **어**. The instructor warns against confusing 어 with 오; while 오 has a stroke pointing up (like an "oak" tree), 어 has a stroke pointing to the left (toward the consonant).

The vowel **우** sounds like the "oo" in "moon." It is a horizontal vowel, meaning the consonant is placed above it. It is written with a long horizontal line from left to right and a short vertical line pointing down from the middle. Combined with the empty consonant, it becomes **우**.
````

#### 이·에/애·y모음

- 원문 줄: 31~52

````md
The vowel **이** sounds like the "ee" in "feet." It is a single vertical stroke from top to bottom. As a vertical vowel, the consonant is placed to its left, forming **이**.

---

### The Vowels 에 and 애
Korean has two vowels that are pronounced almost identically in modern speech: **에** and **애**. Both sound like the "e" in "net." 

**에** is written with three strokes: a short horizontal line to the right, followed by two long vertical lines. **애** is also written with three strokes but in a different order: a long vertical line, a short horizontal line to the right, and another long vertical line. While they sound the same, they are used to spell different words.

---

### Y-Vowels: 야, 여, 요, 유, 예, and 얘
Adding an extra short stroke to a basic vowel adds a "y" sound to the beginning. This creates a new set of vowels:

- **야** (from 아): sounds like "ya" in "yard."
- **여** (from 어): sounds like "yo" in "young."
- **요** (from 오): sounds like "yo" in "yo-yo."
- **유** (from 우): sounds like "you."
- **예** (from 에): sounds like "ye" in "yes."
- **얘** (from 애): sounds like "ye" in "yes" (identical to 예).

The instructor demonstrates the stroke orders for these, noting that the two short strokes are always written before the long strokes in 여, 요, 유, and 얘, but after the long stroke in 야 and 예.
````

````md
### Understanding Diphthongs

A diphthong is defined on screen as two vowels combined into one. To pronounce them, the speaker must say both vowels from left to right quickly. The stroke order follows the same logic: write each vowel from left to right, one at a time, using their standard stroke directions. The instructor emphasizes that even though some diphthongs may sound identical in modern Korean, learning their distinct spellings is essential for correct writing.

---

### The Seven Diphthongs

The first diphthong is **의**, a combination of **ㅡ** and **ㅣ**. While it is pronounced as `ui` when paired with the empty consonant **ㅇ**, it has a specific pronunciation rule: when used with any other consonant, it is almost always simplified to a plain **ㅣ** sound. For example, the syllable **희** is pronounced as `hi` rather than `hui`.

The next three diphthongs involve combinations with basic vertical and horizontal vowels. **와** is a combination of **ㅗ** and **ㅏ**, resulting in a `wa` sound. **워** combines **ㅜ** and **ㅓ** to create a `wo` sound. **위** combines **ㅜ** and **ㅣ** to produce a `wi` sound.

The final three diphthongs are notable because they are pronounced nearly identically in modern speech, all sounding like `wae` or `way`. These are **왜** (combination of **ㅗ** and **ㅐ**), **웨** (combination of **ㅜ** and **ㅔ**), and **외** (combination of **ㅗ** and **ㅣ**). The instructor notes that **외** is a "trick" diphthong; although it looks like a combination of **ㅗ** and **ㅣ**, it was originally a combination of **ㅗ** and **ㅔ** sounds. Despite the identical pronunciation, learners must distinguish them in writing to spell words correctly.

---

### Consonant Naming Rules

The instructor introduces the proper names for Korean consonants using a specific formula. To name a consonant, take the consonant itself and attach the vowel **이**. Then, follow it with the syllable **으** with that same consonant placed at the bottom as a batchim. For example, the letter **ㄴ** is named **니은** by following this pattern: **ㄴ** + **이** = **니**, then **으** + **ㄴ** = **은**.

There are three irregular consonants that do not follow this formula and must be memorized:
- **ㄱ** is named **기역** (not 기윽)
- **ㄷ** is named **디귿** (not 디읃)
- **ㅅ** is named **시옷** (not 시읏)

For vowels, the name is simply the sound of the vowel itself when written with the empty consonant **ㅇ** (e.g., the name of **ㅏ** is **아**). For double consonants, the word **쌍** (meaning "couple" or "pair") is added before the name of the base consonant, such as **쌍기역** for **ㄲ**.
````

````md
### The ㄴ vs ㄷ Confusion: Tongue Position

The primary reason ㄴ and ㄷ sound similar is that they share the same general point of articulation in the mouth. For both sounds, the tongue is placed against the alveolar ridge, right behind the upper teeth. The instructor illustrates this by drawing a diagram of the mouth, showing the tongue touching the top. Because the starting position is identical, a "lazy" or relaxed pronunciation of ㄴ can easily take on the percussive quality of a ㄷ.

However, there is a subtle difference in how much of the tongue makes contact. When pronouncing ㄴ, a larger surface area of the tongue presses against the roof of the mouth. In contrast, ㄷ typically uses just the tip of the tongue. While Koreans are attuned to this distinction and perceive the sounds as completely different, English speakers often miss this nuance and hear them as interchangeable when the speaker is not being overly emphatic.

---

### The Role of Nasal Sound and Vibration

A critical factor in this phonetic overlap is the amount of nasalization. In English, the letter N is pronounced with significant nasal airflow and relatively less vibration. Korean ㄴ, however, is produced with much less nasal sound than its English counterpart. To an English speaker's ear, which is trained to expect a strong nasal "hum" for an N-sound, the lack of that hum in the Korean ㄴ makes it sound more like a plosive ㄷ.

The instructor demonstrates this by comparing the English "na" with the Korean **나**. The English version has a distinct nasality that resonates in the nose, while the Korean version is "cleaner" and more focused on the release from the tongue position. This lack of heavy nasalization is what frequently causes learners to mishear **네** (yes) as **데**.

---

### The ㅁ vs ㅂ Confusion: Lip Position

A similar phenomenon occurs with the letters ㅁ and ㅂ. Just as with the previous pair, these two share a physical starting point: both are bilabial sounds made by closing the lips. Because the mouth motion is nearly identical, they can sound very similar in casual speech. The instructor provides the example of **문** (door) and **분** (minutes/person). Depending on the speaker's level of enunciation, these words can be difficult for learners to distinguish.

Again, nasal sound is the deciding factor. In English, M is highly nasally, while B is not. Korean ㅁ uses significantly less nasal airflow than the English M. When a Korean speaker says **마** or **문** without a strong nasal push, the resulting sound lacks the "M-ness" an English speaker expects, causing the ear to default to the closest non-nasal relative, which is ㅂ.

---

### Perception and Getting Used to the Sound

The instructor emphasizes that Koreans do not perceive themselves as swapping these letters. To a native speaker, a "lazy" **네** still sounds distinctly like **네** and never like **데**. The confusion exists entirely in the ear of the learner who is trying to map Korean sounds onto English categories (N vs D, or M vs B).

The solution for learners is simply increased exposure. The instructor shares his own experience: while he frequently misheard these sounds during his first few months of study—even mishearing **뭐** (what) as **부어**—the confusion vanished once his ears became accustomed to the specific phonology of Korean. Over time, the brain learns to identify the subtle cues of Korean consonants without relying on English nasal patterns.
````

````md
### Understanding Nasal Consonants
To understand why ㅁ sounds like B and ㄴ sounds like D, one must first understand nasal consonants. In linguistics, M and N are nasal sounds because their pronunciation requires air to flow through the nasal cavity. The instructor demonstrates this by having the viewer place a finger under their nose while humming an "mm" sound to feel the airflow. If you pinch your nose while trying to say "mm," the sound stops immediately because the air path is blocked.

Physiologically, this happens because of the velum, which is the back part of the roof of the mouth. When we pronounce nasal consonants, the velum is lowered, allowing air to escape through the nose. If the velum is raised, it obstructs that airflow, changing the quality of the resulting sound.

---

### Denasalization: The "Stuffy Nose" Effect
The instructors use the analogy of having a bad cold to explain denasalization. When someone has a stuffy nose, the sentence "My mommy makes me muffins on Mondays" naturally sounds like "My bubby bakes me buffins on Bundays." In Korean, native speakers often do not completely open the nasal cavity at the beginning of a word. This loss of nasal airflow is called denasalization.

In the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA), this is represented by a specific symbol (a tilde with a slash) above the letter. The less air that escapes through the nose, the more the Korean ㅁ sounds like an English B, and the more the Korean ㄴ sounds like an English D. This is particularly common in word-initial positions.

---

### Examples of Denasalization in Korean
The video provides several examples where this phenomenon is clearly audible. For the letter ㅁ, words like **모자 (hat)**, **미국 (the United States)**, and **미안해요 (I'm sorry)** often sound like they start with a B to English speakers. For the letter ㄴ, the word **네 (yes)** frequently sounds like "de," while **나무 (tree)** sounds like "damu," **노래 (song)** sounds like "dorae," and **누구 (who)** sounds like "dugu."

The instructor cites a dissertation by Dr. Young Shin Kim titled "An acoustic, aerodynamic and perceptual investigation of word-initial denasalization in Korean." The research found that native Korean speakers denasalized ㅁ roughly 76% of the time and ㄴ a staggering 94% of the time at the start of words. This means that the "B-like" and "D-like" pronunciations are actually the statistical norm, even if textbooks focus on the pure M and N sounds.

---

### Critical Rules and Misconceptions
A vital rule to remember is that denasalization only occurs at the beginning of words. In the middle of a word, Koreans always pronounce these consonants with full nasal airflow, meaning they will sound like a standard M or N without exception. Furthermore, native Korean speakers are generally unaware that they are making two different sounds. In their minds, the denasalized version and the pure nasal version are the same single entity.

The instructor also debunks a common misconception regarding tongue position. Some claim that ㄴ sounds like D because Koreans bite their tongue or place it differently than English speakers. However, the instructor demonstrates that even if you bite your tongue while saying "nice," it still sounds like an N. The only factor that makes it sound like "dice" is blocking the nasal airflow. While Koreans do tend to use the blade of the tongue against the alveolar ridge rather than just the tip (as English speakers do), this is a separate articulatory detail that does not cause the D-sound shift.

---

### Practical Advice for Learners
Learners often wonder if they must mimic this "stuffy nose" pronunciation to be understood. The instructors clarify that it is not necessary. Because native speakers perceive both versions as the same letter, they will understand you perfectly whether you use a pure M/N or a denasalized B/D at the start of a word. Choosing to use the denasalized version might make your Korean sound more "native," but it is entirely optional for clear communication.
````

````md
### Basic Consonants (자음)
The instructor introduces 14 basic consonants using visual mnemonics to help students associate the shape of the letter with its sound. For example, **ㄱ** is compared to a "gun" for the `g/k` sound, **ㄴ** to a "nose" for the `n` sound, and **ㅁ** to a "mouth" for the `m` sound. Other mnemonics include **ㄷ** as a "door" (`d`), **ㄹ** as a "rattlesnake" (a sound between `r` and `l`), **ㅂ** as a "bucket" (`b`), and **ㅅ** as a person "standing" (`s`).

A unique feature of the consonant **ㅇ** is highlighted: when it appears at the beginning of a syllable block, it has no sound of its own and acts as a placeholder for the following vowel. The instructor also demonstrates how **ㄱ** (gun) transforms into **ㅋ** by adding a stroke, which she describes as "pulling the trigger to kill," resulting in a sharper `k` sound. Similarly, **ㄷ** (door) becomes **ㅌ** (two doors) for the `t` sound. The remaining consonants include **ㅈ** (`j`), **ㅊ** (`ch`), **ㅍ** (`p`), and **ㅎ** (a man in a "hat" for the `h` sound).

---

### Basic Vowels (모음) and Syllable Construction
Korean vowels are categorized by their position relative to the consonant: "side vowels" and "down vowels." Side vowels like **ㅏ (a)**, **ㅑ (ya)**, **ㅓ (eo)**, **ㅕ (yeo)**, and **ㅣ (i)** are placed to the right of the consonant. Down vowels like **ㅗ (o)**, **ㅛ (yo)**, **ㅜ (u)**, **ㅠ (yu)**, and **ㅡ (eu)** are placed underneath. The instructor points out a consistent pattern: adding a second short stroke to a vowel adds a `y` sound to the beginning (e.g., **ㅏ** to **ㅑ**).

Syllable construction follows a strict Consonant + Vowel (CV) or Consonant + Vowel + Consonant (CVC) structure. The instructor demonstrates this by writing words like `나비 (butterfly)`, `도시 (city)`, and `아기 (baby)`. She emphasizes that a vowel can never stand alone in writing; it must always be preceded by a consonant, even if that consonant is the silent placeholder **ㅇ**.
````
