### The Base-10,000 System and Common Large Numbers
The foundation of large Korean numbers rests on the first four units: `십 (10)`, `백 (100)`, `천 (1,000)`, and `만 (10,000)`. The instructor notes that these have one, two, three, and four zeros respectively. Once you surpass `만 (10,000)`, the pattern of `십`, `백`, and `천` repeats as prefixes. For example, 100,000 is expressed as `십만 (ten ten-thousands)` and 1,000,000 is `백만 (one hundred ten-thousands)`. This pattern continues until you reach 10,000 of `만`, at which point the system introduces a new primary counter.

The next major unit is `억`, which represents 100,000,000 (one hundred million) and contains eight zeros. In practical terms, `억` is a very common number in Korea when discussing the price of real estate; an apartment in Korea will typically cost several `억`. Following the same logic, after `억`, `십억`, `백억`, and `천억`, the system moves to `조` (trillion), which has twelve zeros. Beyond `조` lies `경`, which has sixteen zeros. The instructor explains that `경` is roughly the limit for financial discussions, potentially appearing in contexts like national budgets or the combined wealth of multiple countries.

For contexts involving astronomy, biology, or physics—such as counting atoms in the universe—Korean has specific terms that go far beyond `경`. The instructor lists these in increments of four zeros: `해` (20 zeros), `자` (24 zeros), `양` (28 zeros), `구` (32 zeros), `간` (36 zeros), `정` (40 zeros), `제` (44 zeros), and `극` (48 zeros). **Sino-Korean Number**

Let's start with the easier one: nominal numbers - the Sino-Korean numbers.

I mentioned in the previous lesson that nominal numbers are for naming things like phone numbers, addresses, and so on.

While this is true, Korean also uses Sino-Korean numbers for money.

I'd say this is the only case where it's actually related to quantity.

**Numbers 1-10**

Now, let's get started.

One is 일, two is 이, three is 삼, four is 사, five is 오, six is 육, seven is 칠, eight is 팔, nine is 구, and ten is 십.

On a side note, sometimes you’ll hear people say 륙 for six instead of 육 especially when counting quickly after a syllable without a 받침 - 일, 이, 삼, 사, 오, 륙 – like this.

Why does this happen? You might ask.

Well, there's a rule called 두음법칙—known as the "Initial Sound Rule" in English. I'm not going to go too deep into this because you don't really need to know about it.

But if you're interested, by all means, Google "Initial Sound Rule in Korean."

Simply put: 오륙 flows off the tongue more easily than 오육. Similar to how in English we say "an apple" instead of "a apple" to avoid a jarring sound.

But both 육 and 륙 are perfectly correct. And it's something you'll pick up naturally along the way.

**Building Numbers**

So we just learned that 일, 이, 삼, 사, 오, 육, 칠, 팔, 구, 십 are the first 10 numbers.

And you can basically make any other number with these 10 numbers.

For example, if I want to say 11 in Korean, all I have to do is a bit of math: 10 plus 1.

You have 십, which means 10, and you have 일, which means 1.

Put them together and you get 십일—or 11.

What if I want to say 12?

Then I have 십 and add 이, which is 2.

The answer is 십이—literally "ten two."

What if you flip it?

Now it's 이십. It's really straightforward. There are two tens—in other words, 20.

Final example: what's 99? It's just nine tens plus nine: 구십구.

You can't do this in English though.

To describe this number, you can't just say "10 1"—it's "eleven." I mean, how could you have known? It's a whole new word.

And what about 20? Well, you can't say "two tens." It's "twenty."

My point is, this is so much easier than English numbers because you only need to know 10 different numbers to say any number up to 99.

**Why Korean Numbers Are Great**

See, the reason why the Korean nominal number system is so great is that everything is based on simple math like this.

And in my opinion, this is why Koreans are so good at math—because it's just much easier to do math in Korean

**Hundreds and Thousands**

Now, what if you wanted to go beyond 99? What's a hundred? Here it is: 백. But unlike English where we sometimes say "ONE hundred," we don't really say 일백 - it's just 백 in general.

Although seldom, you might hear people say 일백, it's definitely not common.

So, what would 538 be in Korean? That's 5 hundreds, three tens, plus eight: 오백삼십팔. You build numbers with smaller numbers.

Congratulations! Now you know how to say 999 different numbers in Korean.

Then what's a thousand? It's 천.

So what would 3,672 be? 삼천육백칠십이.

You see the pattern here? It's the same logic no matter how large the numbers get.

**Ten Thousand: 만**

Let's go higher.

According to the same logic, 10,000 would be 십천, right? No, it's not.

Korean has a separate word for ten thousand: 만. So twenty thousand would be 이만. Thirty thousand would be 삼만 and so on. Furthermore, a hundred thousand would be 십만 - ten 만. A million would be 백만, and ten million would be 천만.

Similar to what I previously said about 백 and Korean's not putting 일 in front of it to say 100, it's the same thing with 천 and 만. Normally you wouldn't hear people say 일천 or 일만.

**Exception: "One to Two..."**

One exception is for phrases like "one to two blank."

For example, "one to two hundred people" would be 일이백명, and "one to two thousand won" would be 일이천원.

But this is sort of the only case where this would happen.

**Large Numbers**

Now let's talk about bigger numbers.

When you're reading large numbers in English, you break them up into chunks of three digits. That's what the commas are for.

Here's what I mean: you have your ones, tens, and hundreds, and then you enter the thousands range. Then there's ten thousand, a hundred thousand, and a million, and this keeps on going. Every three more digits is a billion, another three is a trillion. The point is, for every three digits you add, you enter a new range.

Let’s take Gangnam Style’s view count, for instance. This chunk is the ones range, this one is the thousands, this one is the millions, and the final one is the billions. Thanks to chunks of three, I can very quickly tell that this number is 5 billion 704 million 804 thousand and 366.

5,704,804,366회

But in Korean, this happens every four digits, not three, and this is because Korean has a separate word for ten thousand, 만 like I mentioned earlier.

Think about it: in English, after a hundred thousand comes a million. It's not "a thousand thousand," but since Korean has an extra word for ten thousand, we can say 천만, and finally 일억, not 만만.

Noice how I put 일 in front of 억 to say 일억. I know I told you earlier that we don't do this with 백, 천, and 만. But when talking about huge numbers like sums of money, for example, Koreans commonly say 일억원.

Let’s read Gangnam Style view count but this time in Korean. Now, it'd be much easier to read this number if there are commas for every four digits because of what I mentioned previously. That would look something like this: 57,0480,4366

This chunk is the ones range, this next one is the 만 range, and the final one would be the 억 range. Then I'd quickly be able to tell that this number is 오십칠억 사백팔십만 사천삼백육십육

So, this is what I mean when I say that Korean numbers are broken into chunks of four digits.

So let's start from the beginning:

* 일 is one
* 십 is 10
* 백 is a hundred
* 천 is a thousand
* 만 is ten thousand
* 십만 is a hundred thousand
* 백만 is a million
* 천만 is 10 million
* 억 is a hundred million
* 십억 is a billion
* 백억 is 10 billion
* 천억 is a hundred billion
* 조 is a trillion
* 십조 is 10 trillion
* 백조 is 100 trillion
* 천조 is a quadrillion

What comes after 천억 is 조. After 천조, it’s 경, which is 10 to the 16th power or 10 quadrillion. 해 is 10 to the 20th power or 100 quintillion, and 자 is 10 to the 24th power or one septillion.

Notice how these powers are multiples of four, not three unlike in English. That's the most you'll ever need to know in basic Korean learning. Anything after that is complete overkill, and actually, before I even worked on this video, I didn't know what came after 해.

Apparently, there's a ton more, but not only have I never heard them, it's completely unnecessary information. When numbers get this big anyway, Koreans probably start referring to them in powers of ten.

Still, that doesn't stop you from how high you can count. The largest number you can say in Korean is 구자구해구경구조구천구백구십구만구천구백구십구. As I said, complete overkill.

**Negative Numbers & Zero**

Let's take it in the other direction then - the negatives. People mostly use negative numbers for either one of two things: math or temperature.

When we're doing math in Korean, we just say 마이너스 or "minus" in front of the number. So 마이너스오 would be minus five or negative five. As for temperature, we'll talk about that later on in this lesson.

So now you know how to say from negative 10 octillion to positive 10 octillion, except one very important number: zero.

Zero is 영, but it can also be called 공. It’s like how zero is also called "oh" in English - it's a similar concept. 공 is more commonly used than 영, but this depends on the situation.

For example, we use 공 for phone numbers. Korean phone numbers start with 010. Koreans would pronounce this as 공일공, not 영일영.

Other things like zip codes and addresses also usually use 공. On the other hand, you would often use 영 in math. 영 is also used for temperature, which again I'll talk about later, and for things like 1-0 scores or zero points. So this score would be 일 대 영 with 대 meaning versus.

Moreover, when talking about things like zero points, like a test score, we could also say 빵점 for zero, 점 is a counter word for scores, by the way. And 빵 is an informal word, just like how English has words like "nil".

Now, you know how to say practically any negative or positive number, including zero in Sino-Korean. That's impressive!

**Temperature**

I promised earlier that we'd talk about temperature, so here we are.

도 means "degrees," so 25 degrees would be 이십오도. What if you wanted to say 25 degrees Celsius? That would be 섭씨이십오도. 섭씨 means Celsius. But Koreans usually just say 이십오도 without 섭씨 because we use a metric system just like the rest of the world.

What if you wanted to say 77 degrees Fahrenheit? That would be 화씨칠십칠도, because 화씨 means Fahrenheit.

Celsius and Fahrenheit go in front of the number. What about negative temperatures? Then you'd have to put 영하 at the front. 영 is zero, which we learned earlier, and we don't use 공 for temperature. Moreover, 하 means "below," so 영하 means "below zero." So negative 40 degrees would be 영하사십도.

If you wanted to emphasize that the temperature was above zero for some reason, you say 영상, which means "above zero." So positive 40 degrees would be 영상사십도.

**Currency**

Currency also uses nominal numbers. I think you realize by now that a lot of aspects in Korean use nominal numbers, probably more than cardinal numbers.

백만원 would be 1,000,000 won, which sounds like a lot of money, but with today's exchange rates, it's not even a thousand US dollars.

**Math**

Now let's get into more detail - more miscellaneous things such as how these numbers are used in other specific situations in everyday life. Let's take math, for example.

Koreans use nominal numbers for math like addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, equations - you get the point.

Let’s talk about fractions. In English, you say and write the numerator first and then the denominator, so you'd read this fraction as "one over two." However, in Korean, we say and write the denominator first and then the numerator. So the same fraction is read as 이분의일. The word 분의 means "it's a fraction."

If I had to do a literal direct translation, it'd be something like "out of two parts, one." Similarly, for 4 over 5, it would be read as 오분의사. You could directly translate this to "out of five parts, four."

Moving on to decimals, they're really similar to English. If pi in English was 3.141592, in Korean it would be 삼점일사일오구이. 점 means "point," and you just read out the numbers afterwards, just like you would in English.

Zero is a bit weird when it comes to decimals though. If it comes before the decimal point, we usually say 영, and if it comes after the decimal point, we usually say 공. For example, 0.1203 would be commonly pronounced 영점일이공삼.

A little tangent, but I like saying numbers in Korean. I found saying numbers in Korean to be quicker because the nominal numbers from one to nine are conveniently all one syllable. So, talking fast in Korean numbers is easier for me because Korean as a language sounds clearer and sharper compared to English.

And if you can do it fast enough, you get to do things like this: 일이삼사오육칠팔구십 십일십이십삼십사십오십육십칠십팔십구이십.

Whereas in English, it doesn't sound as good or fast: "One two three four five six seven eight nine ten eleven twelve thirteen fourteen fifteen sixteen seventeen eighteen nineteen twenty."

Yes, but since not all numbers aren't one syllable there, it's not as quick. Personally, when I count quickly, I count in Sino-Korean numbers because it’s just much better and faster.

Let's talk more about math. For roots, you just say 루트 and then say the number. So the square root of two would be 루트이.

For exponents, you would use the word 제곱, which means "power." So "10 to the power of three" would be 십의삼제곱, with 십 being ten and 삼 being three. And “의” is a possessive particle which would be equivalent of “apostrophe S” or “of” in English.

Or you might alternatively hear people say 십의세제곱, where 세 is the cardinal number. But like I said, we’ll cover the cardinal numbers in the next lesson.

There is another way to use nominal numbers for the exponents, we can use a different Korean word for power, which is 승. So "10 to the power of 3" could also be 십의삼승.

Speaking of 제곱, numbers to the power of two or square numbers are treated differently. For numbers like 10 squared, you would say 십제곱, not 십의이제곱.

It's more concise, just like how in English people usually say "10 squared" instead of "10 to the power of two."

Now, we’ve pretty much covered everything you need to know about the nominal numbers and its usage in real life.

And in our next lesson, we’re going to learn the other one – Cardinal numbers or Native Korean numbers. See you in the next lesson.

### Sino-Korean Numbers: Logic and Large Values
Sino-Korean numbers are based on Chinese characters and are used for mathematical or "general" numbering. The instructor lists the first ten digits: `일 (1)`, `이 (2)`, `삼 (3)`, `사 (4)`, `오 (5)`, `육 (6)`, `칠 (7)`, `팔 (8)`, `구 (9)`, and `십 (10)`. Building larger numbers is a matter of simple multiplication and addition based on place value. For example, to say 12, you say `십이` (10 + 2). To say 56, you identify five tens and six ones: `오십육` (5 x 10 + 6).

As numbers grow, new units are introduced. The instructor explains that `백` is 100, `천` is 1,000, and `만` is 10,000. A crucial difference between Korean and English is that Korean counts in units of 10,000 (`만`) rather than 1,000. Therefore, 100,000 is expressed as "ten ten-thousands" (`십만`), and 1,000,000 is "one hundred ten-thousands" (`백만`). The instructor demonstrates this by constructing the largest possible number using these units: 99,999,999, which is `구천구백구십구만 구천구백구십구`. He notes that once you hit 100,000,000, a new unit called `억` is used, though it is beyond the scope of this basic lesson.

The foundation of the Sino-Korean system is the set of numbers from one to ten. These are presented on screen in two columns:
- **일 (1)**
- **이 (2)**
- **삼 (3)**
- **사 (4)**

### Constructing Numbers 11 to 99
To create numbers between 11 and 99, the system uses a simple additive and multiplicative logic. For numbers 11 through 19, you simply say the word for ten followed by the single digit. For example, eleven is **십일 (10 + 1)** and twelve is **십이 (10 + 2)**. The instructor highlights a specific rule: you do not say "one-ten-one" for eleven; it is simply **십일**, not **일십일**.

For multiples of ten, such as fifty, you place the multiplier before the word for ten. Thus, fifty is **오십 (5 x 10)**. To form a complex number like fifty-nine, you combine these steps: **오십** (fifty) plus **구** (nine) results in **오십구**.

### Moving into Hundreds, Thousands, and Beyond
As numbers grow larger, new counters are introduced for each decimal place. The counter for one hundred is **백**, for one thousand is **천**, and for ten thousand is **만**.
- **백 (100)**: 120 is **백이십**, 404 is **사백사** (skipping the empty tens place).
- **천 (1,000)**: 1,234 is **천 이백 삼십 사**.
- **만 (10,000)**: 10,400 is **만 사백**.

The instructor explains that Korean numerical logic differs from English at the 10,000 mark. While English continues to count in thousands (e.g., one hundred thousand), Korean shifts to using **만** as the base. Therefore, 100,000 is expressed as **십만 (10 x 10,000)**, one million is **백만 (100 x 10,000)**, and ten million is **천만 (1,000 x 10,000)**. For even larger figures, the counter **억** is used for 100,000,000.

#### 큰 숫자 개요

- 원문 줄: 16~25

````md
Large Sino-Korean numbers are confusing. For English speakers, most of this confusion stems from the fact that “10,000” has its own word in Korean. Let me explain.

There is a word for “1” in Korean and English. It is “일” and “one” respectively.
There is a word for “10” in Korean and English. It is “십” and “ten” respectively.
There is a word for “100” in Korean and English. It “백” and “one hundred” respectively.
There is a word for “1,000” in Korean and English. It is “천” and “one thousand” respectively.

There is a word for “10,000” in Korean (“만”), but there is not a separate word for this in English. We just use the previous denomination “thousand” with “ten” to say “ten thousand.”

### Sino-Korean Numbers (Nominal System)
The first system taught is the Sino-Korean system, often referred to as the nominal system. These numbers are derived from Chinese characters (Hanja), which is why they sound similar to Mandarin. The instructor lists the first ten: 1 is `일`, 2 is `이`, 3 is `삼`, 4 is `사`, 5 is `오`, 6 is `육`, 7 is `칠`, 8 is `팔`, 9 is `구`, and 10 is `십`. A pronunciation note is provided for 6 (`육`): in certain linguistic contexts or when counting quickly, it may sound like `륙` with an "R" sound, though this is best learned through conversational practice.

The strength of this system is its mathematical logic. To create any number from 11 to 99, you simply combine these ten words. For example, 11 is 10 + 1, or `십일`. 12 is 10 + 2, or `십이`. To say 20, you say "two tens" (`이십`). Therefore, 22 is "two tens plus two" (`이십이`). 99 is "nine tens plus nine" (`구십구`). This structure makes math more intuitive in Korean than in English, where words like "eleven" or "twenty" must be memorized as unique entities.
````

### Large Numbers and the Four-Digit Chunking Rule
As numbers get larger, the Sino-Korean system continues its logical progression. 100 is `백`, 1,000 is `천`, and 10,000 is `만`. The instructor highlights a major difference between Korean and English: while English groups numbers by three digits (thousands, millions, billions), Korean groups them by four digits. After `천` (1,000), the next major unit is `만` (10,000). Then comes `십만` (100,000), `백만` (1,000,000), and `천만` (10,000,000). The next unique word is `억` for 100,000,000.

This difference often leads to translation errors on social media platforms like Instagram, where follower counts might be literally translated into awkward Korean phrasing. For extremely large numbers, the sequence follows: `조` (trillion), `경` (ten quadrillion), `해` (one hundred quintillion), and `자` (septillion). The instructor demonstrates that the largest number one could theoretically say in Korean is `구천구백구십구자 구천구백구십구해 구천구백구십구경 구천구백구십구조 구천구백구십구억 구천구백구십구만 구천구백구십구`.

### Zero and Negatives
Negative numbers are expressed by placing the word `마이너스` (minus) before the number, such as `마이너스 오` (-5). For the number zero, Korean has two words: `영` and `공`. `공` is most common for nominal uses like phone numbers (e.g., 010 is `공일공`) or zip codes. `영` is used in math, temperature, or sports scores (zero points). The instructor shares a "genius wordplay" example from a Big Bang song where the word `공` is used to mean both "zero" and "ball," implying a bank account has so many zeros that it looks like a pool table full of balls. Additionally, the informal word `빵` can be used for "zero points," similar to "zilch" in English.

### Temperature and Currency
Temperature uses the word `도` (degrees) with Sino-Korean numbers. Celsius is `섭씨` and Fahrenheit is `화씨`, placed before the number (e.g., `섭씨 이십오도`). Negative temperatures use the word `영하` (below zero), while positive temperatures can be emphasized with `영상` (above zero). Currency also exclusively uses the Sino-Korean system followed by `원` (won), such as `백만원` (one million won).
