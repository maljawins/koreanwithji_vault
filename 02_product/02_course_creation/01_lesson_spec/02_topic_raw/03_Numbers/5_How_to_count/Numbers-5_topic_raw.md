````md
**Intro**

In this lesson, we’re going to learn how to count in Korean.

If you remember from our last lessons, we use cardinal numbers or native Korean numbers for counting things.

And I also mentioned that there’s a concept called “counter words” in Korean similar to how you’d say “Two sheets of paper” or “Three cups of coffee” in English.

**The rule**

The rule for this is very simple.

You put the counter word after the Native Korean number—just like how you tell time in Korean.

And the same exception applies to number one through four.

하나 becomes 한

둘 becomes 두

셋 becomes 세

넷 becomes 네

And from numbers five to nine, it stays the same. You’re already familiar with the rule.

**Counter words**

There’s a LOT of counter words in Korean. But only a handful are commonly used in daily life.

So, we’re going to go over some of the common ones and the correct order of saying things.

Now, the most common one is 개. And it’s used for all non-living things.

On a side note: In Korean, we almost always differentiate between non-living things and people. To do otherwise could sound really rude.

For example, in English, it's common to say something like "This is Lisa," where "this" refers to a person. But in Korean, you would have to say “This PERSON is Lisa”. Or “This FRIEND is Lisa.”

Why? Because to say just "this" would mean "this thing" or "this object." Just a little something to remember.

**Word order**

Now, let’s learn how to say “Three apples” as an example.

We have a particular order of saying it.

**1.** First, you put whatever you're counting **2.** Then comes the number **3.** Finally, the counter word

So three apples in Korean would be 사과 세 개. This order applies to everything.

So, all there’s left for us to do is just learning different words for counting different things.

**명 / 분 - People**

For people, we say 명.

And we also have an honorific version: 분.

For example, when you walk into a restaurant in Korea, you'll be greeted with "몇 분이세요?"

몇 means “how many”. We’re going to talk about this in detail in the lesson “Questions” in level 2.

And 분 is the honorific counter word for people.

So, this means “How many people are you?”

**What Is Honorific?**

If you don't know what "honorific" means: It's a language system used to show respect and politeness to the person you're speaking with or a third party being mentioned.

So, in this case they’re addressing you the customer as 분 to show you respect.

And if there is three of you, you would answer "세 명이에요," meaning "We are three people"—or in natural translation, "There's three of us."

Notice how you'd use 명, not 분, to respond—using 분 would sound like you're elevating yourself.

We’re going to talk about politeness levels in detail in level 1. So for now, just know that 명 is standard and 분 is more respectful.

**마리 - Animals**

Moving on: We have 마리 for animals.

On a sidenote, there’s a part in Squid Game where 남규 asks 민수 how many people survived after a game. [Insert clip] He says “몇 마리나 되는 거 같아?”

Here, he deliberately uses “마리” to refer to people which is a super disrespectful thing to do.

But the subtitle just says “how many do you think survived?” for which I can’t really blame the translators because this nuance is just untranslatable as the concept of the “counter word” simply doesn’t exist in English.

So, twenty eight cats in Korean would be 고양이 스물 여덟 마리.

**Inanimate Objects**

Moving on to inanimate objects. Well, didn’t you say we use 개 for all non-living things?

Yes, you CAN use 개 for counting all these things and it wouldn’t be “wrong” but it’d just sound more natural to use the designated counter word.

**권 - Books**

For books, it's 권. So 31 books in Korean would be: 책 서른한 권

**장 - Sheets/Pages**

But when it comes to sheets of paper—like pages—we use 장. So 52 pages would be: 쉰두 장

**대 - Machinery/Equipment**

We also have 대, which is for machinery or pieces of equipment—like computers, cars, washing machines, etc. So four air conditioners in Korean would be: 에어컨 네 대. Yes, 에어컨 is a loanword for "air conditioner" in Korean.

**잔 - Cups/Glasses**

For cups or glasses, it's 잔. So a glass of beer would be: 맥주 한 잔

By the way, "한 잔해" is a super common phrase which roughly translates to "Let's grab a drink" or "Drink up." [Insert song – 한잔해 by 박현빈]

**병 - Bottles**

Since we're talking about drinks, what about bottles? So four bottles of soju would be 소주 네 병.

**채 - Houses/Buildings**

For houses or buildings, we say 채. So 집 한 채 would be one house.

**그릇 - Bowls**

For bowls of food—such as soup or rice—it's 그릇. 밥 두 그릇 would be two bowls of rice.

On a side note: 밥 means “cooked rice” but Koreans use it like “a meal” And we have a separate word for raw rice: 쌀.

**벌 - Clothing**

For counting pieces of clothing, it's 벌.

So 쟈켓 한 벌 is, as you might have guessed, one jacket.

**켤레 - Pairs of Shoes**

But when it comes to shoes—like pairs of shoes—we say 켤레.

So five pairs of shoes would be: 신발 다섯 켤레

**More Counter Words**

Like I said, there are a LOT of counter words in Korean, so it'd take forever to go over each one.

I'll leave a list of counter words in this lesson so you can download it, print it out, and study them in your own time.

**Practical Usage: Time and Place**

So let's move on to more practical usage—like time and place.

**군데 - Specific Parts of a Location**

For counting places, it's 군데. This is for specific parts of a location.

So three parts would be: 세 군데

**곳 - Locations (General)**

You can alternatively use 곳. But this is for a broader scale of places—like a place in a general sense.

So 두 곳 would be two locations.

**시간 - Hours**

For counting hours—like "two hours" or "three hours"—we use 시간.

Eight hours would be: 여덟 시간

**분 / 초 - Minutes and Seconds (Sino-Korean)**

But when it comes to minutes and seconds, we use Sino-Korean numbers with 분 and 초. But wait—wasn't 분 for counting people respectfully?

Yes, but for counting minutes, we use Sino-Korean numbers.

삼십분 would be 30 minutes.

But 서른분 would be 30 people, honorifically.

**Months: 달 vs. 개월**

Let's move on to bigger scales of time.

For months, we have two ways to count:

**달 - Native Korean**

First, we have 달. With 달, you use Native Korean numbers.

For example, three months using 달 would be: 세달

**개월 - Sino-Korean**

The other version is 개월, where you use Sino-Korean numbers.

So three months in 개월 would be: 삼개월

**Years: 해 vs. 년**

Let’s talk about years. We also have two ways to count years: 해 and 년. And just like with months, we use Native Korean numbers for 해 and Sino-Korean for 년.

**The 해 Limitation**

But note that 년 is much more commonly used than 해.

With 해, for some reason, we only use it to count up to two years—no more than that.

One year would be 한 해, and two years would be 두 해.

But 세 해, meaning three years, sounds a bit off.

So normally people just use 년 with Sino-Korean numbers so three years 년 would be 삼년.

**Wrap-Up**

So that's it for this lesson.

Make sure you download the list of counter words.

In our next lesson, we're finally going to learn about age.

See you there
````

````md
### Important Exceptions Before Counters
A critical grammatical rule is introduced regarding five specific numbers: 하나 (1), 둘 (2), 셋 (3), 넷 (4), and 스물 (20). When these numbers are placed directly before a counter or a noun, their forms change to act as modifiers. 하나 becomes 한, 둘 becomes 두, 셋 becomes 세, 넷 becomes 네, and 스물 becomes 스무. 

The instructor demonstrates this with the age counter:
- **한 살** / 1 year old (not 하나 살)
- **두 살** / 2 years old (not 둘 살)
- **세 살** / 3 years old (not 셋 살)
- **네 살** / 4 years old (not 넷 살)
- **스무 살** / 20 years old (not 스물 살)

It is emphasized that this change only occurs when the number is *directly* before the counter. For a number like 25, the 스물 remains unchanged because it is followed by 다섯, resulting in 스물다섯 살.
````

````md
### Basic Counters: People, Animals, and General Objects
The counter for people is 명, which is used exclusively with pure Korean numbers. It is important to remember that 명 is a counter and not a standalone noun for "person." For example, to say "one person came to my house," you use 우리 집에 한 명이 왔어요. When counting animals, the counter 마리 is used, also with pure Korean numbers. The sentence structure typically involves stating the animal first, followed by the number and the counter, such as 강아지 두 마리 (two dogs).

For general objects that do not have a specific specialized counter, the counter 개 is used with pure Korean numbers. This is the most versatile counter in the language. If a speaker forgets a specific counter for a certain item, using 개 is generally understood and acceptable. Examples include 핸드폰 다섯 개 (five mobile phones) or 의자 스무 개 (twenty chairs).

---

### Specialized Common Counters
The instructor provides a list of specialized counters for specific categories of items, all of which use pure Korean numbers:
- **장** is used for flat items like paper or tickets: 종이 열다섯 장 (fifteen sheets of paper).
- **권** is used for bound items like books: 책 한 권 (one book).
- **병** is used for bottles: 사이다 두 병 (two bottles of soda).
- **자루** is used for long, thin items like pencils or knives: 연필 두 자루 (two pencils).
- **대** is used for machines and vehicles: 컴퓨터 세 대 (three computers).
````

````md
### Asking "How Many" and Additional Notes
To ask "how many" of something, the word 몇 is used immediately before a counter. Common questions include 몇 개? (how many objects?), 몇 명? (how many people?), or 몇 살? (how many years old?). While 몇 살 is polite, the instructor notes there are even more formal ways to ask age that will be covered in future lessons.

Regarding sentence structure, counters can be placed either directly after the noun or after the noun + particle. Both 햄버거 두 개를 먹었어요 and 햄버거를 두 개 먹었어요 are correct. Additionally, for the quantity of "one," it is common to simply use the number 하나 instead of the full number-counter construction like 한 개. Finally, some regular nouns function as their own counters, such as 컵 (cup) and 그릇 (bowl).
````

````md
### The Sino-Korean System: 일 vs 날

The most common way to count days is by using the counter **일 (day)**. The instructor clarifies that **일** is strictly a counter and cannot stand alone as a noun meaning "day" in a general sense. For example, if you want to say "I want to meet you another day," you must use the word **날 (day)** instead of **일**. To count days using this system, you simply place a Sino-Korean number before the counter. Because the Sino-Korean number for "one" is also **일**, the term for "one day" becomes **일일 (one day)**. This continues logically with **이일 (two days)** and **삼일 (three days)**.

A key feature of the Sino-Korean system is its dual purpose. These terms can represent both a duration of time and a specific date on the calendar. Therefore, **일일** can mean "one day" or "the 1st of the month." Similarly, **십일** can mean "ten days" or "the 10th," and **삼십일** can mean "thirty days" or "the 30th."
````

#### 하루·이틀·사흘·나흘

- 원문 줄: 31~40

````md
### The Pure Korean System: Essential Terms

The second system for counting days uses Pure Korean vocabulary. Unlike the Sino-Korean system, these terms are used exclusively for counting the number of days (duration) and never for calendar dates. The instructor identifies the first four terms as extremely common and essential for learners to memorize:

- **하루** / one day
- **이틀** / two days
- **사흘** / three days
- **나흘** / four days

The instructor highlights a frequent point of confusion for learners regarding **사흘 (three days)**. Because it starts with the sound "sa," many mistakenly assume it refers to four days (since **사** is the Sino-Korean number for four). However, **사흘** is three days, and **나흘** is four days. These terms are interchangeable with their Sino-Korean counterparts (**일일/하루**, **이일/이틀**, **삼일/사흘**, **사일/나흘**) when counting duration, but you must never use **사흘** to mean "the 3rd of the month."
````

````md
### Standard Systems for Counting Months

The first and most common way to count months is by using Sino-Korean numbers paired with the counter 개월. For example, one month is 일개월, two months is 이개월, and three months is 삼개월. This system is often perceived as slightly more formal or official, making it the preferred choice for written documents and professional contexts.

The second standard system uses Pure Korean numbers paired with the counter 달. In this system, one month is 한 달, two months is 두 달, three months is 세 달, and four months is 네 달. Both systems are interchangeable in meaning, and learners can choose either for daily conversation.

---

### The Third Option: 석 and 넉

When counting specifically three or four months using the counter 달, a third option exists. Instead of the standard 세 or 네, speakers can use the traditional forms 석 and 넉. This results in 석 달 (three months) and 넉 달 (four months). The instructor notes that while these forms are perfectly natural and carry no difference in formality compared to 세 달 or 네 달, they are becoming less common among younger generations who favor the standard Pure Korean numbers.

It is important to note that 석 and 넉 are not universal replacements for "three" and "four." They only function with a specific set of counters. For instance, they cannot be used with the general object counter 개; saying 석 개 or 넉 개 is incorrect.
````

````md
### Eliminating Redundant Nouns

The most significant tip for sounding natural is knowing when to drop the noun entirely. Many Korean counters are specific enough that the noun becomes redundant. The instructor highlights the counter **명 (people)** as the prime example. Because **명** is only used for humans, including the word **사람 (person/people)** is often unnecessary.

> **다섯 명을 만났어요.** / I met five people.

While **사람 다섯 명을 만났어요** is grammatically correct, it sounds repetitive because the counter already tells the listener you are talking about people.

This principle extends to other counters based on context. For instance, **마리** is a general animal counter. If you are looking at a group of monkeys and someone asks how many there are, you should simply say **세 마리가 있어요** (There are three) rather than repeating the word for monkey. Similarly, **권** is the specific counter for books. If the context of a bookstore is established, saying **다섯 권을 샀어요** is more natural than repeating **책 (book)**.
````

#### 명사 유지

- 원문 줄: 59~65

````md
### When to Keep the Noun

The instructor clarifies that nouns should be kept when they provide necessary specificity that the counter alone cannot provide. Since **마리** can refer to any animal, you need the word **원숭이 (monkey)** if you need to specify which animal you are counting out of a group of different animals. Likewise, if you want to specify a sub-type of an object, such as **역사책 (history book)** instead of just a general book, you would include it:

> **역사책 다섯 권을 샀어요.** / I bought five history books.

However, even in these cases, if the specific noun was just mentioned in the previous sentence or question, it should be dropped in the response to avoid sounding like a "repetitive robot." The ultimate goal is to use the counter as the primary carrier of information whenever the context allows.
````

````md
### Counting People and the Rule of Five
To count people, the counter **명** is used in conjunction with Pure Korean numbers. A critical grammatical rule is introduced: the Pure Korean numbers for 1, 2, 3, 4, and 20 change their form when followed by any counter. Specifically, **하나 (one)** becomes **한**, **둘 (two)** becomes **두**, **셋 (three)** becomes **세**, **넷 (four)** becomes **네**, and **스물 (twenty)** becomes **스무**. For example, one person is **한 명**, not **하나 명**. This change for 20 only occurs when the number is exactly 20; for 21, the number reverts to the standard form for the tens place while the ones place follows the change rule, resulting in **스물한 명**.
````

#### 동물·물건·나이 예문

- 원문 줄: 31~34

````md
### Animals, Things, and Age
Animals of all types are counted using Pure Korean numbers and the counter **마리**. When the context is clear, such as responding to a question about how many cows are present, one can simply say the number and the counter, like **세 마리 (three [animals])**. If the noun is included, it typically comes first: **소 세 마리 (three cows)**.

For general objects, especially those that do not have a specific counter or when the specific counter is unknown, the generic counter **개** is used with Pure Korean numbers. An example provided is **의자 다섯 개 (five chairs)**. To count a person's age, the counter **살** is used with Pure Korean numbers. For instance, "I am 31 years old" is translated as **저는 서른 한 살이에요**.
````

````md
### Measuring Units and Specific Categories
Counters used for measuring—such as distance, volume, or temperature—typically utilize the Sino-Korean number system. Examples shown on screen include **오 미터 (five meters)**, **팔 리터 (eight liters)**, and **이십 도 (twenty degrees)**. The instructor notes that Korea uses the Celsius scale for temperature.

Specific counters are used for certain shapes or types of items. The counter **장** is used for flat items like paper or even T-shirts: **종이 다섯 장 (five sheets of paper)** or **셔츠 천 장 (one thousand shirts)**. For books, the counter **권** is employed with Pure Korean numbers, such as **책 한 권 (one book)** or **책 스물 네 권 (twenty-four books)**.
````

- 원문 줄: 23~25

````md
### Basic Counter Words and Number Rules

The three most fundamental counter words are 개 (for non-living things), 명 (for people), and 마리 (for animals). When counting items, the standard sentence structure is Noun + Number + Counter. For example, "five pens" is expressed as 펜 다섯 개 (pen five [object counter]). The instructor notes that while you can include the noun, it is often omitted if the context is clear, such as saying 다섯 개 (five [objects]) or 여섯 명 (six [people]).
````

#### 자주 쓰는 단위명사

- 원문 줄: 41~48

````md
### Specific Counters for Objects

While 개 is the most general counter for inanimate objects, native speakers use more specific counters to sound natural. The instructor highlights several:
- 잔: for cups or glasses (커피 한 잔 / a cup of coffee)
- 권: for books (책 세 권 / three books)
- 대: for machines or vehicles (컴퓨터 한 대 / one computer)

The instructor mentions that while using 개 for these items is often understood and acceptable for beginners, learning the specific counters is recommended for reaching a native-like level.
````

#### 몇 질문

- 원문 줄: 52~60

````md
### Asking Questions with 몇

To ask "how many," the word 몇 is placed immediately before the counter word. This can be paired with the 이다 (to be) verb or the 있다 (to exist/have) verb.

Common question forms include:
- **사과가 몇 개예요?** / How many apples are there?
- **아이들이 몇 명이에요?** / How many children are there?
- **가족이 몇 명 있어요?** / How many family members do you have?
- **애완동물 몇 마리 키워요?** / How many pets do you raise?
````

#### 기간 세기

- 원문 줄: 80~87

````md
### Duration of Time

When expressing how long something takes or how long one has stayed somewhere, the counters change again:
- 시간: hours (Pure Korean numbers). 몇 시간 걸려요? (How many hours does it take?)
- 달 / 개월: months. 달 uses Pure Korean numbers (한 달), while 개월 uses Sino-Korean numbers (일 개월).
- 년: years (Sino-Korean numbers). 한국에 몇 년 있었어요? (How many years were you in Korea?)

The instructor introduces the particle 동안 (for/during) to specify the length of time, such as 열 달 동안 (for ten months) or 일 년 동안 (for one year).
````

#### 개·명·번

- 원문 줄: 173~179

````md
When counting anything in Korean, you need to use the pure Korean numbers. In addition, one thing that is very hard for English speakers to wrap their head around is that, when counting most things in Korean, you need to also include a ‘counter.’ The most common counters are:

개 = counter for things
명 = counter for people
번 = counter for behaviors/actions

There are many more counters, but if you can’t remember the specific counter of something, you can usually substitute “개” (the counter for “thing”) instead. You will learn the more difficult counters as you progress through future lessons. For now, the goal is to get you accustomed to using these three simple counters.
````

#### 단위명사 띄어쓰기

- 원문 줄: 215~220

````md
When writing out the word instead of using the numeral (for example, writing “한” instead of “1”) the correct form is to have a space between the written number and the counter. For example:

한 개 instead of 한개
두 번 instead of 두번
세 명 instead of 세명

````

#### 본문 · 번째·째 서수

- 원문 줄: 354~390

````md
번째: First, Second, Third, etc…

번째 can be used after a number like a counter to mean first/second/third/etc. When saying “first”, “첫” replaces “한”.

For example:

첫 번째 = first
Play[저의 첫 번째 친구는 착했어요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Lesson10-33.mp3) = My first friend was nice
Play[저는 첫 번째 차를 싫어했어요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/02/Lesson10-Feb-10-10.mp3) = I didn’t like that first car
*If you think that is confusing, explain how ‘one’ gets changed to ‘first,’ ‘two’ gets changed to ‘second’ and ‘three’ gets changed to ‘third.’*

After “first,” you can use the regular Korean numbers. Just like with counters, the numbers 2 – 4 change when 번째 follows. For example:

두 번째 = second
Play[그 두 번째 선생님은 똑똑했어요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Lesson10-35.mp3) = That second teacher was smart
Play[저는 두 번째 남자를 골랐어요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/02/Lesson10-Feb-10-11.mp3) = I chose the second man

세 번째 = third
Play[이 여권은 저의 세 번째 여권이에요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/02/Lesson10-Feb-10-12.mp3) = This passport is my third passport
Play[저는 세 번째 문을 열었어요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/02/Lesson10-Feb-10-13.mp3) = I opened the third door

네 번째 = fourth
Play[저는 네 번째 사람이었어요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/02/Lesson10-Feb-10-14.mp3) = I was the fourth person
Play[이 아이는 저의 네 번째 아들이에요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/02/Lesson10-Feb-10-15.mp3) = This person is my fourth son

After the number four, the words don’t change when adding 번째. For example:

Play[이 수업은 오늘 저의 열 번째 수업입니다](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/02/Lesson10-Feb-10-16.mp3) = This is my tenth class today
Play[저는 백 번째 페이지를 읽었어요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/02/Lesson10-Feb-10-17.mp3) = I read the 100th page

Sometimes you might see these numbers + counters used in the following way:

첫 번째 = 첫째 = first
두 번째 = 둘째 = second
세 번째 = 셋째 = third
네 번째 = 넷째 = fourth
etc.…
````
