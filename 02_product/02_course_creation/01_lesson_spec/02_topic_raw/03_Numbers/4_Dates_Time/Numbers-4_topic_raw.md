**Dates**

In this lesson, I'll be covering dates and time.

Korea uses the year-month-day format for dates.

So a date like September 17th, 2025 would be written as 2025-09-17.

By the way, if you were to write this in the month-day-year format like the U.S. does, it would be 09-17-2025.

In my opinion, the year-month-day format is the most logical because it follows the same descending order we use for time—like hours, minutes, and seconds.

**Years**

Moving on: 년 means "year," so 2025 would be 이천이십오년.

You just add 년 after the number to make it the year.

**Months**

월 means "month," and you would also put this after the number to say a certain month.

Unlike English, where every month has a special name, Korean just uses 월 after each number. For example, January with one, February with two, and so on.

월 means "month," and you would also put this behind the numbers to say a certain month. Unlike English, where every month has a special name, Korea just associates each month with a number: January with one, February with two, and so on.

So January is 일월

February is 이월

March is 삼월

April is 사월

May is 오월

June is 유월. I know six is 육, but Koreans don't say 육월 - it doesn't sound natural, so instead we say 유월.

July is 칠월

August is 팔월

September is 구월

October is 시월. Similar to June, there's a slightly different pronunciation. Ten is 십, but we say 시월, not 십월.

November is 십일월

and December is 십이월.

Let's move on to the days. 일 means "day," and you would also put this behind the number to say a certain day of the month.

As you've probably noticed, it sounds exactly the same as the Korean word for "one," which is also 일.

Because of this, even Koreans get confused sometimes.

For example, if someone said 이십일, it could be interpreted two ways:

**1.** The 20th—because 이십 means 20 and 일 means "day"

**2.** The 21st—because 이십일 means 21, and the speaker just omitted the word "day"

If you ask for clarification, they might make it super obvious by replying 그냥 이십일, with 그냥 meaning "just." So it would refer to "JUST the 20th."

Or they might say 이십일일 with that extra 일 at the end, which would refer to the 21st.

So let's put this all together. September 17th, 2025 would be 이천이십오년 구월 십칠일.

January 21st, 2024 would be 이천이십사년 일월 이십일일.

Hopefully you get the idea and it's not that confusing - they're just nominal numbers.

**Time**

The real confusion might set in when we're talking about time. 시 means "hour," so if you put this behind the number, it means "o'clock." However, the number we use for the hour has to be cardinal numbers, not nominal numbers.

For example, if I want to say one o'clock, it would be 한시, not 일시. Actually, do you remember the whole measure words exception from the last lesson?

I've mentioned that putting unit words or measure words behind a cardinal number slightly alters how you pronounce them. Here, you can assume 시 is a counter word, so it follows the same rule.

* One o'clock is 한시
* Two o'clock is 두시
* Three o'clock is 세시
* Four o'clock is 네시
* Five o'clock is 다섯시
* Six o'clock is 여섯시
* Seven o'clock is 일곱시
* Eight o'clock is 여덟시
* Nine o'clock is 아홉시
* Ten o'clock is 열시
* Eleven o'clock is 열한시
* Twelve o'clock is 열두시

If you use the 24-hour clock and had to go beyond 12 o'clock, from the 13th hour, normally you'd start using nominal numbers. So 13 o'clock would typically be 십삼시, not 열세시. 14 o'clock would be 십사시, and this pattern continues all the way to 이십사시.

By the way, the military has their own way of counting time where they just pronounce each digit in nominal numbers.

For example, one o'clock would be 공일시. But this is probably irrelevant to you, so let's just skip it.

Moving on to minutes and seconds? 분 is minutes, 초 is seconds. These use purely nominal numbers, so the 30th minute would be 삼십분, and the 50th second would be 오십초.

As a result, a time like 11:58:38 would be 열한시오십팔분삼십팔초.

It might sound weird to you that Koreans use cardinal numbers only for the hour and nominal numbers for the rest. You're right, it's a bit confusing. I suggest you think of it this way: 한시, which is one o'clock, could literally be translated to "the first hour." 두시 would be "the second hour," and so on. It's just a small exception to our rule of time.

**Conclusion**

Great! You can now read basically any number in Korean, whether it's related to dates, times, temperatures, math - you name it.

I went into detail in this lesson, covering some exceptions in certain situations. I hope that wasn't too confusing to follow.

While the two Korean number systems might seem intimidating at first and feel more overwhelming compared to English numbers, with a bit of practice and memorization, you'll become great at reading numbers in Korean in no time.

In the next lesson, I’ll be talking about the counter words.

I’ll see you there!

### Counting Hours: 시 vs 시간
The instructor defines a counter as a noun used specifically for counting items. For telling time and counting hours, Pure Korean numbers (하나, 둘, 셋, etc.) are used. However, when a Pure Korean number acts as an adjective before a counter, the first four numbers and the number twenty undergo a transformation: 하나 becomes 한, 둘 becomes 두, 셋 becomes 세, 넷 becomes 네, and 스물 becomes 스무.

The counter **시** is used specifically for "o'clock" to indicate a specific point in time. For example, "three o'clock" is expressed as 세 시. The instructor provides a full drill from one to twelve o'clock and demonstrates the usage in a sentence:
> **일곱시에 일어났어요.** / I woke up at seven o'clock.

In contrast, the counter **시간** is used for counting a duration of hours. While it also uses Pure Korean numbers, it refers to "how many hours" rather than "what time." For example, 두 시간 means "two hours." The instructor illustrates this with a request:
> **두 시간 뒤에 오세요.** / Please come after two hours.

---

### Minutes and Seconds: 분 and 초
Unlike hours, minutes and seconds utilize the Sino-Korean number system (일, 이, 삼, etc.). The counter for minutes is **분** and the counter for seconds is **초**. These are attached directly to the Sino-Korean number. The instructor provides an example for waiting a brief duration:
> **십초만 기다려주세요.** / Please wait just ten seconds.

### Years and Days: 년 and 일
Both years and days use Sino-Korean numbers. The counter **년** is used for both counting a number of years and naming a specific calendar year. For instance, 1999년 can mean "1,999 years" or "the year 1999." The instructor demonstrates counting years in a future-tense sentence:
> **오 년 뒤에 세계여행을 할 거예요.** / I will travel around the world in five years.

The counter **일** is used for counting days and naming the day of the month. For example, 삼일 can mean "three days" or "the third day of the month." An example of duration is shown:
> **이십오일 기다렸어요.** / I waited twenty-five days.

### Telling the Time: Hours, Minutes, and Seconds
To tell the time in Korean, you must combine the correct number system with the appropriate counter. For the hour, the counter is `시 (o'clock)`, and it must be paired with Pure Korean numbers. For example, three o'clock is expressed as `세 시`. When asking for the time, the question word `몇 (how many)` is used with the counter to form `몇 시예요? (What time is it?)`. It is important to distinguish between telling the time on a clock and counting a duration of hours. To count a duration (e.g., "two hours"), the noun `시간 (hour/time)` is used with Pure Korean numbers, such as `두 시간 (two hours)`.

Minutes and seconds utilize the Sino-Korean number system. The counter for minutes is `분`, and the counter for seconds is `초`. Therefore, a time like 2:35 is read as `두 시 삼십오 분`. The instructor notes that seconds are optional in daily conversation but follow the same Sino-Korean pattern.

---

### Specifying the Time of Day
Korean uses several words to specify which part of the day a time refers to. These words are placed before the time. `오전` means "before noon" (AM), and `오후` means "afternoon" (PM). Additionally, more descriptive terms can be used: `아침 (morning)`, `저녁 (evening)`, `밤 (night)`, and `새벽 (dawn/early morning after midnight)`. To express "half past" an hour, the word `반 (half)` can be used instead of saying thirty minutes. An example provided is `오전 열 시 반`, which translates to 10:30 AM.

---

### Years, Months, and Days
Dates in Korean are structured from the largest unit to the smallest: Year, then Month, then Day. All three units use Sino-Korean numbers. For years, the counter is `년`. To say "the year 2021," you say `이천이십일 년`. The instructor provides useful vocabulary for relative years: `올해 (this year)`, `작년 (last year)`, and `내년 (next year)`.

Months are formed by taking the numbers 1 through 12 and adding the counter `월`. There are two critical spelling exceptions to note: June is `유월` (not 육월) and October is `시월` (not 십월). The "k" sound in 6 and the "p" sound in 10 are dropped for easier pronunciation.

Days of the month use the counter `일`. To ask about the date, the word `며칠` is used, which means "what date" or "how many days." This is distinct from asking for the day of the week, which uses `무슨 요일`.

### The Standard Rule and the Exceptions
Naming months in Korean is generally a straightforward process. One takes a Sino-Korean number—such as `일 (one)`, `이 (two)`, or `삼 (three)`—and attaches the suffix `월`, which means "month." Following this logic, January is `일월`, February is `이월`, and December is `십이월`. However, June and October present a unique challenge. A learner might logically expect June (the 6th month) to be `육월` and October (the 10th month) to be `십월`. In reality, these are written and spoken as `유월` and `시월`. The instructor notes that while the quick answer is that these versions sound better and are easier to pronounce, the deeper explanation involves the history of Sino-Korean vocabulary.

### The Logic of 몇 (How Many)

The word 몇 is a versatile determiner meaning "how many" or "some." It is used in numerous standard combinations with counters and time units. The instructor demonstrates this by writing several examples on screen: 삼일 `(three days)`, 십일 `(ten days)`, 몇 달 `(how many months)`, 몇 년 `(how many years)`, and 몇 개 `(how many things)`. Given this pattern, it seems intuitive that "how many days" should be written as 몇일. However, the instructor explicitly marks 몇일 with a red "X" to show it is an invalid form.

---

### Phonological Proof: Why 몇일 Is Impossible

To explain why 며칠 is not just a misspelling of 몇일, the instructor conducts a "thought experiment" regarding pronunciation. If the word were spelled 몇일, it would follow specific Korean sound change rules. Usually, when 몇 precedes a word starting with a vowel, the final ㅊ sound transforms into a ㄷ sound. For example, 몇월 `(which month)` is not pronounced as "myeo-chwol," but as 몇월 `[며둴]`. Similarly, 몇억 `(how many hundreds of millions)` is pronounced as 몇억 `[며덕]`.

Furthermore, when a ㄷ sound meets an 이 or 히 sound, it often undergoes palatalization (like 굳이 being pronounced as 구지). If 몇일 were a real word, it might be expected to sound like "myeo-chil" through simple liaison, but standard phonology for compound words often triggers an ㄴ-insertion. This is seen in words like 깻잎 `[깬닙]` or 앞일 `[암닐]`. If 몇일 followed these rules, it would be pronounced as "myeon-nil." Since the actual spoken word is consistently 며칠, it proves the word did not originate from the combination of 몇 and 일.

---

### The True Origin: Pure Korean 을

The instructor reveals that 며칠 actually stems from an ancient pure Korean word for "day," which was 을. This is distinct from the Sino-Korean 일. Evidence for this root still exists in modern pure Korean day counters such as 사흘 `(three days)` and 나흘 `(four days)`. While Sino-Korean uses 삼일 and 사일, these pure Korean forms preserve the 을 ending.

Historically, the word was 며칠. Over several hundred years, vowel changes occurred in the Korean language, shifting the pronunciation and spelling to the modern 며칠. Because the word is a single unit derived from an ancient root—rather than a modern compound of "how many" + "day"—it is spelled phonetically as 며칠. The instructor concludes that while many modern Koreans mistakenly try to write 몇일 because they recognize the individual components, 며칠 is the only historically and grammatically accurate version.

### The Structure of Korean Dates
In Korean, dates are organized from the largest unit to the smallest: Year, then Month, then Day. This is the reverse of the common English format (Day/Month/Year or Month/Day/Year). Each numerical value is followed by a specific unit marker: **년 (year)**, **월 (month)**, and **일 (day)**.

When reading the year, Koreans do not group the digits into pairs (e.g., "twenty-twenty"). Instead, the full number is read as a single value. For example, the year 2020 is read as **이천이십 (two thousand twenty)** followed by the unit **년**. The instructor illustrates this with several examples, such as 1992 being read as **천구백구십이 (one thousand nine hundred ninety-two)**.

---

### Months of the Year
Korean does not have unique names for months like "January" or "February." Instead, months are simply named by their numerical order followed by the unit **월**. January is the first month, so it is **일월 (one-month)**; February is the second month, **이월 (two-month)**, and so on through December, which is **십이월 (twelve-month)**.

There are two critical pronunciation exceptions that the instructor highlights with on-screen corrections. For June and October, the final consonant (받침) of the number is omitted to make the words easier to pronounce. June, which uses the number six (**육**), becomes **유월** instead of **육월**. Similarly, October, which uses the number ten (**십**), becomes **시월** instead of **십월**. The instructor emphasizes that these are not grammar rules but are purely for phonetic comfort.

---

### Days and Full Date Construction
Expressing the day is straightforward: you state the Sino-Korean number followed by the unit **일**. For example, the 1st is **일일**, the 2nd is **이일**, and the 18th is **십팔일**.

To construct a full date, you simply string the three components together in order. The instructor provides a practice example for December 23rd, 1992:
> **1992년 12월 23일** / December 23rd, 1992

Another example provided is October 15th, 2011:
> **2011년 10월 15일** / October 15th, 2011

The instructor circles the units on screen to reinforce the pattern: [Number] + **년**, [Number] + **월**, [Number] + **일**.
