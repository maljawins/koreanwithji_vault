````md
**Yes & No in Korean**

Okay. Let’s learn how to say yes and no in Korean.

네 and 예 are “yes” in 존댓말. And they’re inherently polite, so you don’t need to add “요”.

And they’re interchangeable, but **네** is more common than **예** as 예 feels a bit more formal than 네.

**Why 네 Sounds Like "De"**

Now, let me address a very common question while we're on the subject: Why does 네 sound like "de" to non-Korean ears?

One thing you have to understand is that Korean sounds are different from English sounds. This is why you need to pay attention to the actual sounds of 한글 rather than romanization.

Korean consonants are pronounced a lot more abruptly than English consonants. They have a shorter "holding time," so to speak, before you open your mouth and vibrate your vocal cords.

영어 자음은 부드럽게 흐르듯이 발음이 되지만 한국어의 자음은 발음할 때 입에서 “short burst”가 생긴다.

Now, when you say N sound in English you put your tongue behind your upper teeth. But ㄴ (ni eun) comes from between lower and upper teeth.

So when Koreans say 네 fast, it makes them slightly bite their tongue thereby adding abruptness on top of that, making it sound like 데.

**Yes in 반말**

Moving on to 반말, “Yes” in 반말 is **응** or **어**. “어” can sometimes feel a bit cold and slightly unwelcoming than “응”. They’re also interchangeable in most cases.

**No in Korean**

For "No," you do need to add 요 to indicate politeness:

**아니요** – Polite. (often shortened to 아뇨)

**아니** – Casual.

You might have heard “아니에요”. This means “It isn’t” rather than a simple “No” and it’s actually a conjugated form of the verb “아니다”, which means “to not be”.

We’ll get into this when we learn how to say “A is not B.” but just know that it’s 아니요 and 아니 are simple “No”.

**How Koreans use “네”**

“네” and “아니요” have special usages in Korean that “Yes” and “No” in English don’t quite do. And here’s a few short clips for you to watch before we talk about them.

<https://youtu.be/dWWeJuv4Xjw>

<https://youtu.be/UdWtkCuL6-M>

Now that you get the idea, let’s first look at how Koreans use “네”

<https://youtu.be/g-dm_A0XymU>

**1) Responding When Someone Calls You**

This is similar to English—when someone calls your name, you could say "yes."

So in a situation like this, you can say “네” or “응” in Korean. For example, in school, when the teacher is taking attendance and you hear your name, you would respond with “here” in English. However in Korean, we respond with just “네”.

**2) Phone call**

The go-to phrase for answering a phone call in Korean is 여보세요, which means “Hello” but only for phone calls - for greeting.

But your phone always shows you the name or the number of the person that’s calling you. And most of the time, that person is someone you know.

And since you know this person, you don’t really have to bother with “여보세요”. Instead, you can just answer with "**네**" or **“응”**.

And you can also end a phone call with “네” and “응” like 네~ or 응~ with an elongated tone which signals the end of the call.

**3) Showing You're Listening**

In a conversation, if you stay too silent while the other person is talking, sometimes it might seem like you're not listening closely. So to show that you’re actually paying attention, you can say 네 or 응.

This is similar to "uh-huh" or "right" in English—it's just a sound to show you're listening.

You can also say 네네 or 응응 by repeating it twice.

**4) "Sorry?” “What?” “Pardon?”**

You can also use 네 to ask someone to repeat what they said.

There are two situations for this:

First, when you didn't hear what they said—like "Sorry?"

Second, when you're surprised or can't believe what they said—like "Really?"

In this case, the intonation goes up like a question: 네?

**5) To respond to a command / suggestion**

Sort of like “Okay” or “Alright” “Got it” in English. This one’s pretty straight forward.

So, let’s move on to how Koreans use “아니.”

**How Koreans use “아니”**

<https://youtu.be/R5IFSfKpJTY>

<https://youtu.be/x74IMN6TV4Q>

If you watch Korean shows, you might have noticed Koreans start their sentences with “아니” a lot - Even in situations where it’s not necessarily in response to a “yes or no” question.

“아니” does mean “No”. But in this specific context, they’re not trying to negate whatever it was said beforehand.

It’s rather used like a filler word at the beginning to add emotion to your sentence. We even have a slang that refers to “아니시에이팅” with “아니” and the English word “initiating” combined.

I’ll show you how 3 cases where 아니 can be used.

Note that I'm not going to cover the grammar in the example sentences that follow — So just focus on feeling how 아니시에이팅 works.

**1. To express surprise, doubt, or frustration**. - This usage is similar to saying "wait", or "But like" in English.

Ex: “아니, 거기를 네가 왜 갔어?” – “Wait, why did YOU go there?”

**2. To Make an Additional Explanation, Add an Excuse, or Offer a Denial**

This can be compared to "I mean" in English.

“아니, 방이 너무 더운거야” – “I mean, the room was too hot”

Then you would come up with some sort of follow up story after that. like “So, I opened the window.” or something like that.

**3. To Grab the Listener's Attention**

Similar to "Look," "Hey," or "By the way" in English.

Example: 아니, 근데 뭐 할까요?
→ "By the way, what should we do?"

I think this is one of those things that can’t really be “taught” logically. Instead, it is “learned” through your intuition. So, give yourself some time to get used to it. And I’m sure you’ll be able to “feel” it in time as you expose yourself to more input.

**When Yes means No**

Sometimes, in Korean, Yes means No and No means Yes. And this happens when you’re answering a negative question.

Let’s say someone asks you “Aren’t you hungry?”

In English, “Yes” would mean “Yes, I AM hungry” because **"Yes"** means “Yes” regardless of whether the question is negative or not.

But in Korean, **you respond to the negative in the question. So if you said,**

* **네** → This would mean "I’m NOT hungry" because it means “Yes, I agree with the negative”
* **And 아니요** would mean "I AM hungry" because it means “No, I disagree with the negative.”

This could take some time to get used to but to give you a tip, think of it like multiplying negative numbers: **(-1) × (-1) = (+1)**.

**Wrap-Up**

Alright. In this lesson, we learned how to say "yes" and "no" in Korean, and when to use them in different situations.

In the next lesson, we're going to learn "hello" and "goodbye." See you there
````

````md
### Speech Levels: 네 vs. 응 vs. 어

The word **네** is the standard polite (존댓말) way to say "yes." It is used in formal situations or when speaking to someone who requires a level of respect. For casual situations with friends, speakers use **응**. In actual spoken conversation, **응** is frequently pronounced as a shorter **어**. The instructor emphasizes that all the functional meanings discussed in the video apply to these casual forms as well as the polite **네**.
````

#### Basic Agreement and Acknowledgment

- 원문 줄: 30~39

````md
### Basic Agreement and Acknowledgment

The most fundamental use of **네** is to express "yes" or to agree with someone. Beyond a simple affirmative, it often functions as "okay" or "all right." In this capacity, it frequently precedes other polite expressions to soften the transition or show immediate acknowledgment. For example, when receiving an instruction, one might say:
> **네, 알겠습니다.** / Yes, I understand.

Similarly, when someone greets you with **안녕하세요**, it is very common to respond by starting with **네** before returning the greeting:
> **네, 안녕하세요.** / Yes, (all right), hello.
````

#### Using 네 to Mean No

- 원문 줄: 40~51

````md
### Using 네 to Mean "No"

A common point of confusion for English speakers is how to answer negative questions in Korean. In English, "yes" and "no" usually refer to the facts of the situation. In Korean, **네** and **아니요** refer to whether you agree with the speaker's statement. If someone asks a negative question like "Don't you like this?" and you do not like it, you answer with **네** because you are agreeing with their assessment that you don't like it.

The instructor demonstrates this with the sentence:
> **이거 좋아하지 않아요?** / Don't you like this?
> — **네.** / Yes (I don't like it).

In this specific grammatical context, **네** effectively translates to "No" in English because it confirms the negative state mentioned by the questioner.
````

#### Backchanneling and Phone Etiquette

- 원문 줄: 52~61

````md
### Backchanneling and Phone Etiquette

In Korean conversation, especially on the phone, it is culturally important to provide constant verbal feedback to show you are still listening. This is known as backchanneling. Without periodic interjections of **네, 네**, the person speaking may think the connection has been lost or that you are not paying attention.

There are also specific rules for starting and ending phone calls. While **여보세요** is used when you don't know who is calling, if you recognize the caller (via caller ID), you should start the call with **네** followed by their title or name. For example, if your boss calls, you would answer:
> **네, 사장님.** / Yes, Boss. (Hello, Boss.)

To end a call politely, Koreans use a lengthened **네** with a rising intonation: **네~~**. This serves as a signal that you are about to hang up. Usually, both parties will exchange this lengthened **네** before disconnecting.
````

#### Intonation and Repetition

- 원문 줄: 63~73

````md
### Intonation and Repetition

The meaning of **네** changes significantly based on how it is pronounced and how many times it is repeated.

A double **네, 네** with a longer first syllable and a shorter second syllable means "All right, I got it, don't worry." It is used when someone is giving you a reminder or a task and you want to reassure them that you've understood.

A quick, double **네, 네** said without a pause means "Yes, of course" or "Go ahead and speak." This is used to give someone permission to continue or to show enthusiastic agreement.

A short, clipped **네?** with a rising question intonation means "Sorry?" or "Pardon?" and is used when you didn't hear what was said. If this rising **네?** is drawn out longer, it shifts meaning to "What?" or "Huh?" expressing surprise or disbelief at what was just heard.
````

````md
### Roll Call and Presence

When a teacher is taking attendance or someone is checking for your presence in a group, the standard response is **네**. While English speakers might say "Here," Koreans do not typically say **여기 있어요** (which literally means "It's over here") in a roll call setting. Instead, they simply say **네** to acknowledge their name being called.
````

#### Backchanneling: The Uh-huh Function

- 원문 줄: 35~42

````md
### Backchanneling: The "Uh-huh" Function

The most significant use of 네 for learners to master is its function as a backchannel—a sound made to show the other person you are still paying attention. In this context, 네 does not literally mean "I agree" or "Yes to your question"; it functions like "uh-huh," "yeah," or "I see" in English.

The instructor highlights that Koreans use this much more frequently than English speakers. It is common to say **네** after every single thought or separate point the speaker makes, rather than waiting for them to finish their entire sentence or story. This is especially critical during phone conversations where visual cues like nodding are absent. If a listener remains silent for too long, the speaker may stop and ask "Hello?" or "Do you understand what I'm saying?" because they assume the connection has been lost or the listener has tuned out.
````

#### Frequency and Politeness Nuance

- 원문 줄: 43~47

````md
### Frequency and Politeness Nuance

The frequency of saying **네** can actually signal the level of respect being shown. When speaking to someone of much higher status, such as a boss or a highly respected teacher, it is appropriate to interject with **네** at the end of every single thought they express. Even in casual conversations with friends, using **응** or **어** whenever there is a brief silence or a new point is introduced helps maintain the connection. The instructor notes that while this constant repetition might sound humorous to a beginner, it is completely normal to a native speaker.
````

#### Variations and Additional Expressions

- 원문 줄: 49~61

````md
### Variations and Additional Expressions

The instructor introduces variations of "yes" that carry specific conversational nuances. Repeating the word twice, such as **네네** (polite) or **응응 / 어어** (casual), often signals to the speaker that you want them to continue. It translates roughly to "Yeah, yeah, go ahead" or "I'm following, keep talking."

Beyond simple agreement, the instructor introduces expressions derived from the verb **그렇다 (to be so)**. These are used to show stronger acknowledgment or to say "That's right" or "Indeed."
- **그렇죠** — A polite way to say "That's right" or "Is that so?"
- **그쵸** — A common, shortened version of **그렇죠** used in speech.
- **그렇지** — The casual version used with friends.

Other sounds include **아~**, which is a reaffirming sound showing you are listening, or **아 네**, which is used when you have just realized or understood something new ("Oh, I see").
````

#### Teaching Points: 아니 beyond no

- 원문 줄: 15~75

````md
## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor explains that while beginners learn **아니** (no) as a simple negative response, native speakers use it far more frequently as an emotional filler or exclamation. In many conversational contexts, **아니** does not actually mean "no" or negate the previous statement; instead, it serves as a linguistic "exclamation point" or a segue that colors the sentence with a specific emotion. By mastering these non-literal uses of **아니**, learners can transition from textbook-style speech to a more natural, native-like conversational flow.

---

### Dictionary Definitions vs. Real-World Usage

The instructor shows an on-screen dictionary entry for **아니** which lists two primary functions. The first is the standard response meaning "no" or "nope." The second is an expression of surprise, equivalent to English exclamations like "Why!", "What!", or "Good heavens!"

However, the instructor argues that real-world usage is much broader than the dictionary suggests. She describes **아니** as a versatile tool used to display any sort of emotion—not just surprise. It often functions as a segue into a sentence without affecting the literal meaning of the words that follow. While there is no strict grammatical formula for when to use it, its presence signals the speaker's internal state before they even get to the main point of their sentence.

---

### 아니 for Surprise and Shock

The most common non-literal use of **아니** is to express surprise. The instructor demonstrates this through several roleplay scenarios. In one scene, she greets a friend she didn't expect to see by saying:

> **아니, 이게 누구야?** / Why, who’s this?

In another scenario, she reacts to someone eating an enormous amount of food:

> **아니, 그걸 다 먹을 수 있단 말이에요?** / So you’re saying you can eat all that?

Finally, she uses it to react to shocking news, such as a friend's breakup:

> **아니, 왜?** / What, why?

In all these cases, the speaker isn't saying "no" to anything; they are using **아니** to heighten the sense of disbelief or astonishment.

---

### 아니 for Negative Emotions: Anger, Frustration, and Defensiveness

**아니** is frequently used to lead into sentences fueled by negative energy. When used with anger, it acts as a sharp starter for a confrontation. For example, a speaker might snap at someone who is late:

> **아니, 지금 몇 시야?** / (Hey,) what time is it right now?

When expressing frustration at someone's lack of knowledge or common sense, **아니** emphasizes the speaker's exasperation:

> **아니, 그걸 어떻게 모를 수가 있어?** / (Seriously,) how could you not know that?

It is also a staple for defensive speech. When someone feels accused or misunderstood, they often start their rebuttal with **아니** to signal their disagreement with the premise of the conversation:

> **아니, 내가 거짓말이라도 했어?** / It’s not like I lied or anything, is it?

---

### 아니 for Softening Speech: Shyness and Excuses

Interestingly, **아니** can also be used to soften a sentence or indicate hesitation, particularly when the speaker is being shy or making an excuse. The instructor provides a roleplay of someone nervously asking for a date:

> **아니, 제가 영화표가 두 장 생겼는데, 혹시 내일 같이 가실래요?** / Oh, it's just that I got two movie tickets, do you happen to be free tomorrow?

In the context of making excuses, **아니** serves as a buffer before delivering the explanation. If someone asks why you didn't call, you might start with:

> **아니, 내가 어제 전화하려고 했는데, 배터리가 없었어.** / Well, I did try to call yesterday, but I had no battery.

In these instances, the word functions almost like "Well..." or "It's just that..." in English, helping the speaker navigate a potentially awkward social interaction.
````

````md
### Speech Level Context: 네 vs. 응

Before diving into the specific meanings, the instructor establishes the social context of the word. 네 is categorized as 존댓말, the honorific or polite form of speech. It is used when speaking to people who are older, higher in rank, or strangers. In casual situations (반말) with friends or younger people, 네 is replaced by 응. #### Five meanings of 네 and phone-call ending
````

- 원문 줄: 29~91

````md
### Meaning 1: Responding When Addressed

The first function of 네 is as a response when someone calls your name or addresses you. This is equivalent to saying "Yes?" or "Here!" in English. The instructor provides the example of a teacher taking attendance in a classroom; whereas an American student might say "here," a Korean student responds with 네. 

This also applies to answering the phone. Because of caller ID, if you know who is calling, you can skip the standard 여보세요 (hello) and answer directly with 네 or 네 followed by the person's title. A visual example shows the instructor answering a call from her mother by saying:
> **네, 엄마** / Yes, Mom.

Similarly, she notes that her aunt might answer a call from her mother (the aunt's older sister-in-law) with **네, 언니**.

---

### Meaning 2: Answering a Yes/No Question

This is the most common meaning: using 네 as a direct affirmative answer. The instructor demonstrates this with a roleplay where one person asks:
> **주말에 저랑 같이 영화 보러 가실래요?** / Do you want to see a movie with me on the weekend?
> — **네.** / Yes.

A crucial grammatical distinction is made here: this is the only situation where 네 can be replaced by 아니요 (no) to change the meaning. For the other four pragmatic functions, 아니요 cannot be used as a substitute because those functions are about communication flow rather than factual affirmation.

---

### Meaning 3: Backchanneling (Showing You Are Listening)

In conversation, especially during long explanations or rants, staying completely silent can be perceived as not paying attention. In English, people use "uh-huh" or "mm-hmm." In Korean, speakers use 네 (or 응 in casual speech) as a "listening sound." It carries no specific meaning other than signaling to the speaker that you are still present and following along.

---

### Meaning 4: "What?" (Clarification and Disbelief)

The fourth meaning is divided into two sub-categories based on intent, both characterized by a rising intonation (**네?**).

Meaning 4-1 is for clarification. When you miss what someone said and need them to repeat it, you use a short, clipped 네? with a rising tone. This is equivalent to "Sorry?" or "Pardon?" 

Meaning 4-2 is for disbelief or shock. In this case, you heard the person perfectly, but the information is so surprising that you respond with an emphatic 네? to express astonishment. The instructor provides an example sentence:
> **저 이번 달까지만 일하고 그만두기로 했어요.** / I decided to work only until this month and then quit.
> — **네?!** / What?! (Disbelief)

---

### Meaning 5: Confirmation and Acknowledgment

The final meaning functions like "Okay," "Got it," or "I understand." It signals that you have received instructions or information and are giving your consent or confirmation. The instructor provides a workplace example:
> **그 보고서 오늘까지 좀 부탁드려요.** / Please have that report done by today.
> — **네.** / Got it. / Okay.

Another example shows the word used to acknowledge plans:
> **그럼 내일 4시에 봐요.** / Then I'll see you tomorrow at 4.
> — **네.** / Okay.

---

### Bonus: Ending a Phone Call

The instructor explains that Koreans rarely use standard "goodbye" phrases like 안녕히 가세요 or 안녕히 계세요 on the phone because those imply physical movement or staying in a location. Instead, phone calls are often ended using a "finalizing 네."

To signal the end of a call, speakers will often use a transition sentence followed by a lengthened 네 with a slightly rising pitch (**네~~**). Common transition sentences include:
- **그럼 내일 봐요.** / Then, see you tomorrow.
- **들어가세요.** / (Literally "Go in") Take care / Goodbye.
- **그럼 끊을게요.** / Then, I'll hang up.

The instructor notes that **들어가세요** is a very common way to end a call, acting as a signal that the parties are returning to their separate lives. Once one person says a lengthened 네~~, the other responds in kind, and they disconnect.
````

#### Slide 5 — YES & NO

- 원문 줄: 48~54

````md
### Slide 5 — YES & NO
- **On screen:** A table for Yes/No. 
    - **YES:** **네** & **예** (존댓말/High), **응** & **어** (반말/Low).
    - **NO:** **아니오** (Informal High), **아니** (Informal Low), **아닙니다** (Formal High).
    - Notes: **네** & **예** are inherently polite (formality irrelevant) and interchangeable (**네** is more common). **응** & **어** are interchangeable. **아니** technically means "Not" and needs a suffix for **존댓말**.
- **Ji's teaching:** **네** and **예** in **존댓말** are inherently polite, so there’s no distinction between formal and informal, and you don’t need to add any suffix. They’re interchangeable, but **네** is more common. **응** and **어** in **반말** are also interchangeable. For "No," **아니** technically means "not" rather than a direct "no," so you need to add a suffix to indicate politeness: **아니오** for Informal High, **아니** for Informal Low, and **아닙니다** for Formal High.

````

#### Slide 6 — When No means Yes in Korean

- 원문 줄: 55~62

````md
### Slide 6 — When No means Yes in Korean
- **On screen:** Comparison between English and Korean logic. 
    - English: "Yes" always means agreement with the fact (I am hungry).
    - Korean: You agree or disagree with the *negative* in the question.
    - Example: "Aren't you hungry?" -> **네** (Yes, I'm NOT hungry / I agree with your negative question). **아니오** (No, I AM hungry / I disagree with your negative question).
    - Visual: **-1 x -1 = +1** (highlighted in blue).
- **Ji's teaching:** In English, answering "Yes" means agreement regardless of whether the question is positive or negative. But in Korean, you respond to the negative in the question. So if someone asks "Aren't you hungry?" and you say **네**, you are saying "Yes, I am not hungry" because you are agreeing with their negative statement. If you say **아니오**, you are saying "No, I am hungry" because you are disagreeing with the negative. Think of it like multiplying negative numbers: **(-1) × (-1) = (+1)**.

````
