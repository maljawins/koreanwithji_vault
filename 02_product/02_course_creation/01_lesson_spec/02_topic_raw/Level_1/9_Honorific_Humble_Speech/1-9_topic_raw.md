````md
**Intro**

Now that we've covered how to make basic SOV sentences including the boring grammar rule about the irregular verbs, I think it's time we learned about honorific and humble speech.

We actually talked about these concepts in the first lesson in level 1, but now, it’s time for a deep-dive.

🔹**Reminder – What is an honorific?**

I have seen many creators and teachers misuse the term “honorific” without properly defining what it even is. And this is not only confusing but downright misleading for so many learners. So, I’m going to say this once again as a reminder.

**Honorific and Speech Level are NOT the same thing.**

You use different speech levels depending on *who* you're talking *to* whereas you use honorifics to show respect to people you’re talking ABOUT like elders, teachers, your boss or clients.

When you’re talking to your best friend about your grandmother, you’d be speaking in 반말 (speech level) but still use an honorific to address your grandmother.

So, I repeat. Honorific and Speech Level are NOT the same thing.

Now that we got that out of the way, let’s get right into the lesson

**How to make honorific verbs**

Here’s how to make a honorific verbs. You add **시** or **으시** to the verb stem.

* If the stem ends in a vowel, just add **시**.
  + Example: **주다 (to give)** → **주시다** (to give [honorific])
    Now you have your new stem, 주시
* If the stem ends in a 받침 (final consonant), you add **으시**.
  + Example: **읽다 (to read)** → **읽으시다** (to read [honorific])
    And now, 읽으시 is your new stem.

**받침-sensitive - Reminder**

As you just saw, 시 and 으시 are “받침-sensitive”. I talked about this very common suffix pattern in level 1 if you remember.

You’ve already seen this with topic and subject marker: 은/는 and 이/가.

* After a **받침**, the following suffix **starts with ㅇ**, the empty consonant.
  (Like 은, 이)
* After a vowel though, the following suffix d**oesn’t start with ㅇ**.
  (Like 는, 가)

Most Korean suffixes, whether it’s a particle, sentence connector, they usually follow this rule with very few exceptions.

So from now on, I’m just going to refer to this whole idea as 받침-sensitive.

**Honorific verbs conjugation – Present Tense (Informal)**

So how do you conjugate honorific verbs? It’s the same rule. 시 doesn’t end in ㅗ or ㅏ, so it’s should be 어.

Let’s use 하다 - to do as an example.

In honorific form, 하다 becomes 하시다. Then when you conjugate it, 하시다 becomes

* Casual: 하셔
* Polite: 하셔요 or 하세요

First of all, in 반말, it’s always 셔**, not 세.** So, 하세 is just wrong.

Example: [엄마가 요리를 하셔 / 하세(X)]

**And as you can see, there are two versions for the polite speech.**

**I’ll talk about which one to use in a minute but let’s first look at** why we have two versions.

**Phonetically**, 시 + 어 becomes **셔**.
When you say 시어 quickly, it naturally sounds like 셔.

하시+어 → 하셔요

**Alphabetically**, 시 + 어 becomes **세**.
The vowels of 시 and 어, ㅣ and ㅓ, combines into ㅔ. [Show the movement of the vowels]

하시+어 → 하세요

Now, 세요 is much more common in modern Korean though you might hear 셔요 from time to time. But from my experience it was mostly from an older generation. So, I’d say just stick with 세요.

Well, that was the simple answer. And here’s the technical answer.

When you’re referring to a second person (Title+님, Name+씨), you’d almost always use 세요, not 셔요.

For example:

왜 거짓말을 하세요? – Why are you lying?

**하셔요** here sounds a bit like old Korean because you’re referring to the 2nd person.

**And when talking about a third person** (이 분, 저 분, 그 분)**, both 셔요 and 세요 sound natural.**

아버지가 영화를 보셔요 – My father watches (is watching) a movie.

아버지가 영화를 보세요.

So, both of these sentences sound perfectly fine.

But again, when in doubt, just stick with 세요 in polite speech and 셔 in casual speech.

**Honorific verbs conjugation – Present Tense (Formal)**

Formal speech is the easiest to make. Since the stem of honorific verbs always ends in 시, all you need to do is add ㅂ니다.

[Show 보시다 (Hon.) – Look, See, Watch]

할머니는 아기를 보십니다

So 보시다 becomes 보십니다.

And 적으시다, which comes from [적다 - to write down], becomes 적으십니다. The only difference is 시 and 으시 and the rest is the same.

Formal high 교수님꼐서 점수를 적으십니다.

**Irregular honorific verbs**

Just like every other language, Korean also has exceptions so not all verbs follow the 시 & 으시 pattern. Instead, they have completely different words in their honorific form.

But don’t worry there’s only a handful of verbs like this.

먹다 to eat and 마시다 to drink become 드시다. And why is 드시다 used for both 먹다 and 마시다?

In Korean, you can “eat” liquid, too. 먹다 technically refers to anything you put in your mouth. There’s also 잡수다 for 먹다 but this is not very common these days although you might hear it occasionally.

We also learned that 있다 turns into 계시다 in level 1, greetings are farewell.

자다 to sleep becomes 주무시다

And 죽다 to die becomes 돌아가시다. Now, 돌아가시다 is actually a compound verb – a combination of 돌다 to turn and 가시다 to go. So, to turn and go – To go back. So it’s a euphemism like “to pass away” in English

**These are just about all the irregular honorific verbs you need to know.** But here are some outdated ones just in case you might encounter them.

시장하시다 is an honorific version of 배고프다 To be hungry. Again, outdated. You could just apply the regular suffix rule and say 배고프시다 instead

편찮으시다 is an honorific version of 아프다 to be sick or ill. But nobody says this.

Let’s talk about humble verbs, now.

**Humble Speech**

What is humble speech?

Whereas we use honorifics to elevate someone, humble speech is for lowering ourselves to show respect.

Just like 나 - I, in humble speech is 저 and 우리 - we becomes 저희.

When it comes to humble VERBS though, this usually happens in interactive actions like giving, seeing, asking or telling. And there’s only a handful of humble verbs.

**만나다 or 보다 to meet or see becomes 뵙다.**

**묻다 to ask becomes 여쭈다.**

**주다 to give becomes 드리다**

**말하다 to tell, say, or speak becomes 말씀드리다.**

So for example, I gave a candy to grandma would be 할머니에게 사탕을 드렸어요, not 줬어요.
````

#### The Subject of Respect

- 원문 줄: 24~32

````md
---

### The Subject of Respect

Honorific speech is used to show extra respect toward the person someone is talking about. This person could be the listener, but it does not have to be; it can be anyone, whether they are present or not. For example, if speaking to a friend (using casual speech) about a grandmother, it would be considered rude not to use honorifics for the grandmother. Therefore, one might use casual endings for the friend but honorific verb forms for the grandmother's actions.

There are specific limits to who receives honorifics. They are reserved strictly for people, never animals or inanimate objects. Using honorifics toward a boss's dog, for instance, would be socially awkward as it places the dog on the same level as the boss. Similarly, honorifics are generally not used for children or regular objects. The instructor notes that while there are rare exceptions for objects in specific commercial contexts, the general rule is "people only." Typical recipients of honorific speech include teachers, mentors, bosses, higher-ranking individuals, strangers, grandparents, and often one's own parents depending on the family dynamic.
````

#### Basic Honorific Verb Endings: (으)시다

- 원문 줄: 34~41

````md
### Basic Honorific Verb Endings: (으)시다

The most fundamental way to create an honorific verb is by attaching the suffix (으)시다 to the verb stem. If the verb stem ends in a consonant, 으시다 is added; if it ends in a vowel, 시다 is added. The instructor demonstrates this with 읽다 (to read) becoming 읽으시다 and 하다 (to do) becoming 하시다.

There are specific rules for stems ending in certain consonants. For stems ending in ㄹ, such as 살다 (to live), the ㄹ is removed before adding 시다, resulting in 사시다. For stems ending in ㅂ that undergo the ㅂ-irregular change, such as 덥다 (to be hot), the ㅂ is replaced with 우 before adding 시다, resulting in 더우시다. Once these honorific stems are formed, they can be conjugated into any tense or speech level.
````

#### Conjugating Honorific Verbs

- 원문 줄: 42~52

````md
### Conjugating Honorific Verbs

While honorific verbs conjugate like normal verbs in most tenses, the present tense 요 form (polite level) has a unique but simple pattern. Instead of the expected 시어요, it almost always becomes 세요. For example, 하시다 becomes 하세요 and 읽으시다 becomes 읽으세요. This 세요 ending is the standard way to use honorifics in the present polite style.

In other tenses, the conjugation follows standard rules. For the past tense, 하시다 becomes 하셨어요 (시 + 었 + 어요). For the future tense, it becomes 하실 거예요 (시 + ㄹ 거예요). The instructor provides example sentences to illustrate these:
> **피자를 좋아하세요?** / Do you like pizza?
> **할머니도 피자를 좋아하세요?** / Does grandmother also like pizza?
> **준비하셨어요?** / Did you get ready? / Are you prepared?
````

#### Unique Honorific Verbs

- 원문 줄: 53~66

````md
### Unique Honorific Verbs

Certain common verbs have unique honorific versions that must be used instead of the (으)시다 suffix. Using the regular suffix with these verbs sounds awkward or incorrect to native speakers. The instructor lists the most essential pairs:
- **먹다 (to eat)** becomes **드시다**
- **자다 (to sleep)** becomes **주무시다**
- **죽다 (to die)** becomes **돌아가시다 (to pass away)**
- **있다 (to exist/be at a place)** becomes **계시다** (when referring to a person's presence)

A special note is made regarding 있다. While 계시다 is used for a person's location (e.g., "Grandmother is at home"), the form 있으시다 is used when talking about things a respected person possesses, such as their time, a bag, or body parts.
> **할머니는 지금 집에 계세요.** / Grandmother is at home right now.
> **지금 혹시 시간이 있으세요?** / Do you happen to have time right now?
````

#### Honorific Nouns and Counter Words

- 원문 줄: 67~76

````md
### Honorific Nouns and Counter Words

Respect is also expressed through specific nouns and counters when referring to a respected person's attributes or presence. These words should never be used to refer to oneself. The instructor highlights the following transitions:
- **집 (house)** becomes **댁**
- **사람 (person)** becomes **분** (also used as a counter: 한 분, 두 분)
- **이름 (name)** becomes **성함**
- **나이 (age)** becomes **연세**
- **생일 (birthday)** becomes **생신**

In a restaurant setting, staff will often ask **몇 분이세요? (How many people?)**, to which the customer should respond using the regular counter 명 (e.g., **두 명이요**) because one does not use honorifics for oneself. Another example provided is **오늘 할머니 생신이에요 (Today is grandmother's birthday)**.
````

````md
### Understanding 낮춤말

The term 낮춤말 comes from the causative verb 낮추다, which means "to make something low" or "to lower." The instructor emphasizes that humble speech and honorific speech are two sides of the same coin; they are often used together because they serve the same purpose of expressing respect. You typically use humble speech when speaking to teachers, bosses, coworkers (if not close), leaders, grandparents, other people's family members, and strangers. It is a way to signal that you recognize the other person's higher rank or the need for social distance and politeness.
````

#### Humble Verbs: 드리다 and 뵈다/뵙다

- 원문 줄: 29~55

````md
### Humble Verbs: 드리다 and 뵈다/뵙다

The first major humble verb is **드리다**, which is the humble equivalent of **주다 (to give)**. The instructor describes the feeling of 드리다 as kneeling down and offering something up with both hands. This verb can replace 주다 in any context, including auxiliary verb constructions.

- **주다** (to give) → **드리다** (to humbly give)
- **도와주다** (to help) → **도와드리다** (to humbly help)
- **만들어 주다** (to make for someone) → **만들어 드리다** (to humbly make for someone)

Example sentences provided:
> **선생님께 드리고 싶었어요.** / I wanted to give it to the teacher.
> **도와드리고 싶어요.** / I want to help (you).
> **이것도 드릴까요?** / Shall I give you this too?
> **제가 만들어 드릴게요.** / I will make it for you.

The second set of verbs is **뵈다** and **뵙다**, which are humble versions of **보다 (to see/meet)**. While they have the same meaning, 뵙다 is slightly more formal but less common than 뵈다. They both conjugate to **봬요** in the polite present tense. The instructor notes that while these replace 보다 when meeting a person, they cannot replace the auxiliary verb 보다 (used for "trying" something).

- **보다** (to see/meet) → **뵈다 / 뵙다** (to humbly see/meet)

Example sentences provided:
> **처음 뵙겠습니다.** / Nice to meet you (Literally: I see you for the first time).
> **내일 봬요.** / See you tomorrow.
> **그럼 언제 다시 뵐 수 있을까요?** / Then when can we meet again?

A critical grammar rule mentioned is that humble verbs cannot be combined with the honorific marker **-(으)시-**. For example, you cannot say **들이시다** or **뵈시다** because it is logically inconsistent to lower yourself and raise yourself at the same time in the same word.

````

#### Humble Nouns and Pronouns: 저 and 저희

- 원문 줄: 56~66

````md
### Humble Nouns and Pronouns: 저 and 저희

Humble speech also requires using specific pronouns to refer to oneself or one's group. Using these instead of their plain counterparts is essential for maintaining a humble tone.

- **나** (I - plain) → **저** (I - humble)
- **우리** (We/Us/Our - plain) → **저희** (We/Us/Our - humble)

The instructor notes that **저** is not just "polite" but specifically "humble" because it lowers the speaker's status relative to the listener.
````

- 원문 줄: 67~84

````md
### Advanced Humble Verbs: 모시다 and 여쭈다

For more advanced learners, the instructor introduces two additional humble verbs: **모시다** and **여쭈다/여쭙다**.

**모시다** is the humble version of **데리다 (to take/bring a person)**. It is used when you are accompanying someone of higher status to a location.
- **데리고 가다/오다** → **모시고 가다/오다** (or **모셔 가다/오다**)

Example sentence:
> **공항에 사장님을 모시고 갔어요.** / I took my boss to the airport.

**여쭈다** (sometimes **여쭙다**) is the humble version of **묻다 (to ask)**. It is used when asking a question to someone you respect.
- **묻다** → **여쭈다 / 여쭙다**

Example sentence:
> **선생님께 여쭤봤어요.** / I asked the teacher.
````

#### Politeness Factors and Honorific Verbs

- 원문 줄: 26~33

````md
### Politeness Factors and Honorific Verbs

The instructor outlines three primary factors that dictate how Koreans speak to one another. First is age, where younger individuals use more respect toward elders. Second is social rank, exemplified by a student speaking formally to a teacher or a company representative being extra polite to a client. Third is the situation, such as giving a formal speech.

Beyond the standard `-(으)시다` ending, certain verbs change completely into honorific forms when speaking about someone else. The instructor presents a chart of these essential transformations: `먹다 (to eat)` becomes `드시다`, which conjugates as `드세요`. `있다 (to exist/be)` becomes `계시다`, conjugating as `계세요`. Its negative counterpart `없다` becomes `안 계시다`. Finally, `자다 (to sleep)` becomes `주무시다`, which conjugates as `주무세요`. A critical rule is highlighted: these honorific verbs are only for other people and should never be used when referring to oneself.
````

#### Understanding Humble Speech

- 원문 줄: 34~41

````md
### Understanding Humble Speech

Humble speech is defined as showing the speaker's own humility to show respect toward another person. Visually, the instructor compares honorific speech to putting someone on a pedestal (raising them up) and humble speech to lowering oneself down in front of that person. Because they share the goal of showing respect, they are frequently used in tandem.

There are two primary humble verbs to learn. Instead of `보다 (to see)`, one uses `뵈다` or the even more humble `뵙다`. Both conjugate to `뵈요`. For example, "I want to see the teacher tomorrow" becomes `내일 선생님을 뵙고 싶어요`. Instead of `주다 (to give)`, one uses `드리다`, which conjugates as `드려요`. This is used when the speaker is giving something to a person of higher status, such as `선생님에게 선물을 드리고 싶어요 (I want to give the teacher a present)`.
````

#### Honorific Particles and Nouns

- 원문 줄: 42~49

````md
### Honorific Particles and Nouns

To complement humble and honorific verbs, specific particles must be used. When saying "to" or "from" a person, the standard `에게` or `한테` is replaced by the honorific particle `께`. For the topic marker, `은/는` becomes `께서는`, and for the subject marker, `이/가` becomes `께서`. The instructor demonstrates this with the sentence `선생님께서 지금 바쁘세요 (The teacher is busy now)`, noting that the subject marker `께서` and the honorific verb ending `-(으)세요` are used together.

Specialized nouns also play a role in lowering the speaker or raising the subject. The word `저` is actually a humble version of "I/me." Similarly, `우리 (we/us)` becomes the humble `저희`. When referring to someone else's house in a polite way, the word `집` is replaced by `댁`. For example, `우리 할머니 댁에 놀러 오세요 (Come to hang out at my grandmother's house)`.
````

#### Honorifics Applied to Objects Over-Honorification

- 원문 줄: 30~38

````md
### Honorifics Applied to Objects (Over-Honorification)
In restaurants, workers often break the rule against honoring objects to maximize the "politeness feel" for the customer. The instructor highlights three common examples. First is the phrase **만원이세요**, used when telling a customer the price is 10,000 won. The worker uses **이시다** (the honorific form of the copula **이다**) instead of the standard **이에요**. This technically shows respect to the money itself—the "honorable 10,000 won"—which is grammatically wrong but socially accepted as a way to be soft and polite.

A second example involves the word for time. A worker might ask **시간 있으세요? (Do you have time?)**. While **있으시다** is the honorific of **있다 (to have/exist)**, the proper honorific for a person's existence is **계시다**. However, since the subject is "time" (an object), **있으시다** is used to honor the customer's time. The instructor notes this is a "passable" error because it indirectly honors the customer.

The third and very common example is heard at cafes: **주문하신 커피 나오셨습니다 (The coffee you ordered has come out)**. The first part, **주문하신 (that you ordered)**, is correct because it honors the customer's action of ordering. However, **나오셨습니다** applies the honorific suffix **-시-** to the verb "to come out," effectively treating the coffee as an honorable person. While strictly incorrect, it is ubiquitous because it sounds more professional and attentive than the plain **나왔습니다**.
````

````md
### Honorifics vs. Politeness Levels

Honorific speech is often confused with general politeness levels, but the instructor clarifies that they are separate systems. Politeness levels like the 요 form and 니다 form are used to show respect to the listener. In contrast, honorific speech shows respect to the person you are speaking about. This combines the honorific verb 계시다 (to exist/be) with a casual ending (어) because the listener is a friend, but the subject (dad) deserves respect.
````

#### The Honorific Suffix (으)시다

- 원문 줄: 34~46

````md
### The Honorific Suffix (으)시다

The most common way to create an honorific verb is by adding the suffix 시다 to the verb stem. If the stem ends in a consonant, 으시다 is used; if it ends in a vowel, simply 시다 is added. This suffix can then be conjugated into various speech levels. The instructor displays a chart of common transformations:

- **하다 (to do)** → 하세요, 하십니다, 하셔, 하시면, 하셨네
- **믿다 (to believe)** → 믿으세요, 믿으십니다, 믿으시고, 믿으셨어요
- **이다 (to be)** → 이세요, 이십니다, 이시죠, 이셨는데
- **알다 (to know)** → 아세요, 아십니까, 아시나요, 아실까요

The instructor highlights that the ending 세요 is a contraction of 시어요. While often used for polite commands like 주세요 (Please give me) or 하세요 (Please do it), it is also a standard honorific statement ending. For example, 선생님이 뭐 하세요? means "What is the teacher doing?" using the honorific form to respect the teacher.
````

#### Humble Verb Substitutions

- 원문 줄: 30~47

````md
### Humble Verb Substitutions

Certain common verbs have specific humble counterparts that must be used when the speaker is performing an action for someone of higher status. The instructor highlights two primary examples:

**1. 주다 (to give) → 드리다**
When giving something to a person who deserves respect, such as a teacher or an elder, the verb `주다 (to give)` is replaced by its humble form `드리다`. The instructor demonstrates this with a comparison:
> **내일 선물을 줄 거예요.** / I will give you a present tomorrow. (Standard/Polite)
> **내일 선물을 드릴 거예요.** / I will give you a present tomorrow. (Humble/Polite)
The second sentence is more appropriate when the recipient is someone like a teacher, as `드리다` shows the speaker's humility in the act of giving.

**2. 보다 (to see) → 뵙다 / 뵈다**
When meeting or seeing someone of higher status, `보다 (to see)` is replaced by `뵙다` or `뵈다`. The instructor notes that these are particularly common in greetings and farewells.
> **내일 봐요.** / I'll see you tomorrow. (Standard/Polite)
> **내일 봬요.** / I'll see you tomorrow. (Humble/Polite)
The form `봬요` is a contraction of `뵈어요`. Using this humble form indicates that the speaker is showing respect by lowering their own status relative to the person they are meeting.
````

#### Honorific and Humble Particles

- 원문 줄: 48~69

````md
### Honorific and Humble Particles

Beyond verbs, certain grammatical particles must be swapped for honorific versions when referring to or addressing a person of higher status. The instructor provides a chart of these transformations:

- **Subject Markers:** `이/가` becomes `께서`.
- **Topic Markers:** `은/는` becomes `께서는`.
- **Dative Markers (to/from):** `에게` and `에게서` are replaced by `께`.

The instructor provides example sentences to illustrate these particles in context:
> **아버지께서 집에 안 계세요.** / Dad is not at home.
In this sentence, the standard subject marker `가` is replaced by `께서` because the subject is `아버지 (father)`. Additionally, the honorific verb `계시다` (the honorific form of `있다`) is used.

Another example demonstrates the combination of honorific particles and humble verbs:
> **이제 할머니께서는 주무실 거예요.** / Grandma will sleep now.
Here, `께서는` replaces `는`, and the honorific verb `주무시다` (to sleep) is used to show respect to the grandmother.

Finally, an example using the dative particle `께` and the humble verb `드리다`:
> **선생님께 선물을 드렸어요.** / I gave the teacher a present.
This sentence uses `께` instead of `에게` and `드렸어요` instead of `줬어요`. This combination shows both extra respect toward the teacher and humility from the speaker.
````

````md
### Humble vs. Honorific: 드리다 vs. 주시다 and 뵈다 vs. 보시다

A common point of confusion for learners is the distinction between humble and honorific versions of the same action. The instructor clarifies that 드리다 is humble (speaker gives to superior), while 주시다 is honorific (superior gives to speaker). It is incorrect to use 드리다 when a superior is giving something to you, as that would imply they are lowering themselves. #### What Is Honorific Speech?
````

- 원문 줄: 24~30

````md
### What Is Honorific Speech?
Honorific speech, or 높임말, is used to show "extra respect" or "honor" toward the person you are talking about. The instructor highlights a crucial distinction: this is not necessarily the person you are talking to. You use 높임말 when saying anything about someone else or asking questions about someone else. It does not matter if the person being discussed is in the room or listening to the conversation; the respect is inherent to the subject.

There are strict boundaries for honorific usage. First, you must never use honorific speech when talking about yourself, as it would sound awkward and arrogant. Second, it is generally not used for close friends or people to whom you wouldn't normally show extra formal respect. Third, honorifics are never used for inanimate objects because there is no social need to show respect to a thing. Finally, honorifics are not used for animals. The instructor explains that using the same level of respect for a pet as for its owner effectively puts the owner and the animal on the same social level, which is inappropriate in Korean culture.
````

#### The 으 시다 Suffix and Verb Formation

- 원문 줄: 31~43

````md
### The (으)시다 Suffix and Verb Formation
The core of honorific speech involves transforming regular verbs into honorific verbs by attaching the suffix (으)시다 to the verb stem. The instructor provides specific rules for this transformation. If a verb stem ends in a vowel, you attach 시다. If it ends in a consonant, you attach 으시다. There are two major irregular rules: if the stem ends in ㄹ, you remove the ㄹ before attaching 시다; if the stem ends in ㅂ, you remove the ㅂ and attach 우시다.

The instructor demonstrates these transformations with several examples:
- **가다 (to go)** becomes **가시다**
- **읽다 (to read)** becomes **읽으시다**
- **살다 (to live)** becomes **사시다** (ㄹ dropped)
- **춥다 (to be cold)** becomes **추우시다** (ㅂ changed to 우)

Once a verb is made honorific, it can be conjugated into any politeness level. In the 요 form, the (으)시다 suffix specifically conjugates to (으)세요. For other forms, it follows standard conjugation patterns, often resulting in (으)셔 in casual speech or (으)십니다 in formal speech.
````

````md
### Honorifics for Things and Indirect Respect

A common phenomenon in Korean involves using honorifics for inanimate objects or body parts belonging to a respected person. ### Professional Titles and the Honorific 님
````
````md
The most respectful way to address someone is by their job title followed by the honorific suffix 님. This is the standard in workplace environments. Common examples include:
- **사장님** (Boss/CEO)
````

#### Forming the Honorific Base Form

- 원문 줄: 23~29

````md
### Forming the Honorific Base Form
The instructor explains that every Korean verb and adjective has an honorific form. To create the base honorific form, one must identify the verb stem and add the suffix -(으)시다. If the stem ends in a vowel, only -시다 is added; if it ends in a consonant (받침), -으시다 is added. For example, 가다 (to go) becomes 가시다, while 넣다 (to put in) becomes 넣으시다.

Several irregular patterns are highlighted during this process. For stems ending in ㄹ, such as 놀다 (to play) or 만들다 (to make), the ㄹ is dropped before adding -시다, resulting in 노시다 and 만드시다. For ㅂ irregular verbs like 눕다 (to lie down) or 덥다 (to be hot), the ㅂ is removed and replaced with 우 before adding -시다, resulting in 누우시다 and 더우시다. The ㄷ irregular is also addressed: for 듣다 (to listen), the ㄷ changes to ㄹ before adding -으시다, resulting in 들으시다. Interestingly, the instructor notes that 닫다 (to close) is regular and simply becomes 닫으시다.
````

- 원문 줄: 37~41

````md
### Unique Honorific Verbs: Group 1
Some common verbs have unique honorific forms that do not use the -(으)시다 suffix. The instructor introduces the first group: 먹다 (to eat) and 마시다 (to drink) both share the honorific form 드시다. The verb 자다 (to sleep) becomes 주무시다. For 말하다 (to speak), the noun 말 is upgraded to the honorific 말씀, resulting in 말씀하시다. These unique forms follow the same conjugation rules as regular honorifics (e.g., 드세요, 주무셨어요, 말씀하실 거예요).
````

#### Unique Honorifics with Contextual Meanings: 있다 and 주다

- 원문 줄: 42~48

````md
### Unique Honorifics with Contextual Meanings: 있다 and 주다
The instructor explains that some verbs have two different honorific forms depending on the intended meaning. The verb 있다 has two functions: possession (to have) and existence/location (to be somewhere). When expressing possession, the honorific form is 있으시다 (e.g., "Do you have a book?" = 책 있으세요?). When expressing location or presence, the form is 계시다 (e.g., "The teacher is in the classroom" = 선생님께서 교실에 계세요). 계시다 is also used for the honorific present progressive form -고 계시다.

The verb 주다 (to give) also has two forms: 주시다 and 드리다. The choice depends on who is being honored. 주시다 is used when the subject (the giver) is the person being honored (e.g., "Grandfather gave me money" = 할아버지께서 용돈을 주셨어요). Conversely, 드리다 is used when the receiver of the action is the person being honored, even if the subject is the speaker (e.g., "I gave the teacher a book" = 저는 선생님께 책을 드렸어요). This distinction is critical for showing proper respect in social interactions.
````

````md
- **On screen:** Suffix 시 or 으시 comes right after the stem (First Branch).
````
