````md
How Verb works Recap from the lesson “How Korean works”

I talked about how Korean verbs work in pretty detailed fashion in the lesson "How Korean Works," so I highly recommend reviewing the verb part of that lesson before we get started.

Now, before we talk about how to conjugate verbs, there's something very important you should know.

**Adjectives are Verbs in Korean**

That’s right. Adjectives ARE verbs in Korean.

Let me show you what I mean.

[Show - “The cat is quiet” VS “A quiet cat”]

In English, “quiet” doesn’t change no matter the usage. Now let’s look at Korean.

Show – 고양이: Cat / 조용하다: To be quiet

Show - 고양이가 조용해요 VS 조용한 고양이

As you can see, the part that corresponds to “quiet” changes its form in Korean.

This is why Korean adjectives are called “descriptive verbs” because they work exactly like verbs.

Let’s compare these two words.

[Show 공부하다: V. To study / 조용하다: Adj. To be quiet]

공부하다 means “To DO study”, it’s an action verb.

And 조용하다 means “To BE quiet”. And here, the state of being quiet itself is treated like one single verb.

But since both words end with 하다, they both conjugate exactly the same way in the present tense.

[Show 공부해요, I study / 조용해요, It is quiet.]

As you just saw, adjectives and verbs follow very similar conjugation rules in Korean. So I’m going to be using the term “descriptive verb” instead of “adjective” from here on out.

Important Verbs 하다, 되다, 나다, 내다
 - 하다 Verb 설명 (Sino-Korean verbs all end in 하다)
 - 되다 4가지 뜻 설명
 > 하다 VS 되다 (Active VS Passive)
````

````md
**Introduction**

Welcome. In this lesson, we are finally going to learn how to conjugate verbs in present tense.

First things first: Here's something you need to know about the Korean present tense.

**How Korean Uses Present Tense**

Koreans use the basic present tense like the progressive tense—like "-ing" in English.

For example, 뭐 하세요? literally means "What do?" and it only TRANSLATES to "What are you doing?" in English.

Of course, Korean does have a progressive tense: 뭐 하고 있어요?

We'll learn about this in Level 3, but this sounds more like "What are you doing RIGHT NOW?" As in, currently.

So, the basic present tense is more commonly used in general situations.

**Intro to Verb conjugation**

Now it's time to finally learn conjugation.

[Show: 먹다]

The first thing we need to do is find the stem by removing ~다 from the dictionary form.

So 먹 is our stem. This is where the verb holds its core meaning, and it stays the same for all regular verbs.

Irregular verb에 대해서는 Lesson 15에서 자세히 다루도록 하겠다.

Now, the ending is where the conjugation happens.

I'm sure you remember my plant analogy from "How Korean Works."

[Show: Stem / Branch / Leaf / Fruit]

**Plant Analogy**

* The **stem** is already there — that’s your base meaning.
* Next, we have the **branch**, which represents the tense but this part stays empty for the present tense.
* Then there’s the **leaf** which indicates the context.
* and finally a **fruit,** where it shows the speech level.

So here we have 살아요 – I live and 먹어요 – I eat.

**아/어 – Informal speech Conjugation**

[Show: 살아요 and 먹어요]

So there's your first "leaf": 아 and 어—the most basic verb suffix we use to make informal speech.

아/어 has no special meaning—sort of like the "s" in "she eats"—which is why I call it "the standard context marker."

Now, in case you forgot what a context marker is, it's a part in the ending that sets the contextual nuance in a conversation, thereby adding emotions to your sentences.

We'll learn about context markers in detail in lesson 22 and 23, but let me just show you a couple of examples.

Note that these are over-simplified translations:

* **살*거든*요** - "Just so you know, one lives."
* **살*잖아*요** - "You know one lives."

Back to 아/어.

So how do you know when to use 아 or 어?

It comes down to just two things: the vowel and 받침 of the last letter in the verb stem.

* Does it end in ㅏ and ㅗ or not?
* Does it end in a 받침 or not?

**When to add 아 - Rule**

So you use 아 if the vowel of the stem ends in ㅏ or ㅗ.

And when it ends in a 받침, you simply “ADD” it.

닫다, to close, conjugates to 닫아요

* 닫다 → 닫 + 아 → **닫아(요)**

Since **닫** has a 받침 and the last vowel is ㅏ, we add **아** or **아요** if you want to be polite.

So, **we get** **닫아 in 반말 and 닫아요 in polite speech.**

**Now we have the verb 놓다, which means “to put”.**

놓다→ 놓 + 아 → **놓아(요)**

놓 ends in ㅗ and has a 받침, although ㅎ is silent. So again we have to add “아요”

**So, we end up with 놓아요.**

Let’s see how this works in some sentences:

**When to add 아 - Sentences**

Alright, now let’s make some sentences using these verbs.

**나는 창문을 닫아(요)** — I close the window.

**저는 핸드폰을 놓아(요) –** I put the phone (down).

I’ve prepared a list of verbs that follow the same pattern with more example sentences. So, please download it use them for your sentence practice.

**When 아 cancels out or merges with the stem**

This happens when ㅏ and ㅗ end in a vowel, not 받침.

가다 means to go. And **가** ends in ㅏ.

So we add **아 but then we get 가아, which sounds redundant. So, we just go with 가.**

* 가다 → 가 + 아 → **가(요)**.

Now, let’s take a look at **보다** – "To see" or "To watch."

보 ends in ㅗ, so again we should use “아”.

But we have this vowel ㅘ which is ㅗ and ㅏ combined. So instead of **보아**, we say **봐(요)**.

* 보다 → 보 + 아 → **봐(요)**.

As you can see, the key is “phonetics”.

**When 아 cancels out or merges with the stem - Sentences**

Let’s look at some sentences.

저는 도서관에 가요 – I go to the library.

에 is a location marker. Just think of it as “to” for the time being. We will talk about it in detail in Level 2.

**저희는 영화를 봐요** – "We watch a movie"

Moving on.

**When to add 어 - Rule**

You use 어 when the stem DOESN’T end in ㅏ or ㅗ – Namely, for all other vowels.

**And just like before, you simply add 어 when the stem ends in a 받침.**

**So, 먹다 - to eat – becomes 먹어요**

* **먹다 (to eat) + 어 = 먹어(요)**

**지수는 피자를 먹어요** – Jisoo eats Pizza.

**When 어 cancels out or merges with the stem.**

This is where things get a little complicated. Let’s first look at when it cancels out.

**When 어 cancels out**

어 cancels out when the Stem Ends in ㅓ/ㅕ and ㅐ/ㅔ with no 받침.

Let’s look at 서다, to stand or to stop still, and 켜다, to turn on.

**서다 becomes 서 not 서어.**

**켜다 becomes 켜 not 켜어**

Same logic as before, redundant sound.

And when the stem ends in ㅐ and ㅔ, like 보내다, to send and 세다, to count, you CAN technically leave 어 intact and say 보내어요 and 세어요. But this sounds too poetic.

So, in spoken Korean, it’s always 보내요 not 보내어요 and 세요 not 세어요.

**You can think of it this way.**

If you look at the shape of **ㅐ** and **ㅔ,** they already have an **ㅓ** in them. So adding 어 would be “kind of” redundant in a way, alphabetically speaking.

**When 어 cancels out – Sentences**

Let’s look at some examples for each case.

**서다** to stand

차가 서요 – The car stops (still)

**켜다** to turn on

직원이 에어컨을 켜요 – The staff turns on the A/C.

**보내다**. *to send*

**선생님이 이메일을 보내요** – The teacher sends an email.

**세다** – *to count*.

**저는 날짜를 세요** – *I count the dates.*

Now, let’s talk about when 어 merges with the stem.

**When 어 merges with the stem.**

When the stem ends in ㅣ and ㅜ, and becomes ㅕ and ㅝ respectively.

If you said, 이어 quickly, you get 여 and if you say 우어 quickly, you get 워.

Let’s look at **고치다** – *to fix*, which ends in ㅣ.

When you say **고치어** quickly, it becomes **고쳐**. Again, the change is phonetic

Next, we have 배우다 to learn, with the stem ending in ㅜ.

When you say 배우**어** quickly, it becomes the **배워** sound.

So, **어** merges with the stem, and becomes **배워요**.

**When 어 merges with the stem – Sentences**

**남자가 핸드폰을 고쳐요** – *The man fixes the phone.*

**저는 한국어를 배워요** – *I learn Korean*.

**ㅡ Verbs**

If you remember from the 한글 lessons, I mentioned that ㅡ is a placeholder vowel.

So when the stem ends in in this letter, **either 아** or **어** takes over and just replaces the **ㅡ**.

For ㅡ verbs that have more than 2 syllables, the suffix that follows corresponds to the syllable right before ㅡ.

So 바쁘다, to be busy becomes 바빠요 because 바 ends in ㅏ

but 예쁘다, to be pretty, 예 doesn’t end in ㅏ or ㅗ so it becomes 예뻐요.

When the verb only have 2 syllables, it’s always 어 that takes over.

So, 끄다, to turn off, becomes **꺼(요)** (not **끄어**)

**ㅡ Verbs - Sentences**

**학생이 불을 꺼요** – *The student turns off the light.*

On a sidenote, 불 can mean fire and light.

Let’s move on to ‘하다’

**하다 – To do**

‘하다’ is arguably the most important verb in the entire Korean language. About 50% of Korean verbs all end in 하다. And they all conjugate the same way.

하다 on its own, means “to do” but it’s a super versatile word and it can have many different interpretations depending on the usage.

Like when 하다 is in a descriptive verb, it can be interpreted as “to be”. Note that it doesn’t mean “to be” literally. Let me make that clear. You can only TRANSLATE 하다 as “to be” for this specific case.

For example, 사랑하다 means “To DO love” and 친절하다 means “To BE kind”

Now, 하다 doesn’t follow the 아/어 rule that we just learned.

Instead, it takes **여** as its standard context marker. And note that 하다 is the ONLY verb that uses 여.

So we get 하여, but we almost always shorten it to 해 because **하여** is way too formal to use and and it’s mostly only used in writing.

**Why This Happens**

If you're wondering why this happens, my answer to that is: “You ABSOLUTELY don’t need to know why." This may not be the answer you wanted but I assure you, it is the one you NEED.

As an English speaker, do you know why the past tense for “go” is “went”, not “goed”?

And why is “K” silent in “know”?

Or do you know why the word “remove” doesn’t mean “to move again”?

And most importantly, does “why” even matter?

It’s perfectly okay and natural to want to know why. And in fact, I **encourage** you to stay curious and that’s why I try and explain why things are the way are as clearly and logically as possible.

But sometimes, you just need to accept things and move on because you’ll hinder your progress if you get too caught up on these meaningless details. You’re not here to become a linguist. You’re here to build your Korean language core.

So, you’re just going to have to memorize this one. Just remember **하다** becomes **해(요)**.

Here are some examples.

저는 여자친구를 사랑해요 – I love (my) girlfriend.

제 동생은 친절해요 – My younger sibling is kind.

**Noun + 하다 Action Verbs**

Most, if not all Sino-Korean action verbs have [Noun+하다] format like 운동하다 – To (do) exercise and 공부하다 – To (do) studying.

And when the word before 하다 is a NOUN in an action verb, you can separate it into object + 하다 by adding the object marker and a space between the noun and the 하다 part.

So:

[저는 운동해요 – I exercise] can become 저는 운동**을** 해요 – I do exercising

[저는 공부해요 – I study] can become 저는 공부**를** 해요 – I do studying

And it has exactly the same meaning, with a slight emphasis on WHAT it is you’re doing.

Now, look out for these two common mistakes.

Not all noun+하다 verbs are action verbs. Like I said, only action verbs can have objects, so you can’t do this with a descriptive verb.

For example, 친절 means kindness and 친절하다 is a descriptive verb that means “to be kind”. But you can’t say 친절을 하다 because you can’t “kind” something.

And also, many 하다 verbs are compound verbs which are basically two verbs combined like 좋아하다, to like which is 좋다 – to be good and 하다 combined.

But you can’t say 좋아를 하다 because a verb can never be an object.

So, this leads us to our next topic - Why is 아/어 important?

**Intro to compound verbs**

**아/어 suffix plays a very important role in Korean grammar.** We have a lot of compound verbs and they all follow this format.

**[Stem] + 아/어 + [Another verb]**.

We will talk about them in detail in level 3.

Let me show you “좋아하다” and “싫어하다” to like and to dislike.

So “좋아하다" is 좋다 and 하다 combined. 좋다 means “to be good” but here you can translate it as “To be likable”.

좋다 becomes 좋아 because 좋 has ㅗ and then there’s 하다 attached. Then all you have to do is conjugate 하다 so it becomes 좋아해요.

* 저는 동물을 좋아해요 – I like animals

And 싫어하다 comes from 싫다 which means “to be dislikable” or “not desirable” and 하다 combined. And it conjugates to 싫어해요.

* 저는 벌레를 싫어해요 – I don’t like bugs.

What matters for now is that you are aware of the concept.

**Why is 아/어 important? – Sentence connectors**

Conjunctions like “and”, “but” and “so” are separate words in English. But in Korean, they can attach at the ending of a verb to connect 2 sentences. **We’ll start with “and” and “but” in level 2.**

And there are many sentence connectors that come after **아/어**, like **서**, which you can translate as "so” for the time being. 서 and “so” sound kind of similar. So, you can remember it this way if it helps.

Here’s an example:

 **저는 케이팝을 좋아해서 한국어를 배워요** – *I like K-pop, so I learn Korean.*

 **한국어** = Korean (language), **배우다** = To learn

However, not all connectors attach after **아/어** Some connectors come directly after the verb stem like “~고”, which means “AND”.

 **저는 케이팝을 좋아하고 한국어를 배워요 – I like K-pop, AND I learn Korean**

So, there aren’t clear rules for the “RIGHT” place for the connectors, so you’ll need to learn each one individually.

**But as you just saw, 아/어 is like the cornerstone you need to understand and make longer and more advanced sentences. So, please make sure to review how to use 아/어 after the lesson.**

**I’m purposefully showing you these “advanced concepts” in advance, no pun intended, to warm you up to get you prepared for a smooth and seamless transition towards the intermediate level.**

**니다 - Formal speech Conjugation**

Alright, now let's talk about the formal speech, the ~니다 form. This is easier to make. The vowel in the stem doesn’t matter here. And it’s only 받침-sensitive.

* If the stem ends in with no 받침, you add **ㅂ니다**.

- So for 하다, you get 합니다.

* If the stem ends in a 받침, you add **습니다**.

- And 먹다 becomes 먹습니다.

That’s it. Way easier than the 아/어 rule.

And as for how pronunciation goes, you don’t have to enunciate them like 합.니.다.

We actually say it like 함니다 and 먹슴니다, which should be easier to pronounce for you. A little reminder on consonant assimilation.

**Don’t overuse the formal speech**

Now, you might be tempted to use the formal speech over informal speech since it’s easier to make, but it can make you sound unnatural and way too formal.

ㅂ/습니다 is actually its own context marker. The idea is that it already marks the formal context.

So, it’s grammatically incorrect to add another context marker like 잖아 and 거든 and this means you can't add emotions to your sentences in formal speech.

So, you’ll sound like a robot if you overuse it.

Now, when DO you use it then? Actually let me rephrase that question.

**Difference between 요 VS 니다**

When should you use the 요 form and when should you use the 니다 form? This is a very common question.

The answer is, even in formal situations, Koreans mix the two speech levels. Here’s how (Show a clip of Korean broadcast where they use 요 and 니다 both.)

So, I suggest you do the same.

The 요 form is much, much more common in real life. So, you’ll be good with 요 99% of the time.

And I’ve seen people adding 요 after 니다, probably because they learned that they should add 요 to everything to sound polite. I can see why they do this but well, don’t do this because it’s just wrong.

니다 is inherently polite on its own, so it’s technically incorrect to say something like “감사합니다요” though you might hear it occasionally. This is how servants and slaves used to talk to the nobles back in the day.

So, don’t say 니다요. And that's all there is to it!

Let’s look at some formal speech sentences.

**보내다** - To send

* **저는 아이를 학교에 보냅니다** – I send (my) child to school
* **아이**: Child / Kid
* **학교**: School
* **에**: Time/Location marker

**Formal high** takes over from leaf to fruit.
If the stem has no 받침, the ending is ㅂ니다.

**세다** - To count

* **직원이 돈을 셉니다** – The staff counts the money
* **직원**: Employee / Staff
* **돈**: Money

**Formal high** takes over from leaf to fruit.
If the stem has no 받침, the ending is ㅂ니다.

**쓰다** - To use

* **남동생이 화장실을 씁니다** – The younger brother uses the bathroom
* **남동생**: Younger brother
* **화장실**: Bathroom

**Formal high** takes over from leaf to fruit.
If the stem has no 받침, the ending is ㅂ니다.

**고치다** - To fix

* **아버지가 지붕을 고칩니다** – Father fixes the roof
* **지붕**: Roof

**Formal high** takes over from leaf to fruit.
If the stem has no 받침, the ending is ㅂ니다.

Now, let’s look at when the stem ends in 받침. In this case, the ending you add is **습니다**.
**먹다** – To eat

* **저는 아침을 먹습니다** – I eat breakfast

Note that 아침 can also mean morning.

**좋다** - To be good, to be nice

* **컴퓨터가 정말 좋습니다** – The computer is really good

Here, *좋다* (to be good) ends in a 받침, so the **습니다** form is used to make it polite.

**적다** - To be little, to write down

* **학생들이 번호를 적습니다** – The students write down the number
````

````md
### Basic Present Tense Conjugation
The instructor outlines three fundamental steps for conjugating a dictionary-form verb into the polite present tense. First, remove the dictionary ending 다 to find the verb stem. Second, determine whether to attach 아 or 어 based on the last vowel of the stem: use 아 if the last vowel is 아 or 오, and use 어 for all other vowels. Third, attach 요 to the end.

For example, the verb 있다 (to exist/to be) becomes 있어요. The stem is 있, and since the vowel is 이 (not 아 or 오), 어 is added followed by 요. Similarly, 안다 (to sit) has the vowel 아, so it becomes 앉아요. The instructor notes that while these rules are the foundation, they often involve further steps to make the words shorter or easier to say.

---

### Vowel Contractions and Shortening
When a verb stem ends in a vowel, it often combines with the added 아 or 어 to form a diphthong or a single shortened syllable. The instructor demonstrates this with 오다 (to come). The stem 오 ends in the vowel 오, so we add 아 to get 오아요. In natural speech, this blends into 와요. Similarly, 주다 (to give) becomes 주어요, which commonly contracts to 줘요.

If the verb stem already ends in the same vowel being added, one of the vowels is simply dropped to avoid repetition. For instance, 사다 (to buy) becomes 사요 rather than 사아요. Other common contractions include 이 + 어 becoming 여, such as 마시다 (to drink) becoming 마셔요 and 가르치다 (to teach) becoming 가르쳐요.

````

````md
### Special Verbs: 하다, 되다, 이다, and 아니다
Certain high-frequency verbs have unique forms that should be memorized. 하다 (to do) always becomes 해요. 되다 (to become) becomes 돼요. The copula 이다 (to be) changes based on whether the preceding word ends in a consonant (이에요) or a vowel (예요). The instructor notes that while written as 예요, it is often pronounced as 에요. 아니다 (to not be) is always 아니에요 regardless of the preceding sound.
````

#### The Dictionary Form and Verb Stem

- 원문 줄: 24~25

````md
### The Dictionary Form and Verb Stem
Every Korean verb is found in the dictionary in a form that ends with the syllable 다. Examples include 가다 (to go), 먹다 (to eat), 좋아하다 (to like), 보다 (to see), and 이다 (to be). To begin the conjugation process, one must identify the verb stem by removing the 다. For instance, the stem of 먹다 is 먹, and the stem of 가다 is 가. This stem is the base to which various grammatical endings are attached to express tense, mood, and social relationship.
````

#### Basic Rules for the 요 Form

- 원문 줄: 29~42

````md
### Basic Rules for the 요 Form
The instructor outlines three primary steps for conjugating into the present tense informal 요 form. First, identify the verb stem. Second, look at the last vowel in that stem. If the vowel is ㅏ or ㅗ, attach the syllable 아. If the stem ends in any other vowel, attach the syllable 어. Third, attach the politeness marker 요 to the end.

The instructor demonstrates this with the verb 앉다 (to sit down). The stem is 앉, which contains the vowel ㅏ. Following the rule, we attach 아 to get 앉아, and then add 요 to result in 앉아요. A complete sentence would be:
> **저는 앉아요.** / I sit down.

Another example is 먹다 (to eat). The stem is 먹, and since the vowel ㅓ is not ㅏ or ㅗ, we attach 어 to get 먹어, then add 요 for 먹어요.
> **저는 피자를 먹어요.** / I eat pizza.

For the verb 만들다 (to make), the stem is 만들. The vowel in the last syllable is ㅡ, so we attach 어 to get 만들어, then add 요 for 만들어요.
> **저는 케이크를 만들어요.** / I make cake.

---

````

#### Exception 1: Stems Ending in a Vowel (Contractions)

- 원문 줄: 43~58

````md
### Exception 1: Stems Ending in a Vowel (Contractions)
When a verb stem ends in a vowel with no final consonant (받침), the attached 아 or 어 will often combine or contract into the stem itself. This makes the word faster to say.

If the stem ends in ㅏ, such as 가다 (stem: 가), adding 아 results in 가아, which naturally contracts to just 가. Adding 요 gives 가요.
> **저는 가요.** / I go.

If the stem ends in ㅗ, such as 보다 (stem: 보), adding 아 results in 보아, which contracts into the single syllable 봐. Adding 요 gives 봐요.
> **저는 TV를 봐요.** / I watch TV.

If the stem ends in ㅣ, such as 마시다 (stem: 마시), adding 어 results in 마시어, which contracts to 마셔. Adding 요 gives 마셔요.
> **저는 차를 마셔요.** / I drink tea.

If the stem ends in ㅜ, such as 배우다 (stem: 배우), adding 어 results in 배우어, which contracts to 배워. Adding 요 gives 배워요.
> **저는 한국어를 배워요.** / I learn Korean.

````

#### Exception 2: Stems Ending in the Vowel ㅐ

- 원문 줄: 60~72

````md
### Exception 2: Stems Ending in the Vowel ㅐ
If a verb stem ends specifically in the vowel ㅐ, such as 보내다 (to send; stem: 보내), you simply add 요 directly to the stem. The instructor explains that adding an extra 어 would be redundant and long, so it is shortened to 보내요.
> **저는 이메일을 보내요.** / I send an email.

---

### Exception 3: The ㅡ Vowel Drop
When a verb stem ends in the vowel ㅡ and has no final consonant, the ㅡ is dropped before adding the ending. To decide whether to add 아 or 어, you must look at the vowel in the syllable immediately preceding the dropped ㅡ. If there is no preceding syllable, you default to adding 어.

For 쓰다 (to write/use), the stem is 쓰. Removing ㅡ leaves just the consonant ㅆ. Since there is no other syllable to look at, we add 어 to get 써, then add 요 for 써요.
> **저는 펜을 써요.** / I use a pen.

For 바쁘다 (to be busy), the stem is 바쁘. Removing the ㅡ leaves 바ㅃ. Looking at the preceding syllable 바, the vowel is ㅏ, so we attach 아 to get 바빠, then add 요 for 바빠요.
````

````md
### Exception 6: 하다 Verbs
````

````md
### The Dictionary Form and Action Verbs

In English, verbs in their base form are often preceded by the word "to," such as "to go" or "to eat." In Korean, every verb in its base or dictionary form ends with the syllable 다. This is a critical marker for learners; when searching for a word in a dictionary, if it ends in 다, it is almost certainly a verb. The instructor provides five essential examples of these base forms: 가다 (to go), 먹다 (to eat), 좋아하다 (to like), 보다 (to see), and 이다 (to be). Most of these are classified as "action verbs" because they describe a physical or mental activity. However, the instructor notes that 이다 (to be) is technically a "descriptive verb" because it does not describe an action, a distinction that becomes more important in later lessons.
````

#### Conjugation Basics and Subject Consistency

- 원문 줄: 30~35

````md
### Conjugation Basics and Subject Consistency

Conjugation refers to changing the form of a verb to reflect how it is used. In English, verbs change based on the subject—for instance, "I go" versus "he goes." Korean verbs are unique because they do not change based on the person performing the action. Whether the subject is "I," "he," "she," or "Billy," the verb conjugation remains identical. For example, the conjugated verb 수영해요 (swim/swims) is used for both 저는 수영해요 (I swim) and 빌리는 수영해요 (Billy swims). While Korean verbs do not change for the subject, they do conjugate for different tenses, such as the past tense 수영했어요 (swam), the future tense 수영할 거예요 (will swim), or the present progressive 수영하고 있어요 (am swimming).
````

#### What Is a Descriptive Verb?

- 원문 줄: 24~42

````md
### What Is a Descriptive Verb?

A descriptive verb is defined as any verb that is not an action verb. While action verbs like `하다 (to do)`, `가다 (to go)`, and `먹다 (to eat)` describe an activity, descriptive verbs describe a state or quality. The instructor notes that while these might be called "adjectives" in other languages, in Korean they are considered verbs because they end in `다` in their dictionary form and undergo conjugation. A key grammatical difference is that action verbs can take an object marked with `을/를`, whereas descriptive verbs describe the subject and do not take objects in the same way.

---

### Common Descriptive Verbs and Comparisons

The instructor provides several examples of descriptive verbs that learners may have already encountered, such as `이다 (to be)`, `있다 (to exist)`, `바쁘다 (to be busy)`, `맵다 (to be spicy)`, and `부럽다 (to be jealous)`. A specific distinction is made between the descriptive verb `좋다 (to be good)` and the action verb `좋아하다 (to like)`.

- **좋다** — Descriptive verb meaning "to be good."
  > **이 영화는 좋아요.** / This movie is good.
  > **그 아이디어는 좋아요.** / That idea is good.

- **좋아하다** — Action verb meaning "to like."
  > **저는 김치를 좋아해요.** / I like kimchi.
  > **저는 한국어를 많이 좋아해요.** / I like the Korean language a lot.
````

#### Basic Conjugation Rules

- 원문 줄: 24~36

````md
### Basic Conjugation Rules
The instructor outlines a three-step process for basic conjugation. First, remove the dictionary ending 다 from the verb or adjective. Second, check the last vowel of the remaining stem. If that vowel is ㅗ or ㅏ, add 아. If the vowel is anything else, add 어. Third, add the polite marker 요 to the end.

The use of 요 depends on the social context. It is required when speaking politely to strangers, elders, or those in higher positions. However, it can be omitted (leaving the word in 반말 or casual speech) when talking to close friends of the same age or younger, small children, and animals. The instructor demonstrates this with the verb 먹다 (to eat), which becomes 먹어 (casual) or 먹어요 (polite) because the vowel is ㅓ, and 좋다 (to be good), which becomes 좋아 (casual) or 좋아요 (polite) because the vowel is ㅗ.

---

### Vowel Contractions and Combinations
When a verb stem ends in a vowel without a bottom consonant (받침), the attached 아 or 어 often combines with the stem rather than staying separate. For example, 가다 (to go) technically conjugates to 가아, but because this sounds repetitive, it simplifies to just 가 (or 가요). Similarly, 오다 (to come) combines with 아 to become 와 (or 와요).

This rule also applies to stems ending in ㅜ or ㅣ. The verb 주다 (to give) combines with 어 to become 줘. Verbs ending in ㅣ, such as 마시다 (to drink) or 가르치다 (to teach), change the ㅣ to ㅕ when combined with 어, resulting in 마셔 and 가르쳐. These contractions are the standard way to speak and write in modern Korean.

---
````

#### What Is the 요 Form?

- 원문 줄: 24~30

````md
### What Is the 요 Form?
The instructor clarifies that the 요 form is not a single set of conjugation rules but rather refers to the specific 요 ending attached to a conjugated verb. Before a verb can be used in a sentence, it must be changed from its dictionary form, which always ends in 다. For example, the dictionary form 가다 (to go) becomes 가요 in the present tense 요 form. Similarly, 하다 (to do) becomes 해요, and 먹다 (to eat) becomes 먹어요.

It is important to understand that the 요 form is a subset of 존댓말 (polite speech). However, 존댓말 is a broad category that includes many other elements, such as honorific vocabulary and specific grammar markers. The 요 form can appear in various tenses and levels of politeness. For instance, the verb 하다 can be expressed as 해요 (present), 했어요 (past), or 할 거예요 (future). When combined with honorific markers, it can become even more polite, such as 하세요 (honorific present) or 하셨어요 (honorific past).
````

#### Conjugation Rules: Present Tense

- 원문 줄: 40~56

````md
### Conjugation Rules: Present Tense
The instructor provides a detailed breakdown of how to form the present tense 요 form. The first step is always to identify the verb stem by removing the 다 from the dictionary form.

If the last vowel of the verb stem is 아 or 오, you attach 아 followed by 요. Examples include 좋다 (to be good) becoming 좋아요 and 앉다 (to sit) becoming 앉아요. If the verb stem ends in any other vowel, you attach 어 followed by 요, such as 먹다 (to eat) becoming 먹어요.

When a verb stem ends in a vowel rather than a consonant, the added 아 or 어 will combine with the stem vowel into a single syllable. For example, 가다 becomes 가요 (가 + 아 combines), 보다 (to see) becomes 봐요 (보 + 아 combines), and 마시다 (to drink) becomes 마셔요 (시 + 어 combines). Verb stems ending in the vowel 에 simply attach 요 directly, such as 보내다 (to send) becoming 보내요.

There are several irregular patterns to note:
- **으 Irregular:** If the stem ends in 으, remove the 으 and look at the preceding syllable to determine whether to use 아 or 어. If there is only one syllable, use 어. Examples: 잠그다 (to lock) becomes 잠가요, 예쁘다 (to be pretty) becomes 예뻐요, and 쓰다 (to write/use) becomes 써요.
- **르 Irregular:** If the stem ends in 를, remove the 으, add an extra ㄹ to the bottom of the preceding syllable, and then apply the 아/어 rule based on that preceding syllable. For example, 모르다 (to not know) becomes 몰라요 and 부르다 (to call/sing) becomes 불러요.
- **ㅂ Irregular:** For most stems ending in ㅂ, remove the ㅂ, add 우, and then add 어요 (which combines to 워요). Examples: 맵다 (to be spicy) becomes 매워요 and 춥다 (to be cold) becomes 추워요.
- **하다 Verbs:** Any verb ending in 하다 always conjugates to 해요.
- **되다:** This verb conjugates to 돼요 (or sometimes 되어요 in more formal contexts).
- **이다 (To be):** This becomes 이에요 after a consonant and 예요 after a vowel.

---

````

#### Adjectives as Verbs: Basic Conjugation

- 원문 줄: 23~32

````md
### Adjectives as Verbs: Basic Conjugation

When an adjective functions as a verb, it follows the same general conjugation rules as Korean verbs. The instructor demonstrates this with the base form `슬프다 (to be sad)`. To say "Jeff is sad," the subject `제프 (Jeff)` is followed by the subject marker `가`, and the adjective is conjugated into the present tense `슬퍼요`. Similarly, for "The cat is small," the subject `고양이 (cat)` takes the marker `가`, and the adjective `작다 (to be small)` conjugates to `작아요`. Adjectives can be conjugated into present, past, or future tenses just like action verbs.

---

### Group 1: Stems ending in 아 or 오

The first group of conjugation follows the rule of adding `아` to the stem. This applies when the last vowel of the stem is `아` or `오`. For adjectives like `좋다 (to be good)`, which has an `오` vowel and a final consonant (받침), the stem becomes `좋아`. If the stem ends in the vowel `아` without a final consonant, such as `싸다 (to be cheap)` or `비싸다 (to be expensive)`, the `아` is deleted to avoid redundancy, resulting in `싸` and `비싸`. The instructor notes that while verbs like `오다 (to come)` or `보다 (to see)` combine `오+아` into `와`, there are very few adjectives that follow this specific contraction pattern.

````

#### Group 2: Stems ending in other vowels

- 원문 줄: 35~45

````md
### Group 2: Stems ending in other vowels

The second group adds `어` to the stem when the last vowel is anything other than `아` or `오`. Adjectives with a final consonant like `적다 (to be few/less)` become `적어`, `넓다 (to be wide)` become `넓어`, and `길다 (to be long)` become `길어`. If the stem ends in the vowel `이` without a final consonant, the `이` and `어` combine into `여`. Examples include `느리다 (to be slow)` becoming `느려`, `흐리다 (to be cloudy)` becoming `흐려`, and `시다 (to be sour)` becoming `셔`.

---

### Group 3: 하다 Adjectives

Adjectives ending in `하다` follow the simplest rule: `하` changes to `해`. The instructor provides examples such as `편하다 (to be comfortable)` becoming `편해` and `불편하다 (to be uncomfortable)` becoming `불편해`. She notes that the prefix `불` comes from a Chinese character meaning "not," effectively negating the adjective. Other examples include `뚱뚱하다 (to be overweight/fat)` becoming `뚱뚱해` and `날씬하다 (to be skinny)` becoming `날씬해`.
````

````md
### The Standard Conjugation Rule: Vowel Harmony
The primary rule for present tense conjugation depends on the last vowel of the verb stem. If the stem ends in the vowels 아 or 오, the ending 아 is added. For example, the stem of 알다 is 알, which contains the vowel 아, so it becomes 알아. If the stem ends in any vowel other than 아 or 오, the ending 어 is added. For example, the stem of 먹다 is 먹, which contains the vowel 어, so it becomes 먹어. To make these polite (존댓말), the particle 요 is simply added to the end: 알아요 and 먹어요.
````

````md
### Usage of the Present Tense
The instructor notes that the present tense in Korean is versatile. It is used to describe what someone is currently doing or what they do generally. It can also be used to make suggestions, offers, or commands, similar to the English imperative. Furthermore, the present tense can often indicate future intent or scheduled events, such as 내일 눈 와요 (It will snow tomorrow) or 너무 비싸면 안 사요 (If it's too expensive, I won't buy it).
````

#### Slide 8~10 — Conjugating 하다

- 원문 줄: 46~52

````md
### Slide 8~10 — Conjugating 하다
- **On screen:** Tree diagrams for 하다. 
    - Present: 하 + 여 = 해(요). Note: 하다 is the only verb taking 여 as a plain context marker; 해 is a shortened 하여.
    - Past: 하 + 였 + 어요 = 했어(요). Note: 했 is shortened 하였.
    - Future: 하 + ㄹ 거 + 예요 = 할 거예요. Note: 여 is NOT included in future tense; this is why it is 할, not 핼.
- **Ji's teaching:** Now, let’s talk about 하다. First, 해 is just a shortened form of 하여, and 여 works like the 아/어 suffixes. However, 하다 is the only verb that takes 여 as its plain context marker. That’s why 하다 in the present tense is 해요. The same rule applies to the past tense: 했 is just a shortened version of 하였, which is why it’s 했어요. Just like how we don’t add 어 in the future tense of 먹다, we don’t include 여 in the future tense of 하다 either. That’s why it should be 할 거예요, not 핼 거예요.

````

#### **1. Revisiting "하다" to do Verbs**

- 원문 줄: 27~30

````md
**1. Revisiting "하다" (to do) Verbs**
*   **Present Tense:** "하다" is unique because it takes the suffix "여" instead of "아/어". "하 + 여" contracts to "해", resulting in "해요".
*   **Past Tense:** Follows the same contraction: "하 + 였" becomes "했", resulting in "했어요".
*   **Future Tense:** Unlike the present/past, the "여" suffix is NOT used. You simply add "ㄹ/을 거예요" to the stem "하", resulting in "할 거예요". Ji corrected Amira's "요리헬 거예요" to "요리할 거예요".
````
