**Introduction to Speech level**

When you learn a new language, the very first thing you learn after the alphabet tends to be basic phrases like "hello," "bye," or "thank you".

But as we just learned about the "speech level" in the previous lesson, the way you say these in Korean **changes** depending on the relationship you have with the person you’re talking to.

The way you say "hi" to your best friend and your boss would be completely different.
And this means these “basic phrases” inherently contain grammar because we use different “endings” to show different levels of respect. So, this is the very first thing we’re going to learn.

**Terminology Simplified**

Before we get started, you might have noticed some inconsistency in the terms being used. Like they throw around words like "formal," "polite," and "honorifics" everywhere without really defining them clearly.

This can be confusing, so we’re going to standardize our terminology and clear things out once and for all.

Now, there are 7 speech levels in Korean. I know. It’s a lot.
But the good news is, you can completely forget about 3 of them because they are “old” Korean which you can only hear in historical TV-shows called “사극”.

Insert 사극 clips

하소서체
하게체
하오체

Kind of equivalent to Bible speak in English like "Thou shall not steal." And obviously, nobody talks like that in real life. And that leaves us with only 4 speech levels.

Show:

1. **Informal High** – Polite (먹어요)
2. **Informal Low** – Casual (먹어)
3. **Formal High** – Formal (먹습니다)
4. **Formal Low** – Plain (|먹는다)

**High** and **Low** indicate the level of respect toward the listener. **Note that low respect level does not mean disrespectful;** it's just a classification.

In Korean, High is 존댓말, meaning respectful speech and Low is 반말, meaning half speech literally.

And I’m going to be using the Korean term “존댓말” and “반말” more often than high or low. So I advise you to get familiar with them.

**The Plain Form (Formal Low)**

And at this point, you're probably wondering about the fourth one.

Like, "How can you be formal with low respect level?"

This speech level is mainly used **when you’re speaking to someone you don’t know personally. It’s commonly used in** books**,** advertisements, essays and news articles.

And it’s also the form you’d use when you’re talking to yourself. So as far as the respect level, it sounds really “plain” hence the name “plain form”.

So, it’s not as commonly used as the other three in conversations. But that’s not to say it isn’t EVER used in speech. And when used in speech, it’s considered 반말.

We’re not going to pay too much attention about the specific usage of the plain form, but just know that this will become more relevant later.

Let’s talk about the two types of 존댓말 first. The famous ~요 form and the ~니다 form.

**Polite Speech (~요 Form)**

The 요 form is called informal high because it’s not too formal but still polite like in “안녕하세요”, meaning “Hello”.
THIS is the one you’ll be using most of the time. It’s how you would speak to someone you meet for the first time or to a stranger like a restaurant server or a staff in a movie theater. We’re going to call this speech level the “polite” speech.

**Formal Speech (~니다 Form)**

Next, we have the formal high speech. If you’re a K-drama fan, you’ve probably noticed a lot of sentences ending in “니다” like in 감사합니다, which means “thank you” or “니까?” for questions like in “안녕하십니까?” which is “Hello” for formal situations.

This speech level is not as commonly used as the polite speech in our day-to-day life because it’s more suited for formal situations like a business presentation or the news.

Fun fact, we call this speech level 다나까 in slang, which literally means 다 or 까 because your sentence would ALWAYS end in 다 or 까 in this form.

And this is the only speech level you’re allowed to use to your superiors in the military and God forbid you ever say 요 by accident. They WILL make your life harder than it needs to be. But this probably doesn’t concern you so, let’s call this speech level the “formal” speech and move on.

**반말 (Casual Speech)**

Lastly, we have the casual speech. Grammatically, it’s exactly the same as the 요 form but just without the 요 at the end. This is why it’s called 반말 – Half speech, like half-completed speech. And this is how you would speak to people who are close to you, like your best friend.

The keyword here is “CLOSE”. This is very important to remember. Even if someone is younger than you, that doesn’t mean you get to talk to them in 반말 straight away; especially if you’ve met them for the first time. That would be very rude.

For example, I talk to my mother in 반말, who’s obviously a lot older than me but I would speak in 존댓말 to a high school student I meet for the first time, who is 20 years younger than me.

What we do in most cases is that we mutually agree to speak in 반말 with clear verbal consent – we have a phrase for that. “말을 놓다” which literally means “to drop speech”.

Again, the key is that you have to be “close” enough to that person to use 반말.

So, to keep things nice and simple, we’re going to call this the “casual” speech.

So, there we have it. We have 2 kinds of 존댓말, the polite speech and the formal speech and 반말 the casual speech.

**Honorific & Humble Speech**

There are two more ways to show respect in Korean.

First, we have honorific speech. I have seen so many people and resources misusing the term “honorific”. And this is not only confusing but also misleading for so many learners. So, I’m going to make this very clear.

**Honorific and Speech Level are NOT the same thing.**

Whereas you use different speech levels depending on who you’re talking TO, honorific speech is the person you’re talking ABOUT.

Let’s say you’re talking to your closest friend about your grandmother. The speech level you’d be using would of course be 반말, casual speech because it’s your best friend you’re **talking TO** but you’d need to use the **honorific speech** to address your grandmother because that’s who you’re **talking ABOUT**.

In Korean, this is called 높임말, which comes from the verb 높이다 “to elevate”. So, it elevates someone you’re talking about.

We’re going to cover in depth how to change a regular verb into an honorific verb in lesson #. But you might have heard many Korean sentence ending in 세요 like in 안녕하세요.

If you see a syllable that has a ‘ㅅ’ after the verb stem, it’s most likely an honorific verb.

We also have what’s called humble speech. While honorific speech is used to elevate someone, humble speech is about lowering yourself. It’s called 낮춤말 in Korean, coming from 낮추다 meaning to lower.

For example, there’s two ways to say “I” in Korean, 나 and the humble version 저.

And there are only a handful of words you need to know for humble speech, as they're mainly used in interactive actions like giving, asking, telling, or meeting. We’re going to learn more honorifics and humble speech in lesson 9.

**Summary**

To sum up: There are three ways to show respect in Korean—Speech level, honorific speech, and humble speech.

And as far as the speech level, there are four speech levels used in modern Korean: Informal High & Low, and Formal High & Low.

But of those four, three are most commonly used in actual conversations:

We have two kinds of 존댓말: The polite speech – or the 요 form and the formal speech – the 니다 form.

And we have the casual speech, or 반말, which is just the 요 form without the 요 at the end.

**Closure**

In this lesson, we have learned about different ways to show respect in Korean. We also clearly defined the terminology and went over the concepts.

With all this mind, in the next lesson, we’re going to learn about the two kinds of 존댓말 in depth.

An we'll look at the most basic responses like "yes" and "no," along with various greetings, each at different politeness levels.

We'll go through them one by one, step by step.

And while doing that, I’m also going to give you some tips on how to recognize patterns that comes with the change in different respect levels using these basic phrases so you can be better prepared to learn how to change them in the future understand which speech level to use.

We won't be diving into conjugation just yet—we’re going to start nice and slow and get yourself used to the patterns first.

And I will see you in the next lesson.

### When to Use Casual Speech (반말)

Casual speech is appropriate for people with whom you have a close relationship and where no formal respect is required. This primarily includes close friends who are the same age or younger, as well as romantic partners like boyfriends, girlfriends, or spouses. However, the instructor notes that age still plays a significant role; even with friends, if one person is older, the younger person might still use polite speech while the older person speaks casually.

Family members are another common group for casual speech, including parents, though this varies by individual family dynamics. Additionally, casual speech is used when talking to animals, small children (though some people choose to remain polite), and most importantly, when talking to yourself. The instructor points out that you never show extra respect to yourself in your own thoughts or when looking in the mirror. Finally, you can use casual speech with anyone who has explicitly given you permission to do so, regardless of age or rank, though it is often best to keep this "secret arrangement" private and revert to polite speech in public to avoid looking disrespectful to others.

### The Four Pillars of Korean Speech Levels

The way a speaker addresses someone in Korean is governed by social hierarchy. The instructor identifies four primary factors that dictate which speech level to use. First and most critical is age, specifically the year of birth (연 나이). While one might speak casually to those of the same age or younger, this is only permissible once a close relationship is established; speaking casually to a stranger just because they are younger is considered very rude. Second is the relationship: family members, spouses, close friends, co-workers, and bosses all require different nuances. Third is work position, where professional rank can override age differences. Finally, the situation matters; a private conversation allows for different rules than a public speech or an official announcement.

#### Informal Speech: The 요-Form

### Informal Speech: The 요-Form

Informal speech, commonly known as the 요-form, is the most frequent way verbs are conjugated in daily life. The instructor emphasizes that "informal" does not mean "impolite." Instead, the 요-form carries a soft and respectful feel. It is the standard polite choice for most situations, such as talking to older friends or acquaintances. However, the instructor notes a crucial nuance: the 요-form by itself shows respect to the person you are speaking to, but if you are talking *about* someone else (or asking a question to someone directly), you must often add honorific or humble speech elements to ensure the sentence is polite enough.

#### Formal Speech: The 니다-Form

- 원문 줄: 35~45

````md
### Formal Speech: The 니다-Form

Formal speech is characterized by the endings ~습니다 and ~ㅂ니다 for statements, and ~습니까 and ~ㅂ니까 for questions. Unlike the soft feel of the 요-form, formal speech carries a strong and respectful feel. This makes it the default choice for official announcements, business letters, military contexts, and first-time meetings with people you wish to impress, such as a new boss or parents-in-law.

The instructor demonstrates the conjugation mechanics on screen. To form a statement, one takes the verb stem (the verb minus 다). If the stem ends in a vowel, ~ㅂ니다 is attached; if it ends in a consonant, ~습니다 is attached. For stems ending in the consonant ㄹ, the ㄹ is dropped before attaching ~ㅂ니다.
- **하다 (to do)**: stem 하 ends in a vowel → 합니다 (statement) / 합니까? (question)
- **먹다 (to eat)**: stem 먹 ends in a consonant → 먹습니다 (statement) / 먹습니까? (question)
- **놀다 (to play/hang out)**: stem 놀 ends in ㄹ → ㄹ drops → 놉니다 (statement) / 놉니까? (question)

#### Social Hierarchy and Factors of Politeness

- 원문 줄: 23~29

````md
### Social Hierarchy and Factors of Politeness
Korean politeness is entirely dependent on social hierarchy. The instructor explains that unlike English, where you might speak similarly to a stranger and a celebrity, Korean requires constant adjustment based on your relative position to the listener. The first major factor is age; an older person generally occupies a higher social position. For example, a 15-year-old student must speak differently to a 35-year-old teacher than they would to a same-aged classmate.

The second factor is the specific relationship. While age matters, certain relationships like dating or family can override strict age-based rules. In a professional setting, the hierarchy is even more rigid. A customer is always viewed as "higher" than the service provider, and a client is higher than the contractor. The instructor introduces the terms 선배 (senior) and 후배 (junior) to describe workplace or school hierarchies based on experience rather than just chronological age. Finally, "permission" is a unique factor for learners; sometimes an older Korean friend may give a non-native speaker permission to speak casually to foster closeness, a transition that happens differently among native speakers.

#### The Three Major Speech Levels

- 원문 줄: 30~36

````md
### The Three Major Speech Levels
The instructor categorizes Korean speech into three main types. First is 반말, which is casual speech used with equals or those "below" you in the social hierarchy. Second is 존댓말, the broad category for polite or honorific speech used when "talking up" to someone. Third is the "Plain Form," a neutral style used in non-interactive media like newspapers, books, and journals.

The instructor highlights that 존댓말 is the ultimate safety net for learners. If you are unsure of someone's age or status, using polite speech is never wrong. While using 존댓말 with a very close friend might feel slightly awkward, it is far better than the alternative of using 반말 with a stranger or superior, which is considered deeply rude.

#### 존댓말: Polite and Honorific Speech

- 원문 줄: 44~50

````md
### 존댓말: Polite and Honorific Speech
존댓말 is the standard for most social interactions. It includes the common 요 form and the more formal 니다 form. A key feature of polite speech is the use of titles. You should never call a superior by their name alone; instead, you attach a title like 선생님 (teacher), 사장님 (boss), or the suffix 씨 (Mr./Ms.) to their name.

The instructor explains that the pronoun 저 (I) is actually a "humble" noun. By using 저, you are metaphorically lowering yourself to show respect to the listener. Within 존댓말, there is also 높임말 (honorifics) and 낮춤말 (humble speech). Honorifics involve changing the verb itself to show respect to the subject of the sentence—for example, using 계세요 instead of 있어요 when talking about a teacher's location. Humble speech involves using specific verbs to lower your own actions in relation to a superior.

#### The Plain Form and 당신

- 원문 줄: 51~56

````md
### The Plain Form and 당신
The Plain Form is unique because it is intended for situations where there is no specific audience, such as in writing. It is the standard for newspapers and essays. In this written context, the pronoun 나 (I) is used because there is no social hierarchy between the writer and an unknown reader.

The instructor gives a stern warning about the word 당신 (you). While often translated as "you," it is an element of the plain form used only when you have zero information about the person, such as in freeway advertisements or general public posters. Using 당신 in spoken conversation is often interpreted as a challenge or a sign of extreme anger. Finally, the instructor explains that the plain form can be used in speech as a type of "announcement" or "exclamation" among friends, such as shouting 춥다! (It's cold!) or 간다! (I'm going!).

Honorific speech is used to show honor or respect to the subject of the sentence. Crucially, the person being spoken about is not always the person being spoken to. #### Reframing Formality as "Extra Respect"

- 원문 줄: 28~34

````md
### Reframing Formality as "Extra Respect"
Rather than focusing on the word "formal," learners should think of `~니다` as "extra respectful." This level is appropriate whenever you want to sound particularly deferential to the person you are addressing. For example, when meeting someone for the first time who is significantly older, or when speaking to a professor or a high-ranking boss at a large company, `~니다` is the standard. 

However, the instructor notes that as relationships evolve, the speech level might shift. If you become close with a professor or work in a small office where you eat lunch with your boss, the "extra respectful" tone might become awkward, and a shift to standard polite speech (`~요` form) might occur. A key takeaway is that if sounding extra polite would feel socially "stiff" or "heavy" in a relaxed atmosphere, it is better to avoid the `~니다` form.

#### Fixed Expressions That Require ~니다

- 원문 줄: 40~49

````md
### Fixed Expressions That Require ~니다
The instructor highlights several common expressions where the `~니다` form is the "default" and using the `~요` form can actually sound insufficiently polite or even rude to a stranger.

- **감사합니다** `(Thank you)`: While `감사해요` exists, it is reserved for people you know well. To a stranger or in a formal setting, `감사합니다` is the expected standard.
- **반갑습니다** `(Nice to meet you)`: This is the standard introduction. Using `반가워요` with a superior like a boss would sound overly casual.
- **죄송합니다** `(I am sorry)`: This is the default apology in any polite situation. `죄송해요` is acceptable among friends or acquaintances, but `죄송합니다` carries the necessary weight for a formal apology.
- **잘 먹겠습니다** / **잘 먹었습니다** `(I will eat well / I ate well)`: These expressions of gratitude for a meal almost always use the `~니다` form unless speaking to a very close friend.

### Overview

#### Formal speech: concept and use

- 원문 줄: 25~29

````md
### Formal Speech (하십시오체)

Formal speech is characterized by endings like `~니다` and `~니까`. For example, the verb `하다 (to do)` becomes `합니다`, and `먹다 (to eat)` becomes `먹습니다`. This style is the standard for official announcements, public speeches, formal letters, and the military. It is also the safest choice when meeting authority figures for the first time, such as a new boss, teacher, or parents-in-law.

The instructor notes a common misconception that this form is only for "giving speeches." In reality, it is used in regular conversation to convey a strong, respectful feel toward the listener. It establishes a clear professional or social distance that signals high levels of respect.
````

#### The 요 Form: social feel and usage

- 원문 줄: 33~37

````md
### The 요 Form (해요체)

The `요` form is the most common politeness level in daily life. It includes any conjugation ending in `요`, as well as the polite "no," `아니요`. While it is generally "polite," its specific nuance is soft and polite rather than the "strong and respectful" feel of formal speech.

This style can be adjusted for even more softness or politeness. Adding endings like `~나요?` or `~가요?` when asking questions makes the sentence sound extra gentle. Because it is versatile, it is often the default for strangers or acquaintances where one wants to be polite without being overly stiff.

#### Casual speech: social meaning

#### Honorific and Humble Speech: concepts

- 원문 줄: 51~55

````md
Unlike the previous three levels which focus on the listener, Honorific and Humble speech focus on the person being talked about.

Honorific speech typically involves the `~(으)시다` ending, seen in forms like `하세요`. The instructor uses the visual metaphor of putting someone on a pedestal. By using honorifics, you are "raising up" the subject of the sentence because you look up to them or they deserve extra respect. This can be used even when talking to a close friend (in casual speech) if the person you are talking about is someone like a grandparent or a teacher.

Humble speech functions as the opposite side of the same coin. It involves specific verbs like `드리다 (to give - humble)` or `뵙다 (to see/meet - humble)`. Instead of raising the other person up, you are "lowering yourself" or "bowing down" in your speech. This effectively achieves the same result of looking up to the other person. Humble speech is generally used when the person receiving respect is the object of the verb (e.g., "I gave a gift to the teacher").

#### Plain Form: written and spoken use

- 원문 줄: 59~63

````md
### Plain Form (해라체)

The Plain Form is identified by verbs ending in `~다` in their dictionary-like conjugated state, such as `한다`, `먹는다`, or `춥다`. This form is unique because it is generally not directed at a specific audience. It is the standard for newspapers, journals, books, and general writing.

In spoken Korean, the Plain Form is used for "self-talk" or making general announcements to no one in particular. It can also be used among friends to get someone's attention or announce a fact casually. Unlike casual speech, it feels "plain" rather than "friendly."

#### Matching Pronouns: 저 vs 나

- 원문 줄: 42~48

````md
### Matching Pronouns: 저 vs 나
A critical part of using formal speech correctly is choosing the right word for "I" or "me." Korean has two main options: `저` (humble) and `나` (plain). `저` is used to lower oneself relative to the listener, making it the appropriate choice for polite and formal situations. `나` does not carry this humble nuance and is used when speaking to equals or subordinates in casual settings.

The instructor emphasizes that you must match the pronoun level with the verb ending level. Using `저는` with the formal ending `입니다` is correct because both are polite/humble. However, mixing them creates a mismatch. `나는 빌리입니다` sounds awkward because `나` is not humble while `입니다` is very formal. Similarly, `저는 빌리야` is incorrect because `저` is humble but the ending `~이야` is casual (반말).

#### Defining Politeness and Speech Levels

- 원문 줄: 24~30

````md
### Defining Politeness and Speech Levels
Politeness levels are essentially the way you speak to others, varying from more polite to less polite. The instructor provides several visual examples to illustrate this. A teacher might greet a young student with a casual `안녕 (Hi)`, while the student must reply with the polite `안녕하세요 (Hello)`. Similarly, a younger person might ask an older friend a question ending in `해요`, while the older friend replies with the casual `해`. In some cases, a sentence can even mix levels; for instance, telling a friend that your father bought a computer uses casual speech for the friend but honorific speech `사 주셨어` for the father.

Officially, the Korean language has seven distinct politeness levels, though only four are commonly used in modern daily life. The other three are primarily found in historical dramas (Sageuk), religious texts, or poetry. The instructor lists all seven: 하소서체 (very formal, used for kings), 하십시오체 (formal/official, also called 합쇼체), 하오체 (historical/older generation casual), 하게체 (historical/older generation friendly casual), 해라체 (plain/written style), 해요체 (standard informal polite), and 해체 (casual speech with close friends). The four modern essentials highlighted are 하십시오체, 해라체, 해요체, and 해체.

#### Key Terminology

- 원문 줄: 31~35

````md
### Key Terminology
To navigate Korean politeness, learners must understand five key terms. Informal speech, such as `해요`, shows respect toward the listener. Formal speech, like `합니다`, shows extra respect toward the listener and is common in official settings. Casual speech, such as `해`, does not show extra respect toward the listener and is used with close friends or those younger. Honorific speech, like `하세요`, shows extra respect toward the *subject* of the verb (the person you are talking about). Finally, humble speech, involving verbs like `드리다 (to give)` and `뵈다 (to see)`, shows extra respect toward the *object* of the verb (the person receiving the action).

### Why Politeness Levels Matter

The instructor addresses three misconceptions: first, that the `요` form is always "safe" (it can actually be too casual when speaking about a highly respected person); second, that natives don't care if beginners make mistakes (they are patient, but you will be seen as a perpetual beginner); and third, that you can use casual speech with all friends (in Korea, younger friends usually still speak politely to older friends).

#### 요 form and social usage

- 원문 줄: 27~38

````md
It is important to understand that the 요 form is a subset of 존댓말 (polite speech). However, 존댓말 is a broad category that includes many other elements, such as honorific vocabulary and specific grammar markers. The 요 form can appear in various tenses and levels of politeness. ### Social Usage: When to Use the 요 Form
The standard 요 form is considered polite and appropriate for most daily situations. It is the "safe" default for speaking with people you are not close with. Using the 요 form with close friends of the same age or younger can sound awkward and overly distant; in these cases, 반말 (casual speech) is preferred.

Conversely, the standard 요 form can sometimes sound too casual or even slightly impolite when speaking about or asking questions to someone who deserves extra respect, such as a teacher, a boss, or a complete stranger. In these instances, the instructor recommends adding honorific speech (시) to the 요 form to ensure proper respect is shown.

A unique dynamic exists among friends of different ages. Typically, the younger friend will use the 요 form to show respect to the older friend, while the older friend responds with casual speech. This arrangement is often comfortable for both parties. Beyond simple politeness, the 요 form carries a "soft" and respectful tone. Because of this nuance, even some couples in very close relationships occasionally choose to use the 요 form with each other, though this is less common than using casual speech.

### Defining Formal Speech and Common Phrases

Formal speech carries an "official" or "public" tone. Because of its high level of respect and social distance, it is typically not used with friends, family members, or regular acquaintances. However, several fixed expressions in Korean default to this formal level regardless of the specific relationship, simply because they are standard social formulas. ### When to Use Formal Speech

### 반말 vs 존댓말 — The Two Ways to Speak Korean
