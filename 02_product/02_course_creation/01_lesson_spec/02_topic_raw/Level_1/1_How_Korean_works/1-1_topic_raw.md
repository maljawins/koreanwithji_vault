````md
**How Korean Works: Korean Sentence Structure**

Congratulations on completing the Hangul course.

In Korea, there's a saying: “나무를 보지 말고 숲을 보라” - “Don't look at the trees—look at the forest."

which is exactly what we're going to do in this lesson - take a step back to understand the big picture before you start any kind of endeavor.

So, I'm going to show you the big picture here in this lesson and go over how Korean works as a language as a whole, before we get into the specifics.

**Standard Word order - STLOHV**

People think word order in Korean is just like English in reverse like Yoda from Starwars. But that's not entirely true.

The "standard" Korean word order goes like this. S T L O H V stands for Subject, Time, Location, Object, How, and Verb.

There’s actually a pretty clear logic to it.

The way it works is, it generally starts off with the big picture first, then moves toward the details – Kind of like zooming in from a wide view down to the specific.

In other words, you arrange the elements in the order of "what needs to exist first" for the next thing to happen.

Let's look at this example sentence together:

**나는 저녁에 집에서 치킨을 맛있게 먹어요**

[저녁에 집에서 치킨을 맛있게 먹고 있는 이미지 첨부]

I know this is a pretty long sentence, but this is just to show you the overall logic. And don’t worry because we’re going to start from short basic sentences and slowly work our way up in the main levels. So, this is just for your reference.

and I promise you things will make perfect sense in the end.

**나는 저녁에 집에서 치킨을 맛있게 먹어요**

Subject Time Location Object How Verb

**So, first, we have "나는"—"I."** Obviously, "I" exist, therefore I can speak about my experience.

**Next, we time and location.**

"저녁에" means "in the evening." "집에서" means "at home."

You need to think a bit scientifically here. Everyone says "time and space," not "space and time." So typically time comes before place.

**Then we have the object 치킨을** because the object exists WITHIN time and space.

**Lastly, we have "How" and "Verb."**

Korean is what’s called a “verb-final language”. So, the verb comes at the very end of a sentence and using our logic, whatever it is you “do” is obviously the most specific information, which explains why it comes last. And since the whole purpose of an adverb is to say HOW you do the action, it usually comes right before the verb.

So, there it is.

All you need to do is think in terms of: what needs to come first in order for the next thing to exist.

Set and settings first and then the specifics later. Like a funnel that goes from wide to narrow.

**Can you change the word order?**

Some people say Korean word order is flexible. But this is only half-true.

Yes, it IS more flexible than English. But that doesn’t mean you can just randomly start switching any words in a given sentence, mainly for two reasons.

First, it can make your sentence sound very unnatural.
The same goes for English. For example, "At home, I ate chicken" isn't “grammatically wrong”, but it just sounds awkward. So instead, you just say "I ate chicken at home".

Second, it can change the nuance of your sentence.

In English, you do this by verbally stressing certain words.

Let’s look at this sentence. "I'm not in love with her."

* + - 1. I’m NOT in love with her – means I’m absolutely not in love with her
      2. I’m not in LOVE with her - means I still like her, but just that I’m not in love.
      3. I’m not in love with HER - means I’m in love with somebody else.

Same sentence, totally different meaning. You get the idea.

In Korean, there are mainly 2 ways to do this.

And changing the word order is one of them. The other one is using “particles” which we’ll get to in a minute.

Now, when you change the word order in Korean, the difference is often so subtle that it’s nearly impossible to explain in words in most cases.

And I’m sure you can agree that some things are better learned through intuition rather than logic.
And this is a classic example.
So, you’re going to need a lot of input until you can pick up on these subtle changes.
And yes, I know it sounds confusing, which is why I have one tip to share to help you navigate this:

**The word that comes before the verb tends to be the most important information.**

Keyword - “Tends to be”. This is not an official grammar rule or anything like that but it is something I’ve noticed to be true in 99% of the time as a native speaker.

[Show 나는 집에서 **치킨을** 먹어요 - I eat **CHICKEN** at home]

In "나는 집에서 **치킨을** 먹어요," “치킨을”, the object, is placed right before the verb 먹어요, which means “chicken” is the most important piece of information. So, this could answer the question "**What** do you eat at home?"

But let’s look at this one [Show 나는 치킨을 **집에서** 먹어요 – I eat chicken AT HOME]

집에서 means “At home”. So now, it’s about **WHERE** you eat the chicken.

This is a little something to keep in mind when you’re watching a Korean TV show or listening to a K-pop song, try and understand the change in the feeling that come from these changes.

Until then, you can use the standard word order – STLOHV - as your foundation.

Let’s wrap it up before we move on.

There are two key take aways for you to remember about the Korean word order.

One, **S T L O H V and its logic: Going from wide to narrow like zooming in from afar.**

And two, the fact that Korean word order is not ENTIRELY flexible and that the word before the verb is typically the most important piece of information.

Alright. Let’s move on to the particles.

(highlight the particles in the example sentence)

**Particle 개념 설명**

나 저녁 집 치킨 I Evening Home Chicken. These are all nouns.

And these words [는 에 에서 을] that come after the nouns are called “particles”.

And what particles do is not limited to - but includes what prepositions do in English like “in”, “at” or “on”, etc.

But unlike English prepositions, Korean particles come AFTER the noun, which is why they’re also known as “**post**-positional particles”.

Some particles are also called “markers” because they literally “mark” the role of the noun.

Well, particles and markers; they do have their differences but it's too early for you to learn at this point.

Just know that what they do is practically the same. We're going to cover them in more detail in level 2.

They indicate the role of each noun and connect the words to make them into a single sentence. Now, I’m going to explain the particles in our example sentence very briefly just to show you how they work in general but note that each particle deserves a separate lesson of its own.

Also, some particles cannot be translated because they simply do not exist in English.

[는] 설명

는 is a topic marker because it marks the topic of the sentence. Again, this concept simply does not exist in English.

Simply put, it marks who or what the sentence is ABOUT. And here it’s marking 나, “I” as the topic of this sentence. I’m talking about ME.

It’s also very common to drop this part if the topic is clear from context. Once it’s been established, you don’t need to repeat “나는” in your next sentence unlike in English where you have to repeat I, I, I.

에 & 에서 These two are fairly straightforward.

에 here is marking the time like “In” the evening.

에서 is marking the location like “At” home.

**을 - Object Marker**

을 is an object marker. Also non-existent in English.

In case you're not familiar with the term "object": An object is the noun that receives the action of a verb. So, in "I watch a movie," "movie" would be the object.

And in Korean, we mark that using the object marker. So 을 is marking 치킨 as the object of the sentence.

As you can see, particles play an essential role in constructing Korean sentences.

And we’re going to learn them in depth in future lessons. So, you can look forward to it.

**Can we omit the particles?**

Now, here’s something many people say: You can omit particles.

For example, instead of "나는 치킨을 먹어요," you could say "나 치킨 먹어요" without the topic marker는 and or the object marker 을.

And there are times when the omitted version sounds more natural in spoken Korean.

But here's the problem: The meaning of the sentence can sometimes be ambiguous when you omit them.

So, when can you omit particles? Well, this is WAY too advanced to talk about at this point. You first need to MASTER the usage of the particles in order to understand when to omit them. So, when you're starting out, I recommend NOT omitting them.

**Adverb 설명**

As I mentioned briefly earlier, adverbs generally provide information on how an action is performed. So they almost always come right before the verb.

맛있게 comes from the adjective 맛있다—which means "delicious"—and changes form to become an adverb. Many Korean adverbs end in “게” or “히”. You could say that these affixes are somewhat equivalent to “ly” in English.

But personally, I recommend just memorizing adverbs as they are. Why?

**First**, there are other affixes besides 게 and 히—like 리 and 이. And there’s no clear-cut rule as to which affix to attach to which adjective.

**Second**, many adverbs are just standalone words. For example, 조금 means "a little" and 잘 means "well." There are tons of adverbs like these.

So, learn them separately and as a whole just like you would learn nouns.

**Verbs**

And finally, it’s time to talk about the verb, the most difficult part.

But I have good news for you: In Korean, verbs don't change based on the subject.

Here’s what I mean.

[Show: She studies Korean.]

For example, in English, the verb “study” becomes “studies” when the subject is a 3rd person.

In Korean, you don’t have to worry about this.

However, that’s not to say that Korean verbs don’t change at all.

**Dictionary form**

When you look up Korean verbs in a dictionary, you'll notice they all end with ~다.
Like 먹다—"to eat." This is called the dictionary form also known as the infinitive or the base form.

But you don't use this dictionary form as is in actual sentences.
You have to change it. We call this "conjugation."

So 먹어요 in our example sentence is the conjugated form of 먹다—"to eat"—in the regular present tense.
By the way, we'll cover conjugation in detail soon.
So, for now, just focus on understanding the overall concept.

Now, Korean verbs are divided into two main parts: Stem and Ending.

**Stem**

Once you remove 다 from the dictionary form 먹다, you're left with 먹. We call this the "verb stem." And the stem stays the same in most cases. Notice I said "most cases" because of course, every language has something called “irregular verbs”. But don’t worry about it for now.

So “먹” is our stem here.

**Ending**

And ending is where all the magic happens. And we have a LOT of endings in Korean. Tons of variations.

And I would argue that what dictates the level of your proficiency is how well you use the endings.

The ending can be divided into three main categories: Tense, Context, and Speech levels.

Since "Stem" is the official term, we’re going to call each part of the ending “branch, leaf, and fruit”.

Note that these are not official grammar terms. I created this plant analogy to help you learn visually.

So, if you google something like “Korean verb branch”, you’re not going to find anything.

**Branch**

[Show 먹었어요 – I ate]

The branch is where the tense goes.

“었” here means it’s past tense.

And if there's nothing in this spot, it means it's present tense. Just like we saw in 먹어요.

**Leaf**

Let’s come back to 먹**어**요 and look at 어.

Officially, this part is called "verb ending" or "sentence ending," But we just talked about how an “ending” consists of 3 different parts. It sounds vague, like "Which part of the ending are we talking about?"

So, we're not going to using the same term call this part "the context marker." because what goes in here literally marks the context of the conversation.

Let me show you an example.

어 in 먹어요 doesn’t have any context implication. It doesn’t mean anything, sort of like the "s" in "she eats."
I call this "the standard context marker."

But let's replace it with 잖아. Now, we have 먹잖아요.

[Show 먹잖아요]

“잖아” assumes the listener already knows—or at least SHOULD know what you're talking about.
So 먹잖아요 can loosely translate to "You know I eat".

To do this in English, you have to use an additional phrase “You know”.
And it just doesn’t quite feel the same because context markers let you express emotions and nuances in ways that English simply can't.

I mean, Korean is such an expressive language and this is what I love the most about my language.
And I can’t wait for you to experience this rich expressiveness of the Korean language for yourself.

Now, some context markers are specifically used for different types of sentences. And that leads us to our next topic - “Sentence types”.

**Sentence Types**

There are 4 sentence types in Korean: Statements, Questions, Suggestions, and Commands.

Some resources use fancier terms: Declarative, Inquisitive, Propositive, and Imperative.

I'm just telling you these terms for your information because you might need to know them when you're doing research, but they mean the same thing.

Now, since the sentence type inherently contains information about the context, this part usually falls under the "Leaf" category.

Let me just show you a few quick examples:

For questions
 - 먹지(요)? : You eat, right? / don’t you?
 > We use 지 to ask for confirmation.

For suggestions
 - 먹자 : Let’s eat (casual)
 > 자 is like “Let’s”

For commands
 - 먹어(라) : Eat (casual)
 > You can say ‘먹어’ but adding 라 makes it sound stronger.

We’ll properly learn about the context markers later on.

Moving on.

**Fruit – Speech level**

Finally, we have the “Fruit” which indicates the politeness level.

예시: 먹어**요**

Korean culture is highly **hierarchical**, and this is deeply reflected in the language. The way you speak changes depending on your relationship with the listener.

For example, how you speak to your best friend is different from how you speak to a stranger or your boss.

This is called "speech level".

It is broadly divided into **polite speech** (aka **존댓말**) and **casual speech** (**반말**). Here, **말** means "speech."

You would use casual speech - 반말 to people who are close to you, like your friends or your family.

And polite speech - **존댓말** for everyone else.
And within polite speech - **존댓말**, there are two types: **formal** and **informal** polite speech.

 **Formal polite speech** is typically used in **news broadcasts** or **business settings**.

- You probably know this phrase 감사합니다, which means “thank you”. The 니다 at the end indicates the formal speech.

 **Informal polite speech** is the most commonly used speech level. This is what you’d use in **stores** or between **strangers**. And the 요 at the end indicates this speech level.

- Show 먹어**요**

As we just saw, the speech level is reflected in the very last part of the ending. That's why I call this part the "fruit".

If you drop the 요 at the end, you get the casual speech, or in Korean, 반말 which literally means “half speech.” It feels “half complete” without the 요.

**Sentence connectors**

Verbs can also have something called a "sentence connector" attached to them. Sentence connectors are similar to conjunctions in English—like "and" and "but."

Here’s how it works:

먹**고** Eat, and

먹**는데** Eat, but

Whereas in English, you need to use a separate word like “and” or “but” interestingly in Korean, these conjunctions can quite literally replace the endings in the verb. And because of this, sentence connectors are also called "connective endings."

But personally, I prefer the term "sentence connector" because "connective" and "ending" contradict each other. As you progress through the course, you'll learn many sentence connectors.

**Closure**

So that's it. We've covered the overall concept of how Korean works.

When you start learning in Level 1, if you get confused about anything, come back to this lesson and review it.

In the next lesson, we'll talk about how to study vocabulary.
````

- 원문 줄: 24~33

````md
### The Basic Foundation: SOV Structure

The most fundamental rule of Korean sentence organization is SOV: Subject, Object, and then Verb. The subject is the person or thing performing an action or being described. The object is the noun affected by the verb. Verbs, which always come at the very end of the sentence, include actions like `먹다 (to eat)` or `가다 (to go)`. For example, the English sentence "I like Korea" is reorganized in Korean as "I Korea like." The instructor writes this on the board as:

> **저는 한국을 좋아해요.** / I like Korea.

In this example, `저 (I)` is the subject, `한국 (Korea)` is the object, and `좋아해요 (like)` is the verb. While this simple structure is easy to learn, the instructor notes that real conversations are rarely this brief and require additional elements to function correctly.
````

#### Adverbs and Adjectives: adverb placement

- 원문 줄: 42~44

````md
### Adverbs and Adjectives

Adverbs and adjectives have strict placement rules in Korean. Adverbs, which describe how an action is performed, must always come before the verb or verb phrase. For example, to say "to eat quickly," the adverb `빨리 (quickly)` precedes the verb `먹다 (to eat)`, resulting in `빨리 먹다`. If multiple adverbs are used, such as "very quickly," they stack before the verb: `아주 빨리 먹어요`.
````

#### Basic Word Order: SOV vs. SVO

- 원문 줄: 29~35

````md
### Basic Word Order: SOV vs. SVO
Korean follows a Subject-Object-Verb (SOV) structure, which is actually the most common sentence structure among world languages. This contrasts with English, which uses Subject-Verb-Object (SVO). The instructor uses a simple comparison: in English, one says "I eat pizza," but in Korean, the order is "I pizza eat."

While some claim that Korean word order is flexible because particles clarify the roles of words, the instructor warns that this is only partially true. Changing the order often changes the nuance or makes the sentence sound awkward. For example, while `집에서 저는 공부했어요 (At home I studied)` is grammatically possible, `저는 집에서 공부했어요 (I studied at home)` is the natural standard. Moving a verb to the beginning of a sentence, such as saying `공부했어요 집에서 저는`, sounds just as backward in Korean as "Studied at home I" does in English.
````

#### The Expanded Sentence Framework: T/S L N/O V

- 원문 줄: 36~45

````md
### The Expanded Sentence Framework: T/S L N/O V
To help learners organize more complex information, the instructor introduces a custom abbreviation: **T/S L N/O V**. This stands for Time, Subject, Location, Noun/Object, and Verb.

- **Time (T) and Subject (S):** These usually appear at the very beginning. One can say `오늘 제가 (Today I...)` or `제가 오늘 (I today...)`; both are natural.
- **Location (L):** This typically follows the time and subject.
- **Noun/Object (N/O):** Other nouns or the direct object of the verb come after the location.
- **Verb (V):** The verb always concludes the sentence.

The instructor demonstrates this with a full example:
> **저는 오늘 학교에서 한국어를 공부할 거예요.** / I will study Korean at school today.
````

#### Grammar and Structural Differences

- 원문 줄: 23~30

````md
### Grammar and Structural Differences

One of the most prominent features of spoken Korean is the frequent removal of particles. The instructor notes that topic markers `은/는`, subject markers `이/가`, and object markers `을/를` are often omitted in conversation as long as the meaning remains clear. However, they are essential in written Korean to avoid vagueness, especially in formal contexts like essays or speeches. Learners are cautioned not to skip learning these particles just because they hear natives omitting them, as failing to master them prevents the formation of proper complex sentences later on.

Word order also becomes more fluid in speech. While adverbs typically precede the verb directly in writing—such as `빨리 공부하다 (to study quickly)`—speakers might occasionally place the adverb after the verb if they think of it mid-sentence. This "afterthought" structure is rare in writing because writers have the opportunity to review and correct their sentences.
````

````md
### The Core SOV Structure
The most significant difference between English and Korean is the placement of the verb. English follows a Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) order, such as "I kicked the ball." In this example, "I" is the subject, "kicked" is the action, and "the ball" is the object receiving the action. Korean, however, is a Subject-Object-Verb (SOV) language. This means the same sentence would be organized as "I the ball kicked." The instructor emphasizes that verbs must always come at the very end of the sentence.

This rule applies consistently to objects. For example, the English sentence "I eat snacks" becomes:
> **저는 과자를 먹어요.** / I snacks eat.
````

#### Incorporating Locations

- 원문 줄: 31~37

````md
### Incorporating Locations
Just like objects, locations are placed before the verb in a Korean sentence. Whether the verb describes moving to a location or performing an action at a location, the place name precedes the final verb. The instructor provides two examples to show this consistency:
> **저는 학교에 가요.** / I to school go.
> **학교에서 공부해요.** / At school (I) study.
````

#### Adding Time Markers

- 원문 줄: 38~45

````md
### Adding Time Markers
Time information, such as "today" or "tomorrow," is typically added at the very beginning of a Korean sentence or phrase. While English often places time at the end (e.g., "I study at school today"), Korean prioritizes it early. It is also grammatically acceptable to place the time marker immediately after the subject; doing so maintains the same meaning but adds a slight emphasis to the time itself.

When dealing with specific times, Korean follows a "large to small" logic. You must state the largest unit of time first, followed by smaller units. For example, to say "Today, I'm going to school at 2:30 p.m.," the order is: Today + p.m. + Hour + Minute + Location + Verb.
> **오늘 오후 2시 30분에 학교에 가요.** / Today p.m. 2:30 at school to go.
````

#### What is a Sentence Connector?

- 원문 줄: 27~27

````md
A sentence connector is a piece of grammar used to join multiple independent sentences into a single, longer sentence. In English, these are words like "and," "but," "if," or "in order to." In Korean, these functions are performed by specific grammar suffixes or forms attached to verbs and adjectives. Common examples include **~고 (and)**, **~지만 (but)**, **~(으)려고 (in order to)**, and **~(으)면 (if)**. The instructor explains that mastering these connectors is the key to transitioning from a beginner level to a more natural, intermediate or advanced level of fluency.
````

#### Basic Korean Sentence Structure S.O.V.

- 원문 줄: 31~42

````md
### Basic Korean Sentence Structure (S.O.V.)

The instructor reviews the fundamental difference between English and Korean word order. While English follows a Subject-Verb-Object (S.V.O.) pattern, Korean follows a Subject-Object-Verb (S.O.V.) pattern. The verb must always come at the very end of the sentence.

> **저는 피자를 만들어요.** / I make pizza.

In this example, **저 (I)** is the subject, **피자 (pizza)** is the object, and **만들어요 (make)** is the verb. However, real-world Korean sentences are rarely this simple. They often include adjectives, adverbs, time markers, and locations. Billy demonstrates how adding a single adjective like **맛있는 (delicious)** changes the sentence:

> **저는 맛있는 피자를 만들어요.** / I make delicious pizza.
````

#### Detailed Sentence Formula; nuance and emphasis

- 원문 줄: 57~83

````md
### Detailed Sentence Formula and Word Order

For learners to understand where to place various parts of speech, the instructor provides a more detailed formula for standard Korean word order:

1.  **Time or Subject:** Usually the first part. If time includes multiple units (e.g., year, month, day, hour), it goes from the largest unit to the smallest.
2.  **Location:** Where the action happens.
3.  **Noun/Postpositional Noun:** People involved or spatial relationships (inside, behind, etc.).
4.  **Object:** The thing receiving the action.
5.  **Verb:** Always at the end.

Descriptions (adjectives for nouns and adverbs for verbs) always come immediately before the word they describe. For example, in the sentence **저는 내일 다른 도서관에서 한국어를 많이 공부할 거예요 (Tomorrow I'll study a lot of Korean at a different library)**, the adjective **다른 (different)** precedes **도서관 (library)**, and the adverb **많이 (a lot)** precedes the verb **공부할 거예요 (will study)**.

---

### Nuance and Emphasis through Reordering

While the S.O.V. structure is the standard, Korean is a relatively flexible language. Parts of a sentence can be moved around as long as the verb remains at the end. However, reordering words changes the nuance or emphasis of the sentence. The part of the sentence moved closer to the verb or placed in an unusual position tends to stand out more.

Comparison 1:
> **저는 도서관에서 한국어를 공부해요.** / I study Korean at the library. (Standard)
> **저는 한국어를 도서관에서 공부해요.** / At the library, I study Korean. (Emphasizes the location)

Comparison 2:
> **두 시부터 공부를 할 거예요.** / I'll study from two o'clock. (Standard)
> **공부를 두 시부터 할 거예요.** / From two o'clock, I'll study. (Emphasizes the time)

The instructor concludes by advising students not to stress over "perfect" structure initially, as the overall meaning usually remains clear even if the order is slightly non-standard. Reordering should eventually be done intentionally to convey specific nuances.
````

#### Word Order: Subject-Object-Verb

- 원문 줄: 23~29

````md
### Word Order: Subject-Object-Verb
The most fundamental rule of Korean grammar is the word order. In simple sentences consisting only of a subject and a verb, such as "I work" or "Mom cooks," the order is identical to English: Subject + Verb. However, as soon as an object is introduced, the structure changes. While English follows a Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) pattern (e.g., "I study Korean"), Korean follows a Subject-Object-Verb (SOV) pattern.

The instructor illustrates this by writing out the English sentence and then physically rearranging the components for the Korean equivalent. In Korean, the verb is always placed at the very end of the sentence. This means a sentence like "I study Korean" is structured as "I Korean study." This SOV structure is the foundation upon which all other grammar rules are built.

---

````

#### Verbs and the Concept of the Stem

- 원문 줄: 30~35

````md
### Verbs and the Concept of the Stem
Korean verbs are characterized by having a "base form" (or dictionary form) that always ends in the syllable **다**. Examples shown include **먹다** (to eat), **자다** (to sleep), and **마시다** (to drink). The instructor explains that these base forms cannot be used directly in a sentence; they must be conjugated to reflect tense and politeness levels.

To conjugate a verb, one must identify the "stem," which is everything in the base form except for the final **다**. For instance, the stem of **먹다** is **먹**. Conjugation involves changing or adding to this stem based on specific rules, such as whether the stem ends in a vowel or a consonant (**받침**). The instructor notes that while most verbs follow regular patterns, there are irregular verbs that require rote memorization.
````

#### Sentence Endings: Politeness and Formality

- 원문 줄: 37~45

````md
### Sentence Endings: Politeness and Formality
While the verb is the last "word" in a sentence, it is almost always combined with a sentence ending. These endings are crucial because they convey the relationship between the speaker and the listener. The instructor introduces the two primary categories: **반말** (informal/casual) and **존댓말** (formal/polite).

- **반말** is used with close friends, family members, or people younger than the speaker. In its plainest form, it often has no additional ending beyond the conjugated verb.
- **존댓말** is used with strangers, older people, or in professional settings. The most common polite ending is **-요**, which is why many Korean sentences end with this sound. A more formal version used in official settings is **-습니다**.

The instructor clarifies that "formal" in Korean doesn't just mean a black-tie event; it is the default way to show respect to anyone you aren't intimately close with.
````

#### Adverbs and Their Placement

- 원문 줄: 54~60

````md
### Adverbs and Their Placement
Adverbs provide additional information about verbs, adjectives, or entire sentences. Because the verb must always come last in a Korean sentence, adverbs are typically placed immediately before the verb. For example, in the sentence "I sleep peacefully," the Korean structure is "I peacefully sleep" (**저는 평화롭게 자요**).

Many adverbs are derived from adjectives by adding specific endings, similar to adding "-ly" in English. The instructor points out that learning adjectives and adverbs often goes hand-in-hand. Additionally, phrases that function like adverbs (such as "at school" or "in Korean") are also placed before the verb in the sentence structure.

---

````

#### Particles: Post-positions and Markers

- 원문 줄: 61~70

````md
### Particles: Post-positions and Markers
Particles are perhaps the most unique element of Korean grammar for English speakers. They are "post-positions," meaning they are attached to the end of a noun to indicate that noun's role in the sentence.

- **Subject Marking Particles:** **이/가** or **은/는** are attached to the subject.
- **Object Marking Particles:** **을/를** are attached to the object.

The instructor explains that these markers allow Korean speakers to identify the subject and object regardless of word order, though the SOV order remains the standard. While these particles are sometimes dropped in casual conversation, they are essential for clarity and natural-sounding speech. Other particles function like English prepositions, such as **-에서** (at/in) and **-로** (by/using).
````

#### Conjunctions and Sentence Merging

- 원문 줄: 71~74

````md
### Conjunctions and Sentence Merging
Conjunctions like "and" (**그리고**) and "but" (**하지만**) are used to connect sentences. However, Korean has a unique feature where these conjunctions can "merge" with the verb of the first sentence. Instead of having two separate sentences connected by a word, the verb ending itself changes to act as the connector. For example, the verb and the word for "but" can combine into a single conjugated form that carries the meaning of "but" while transitioning to the next clause.
````

#### 큰 그림에서 세부로 가는 어순

- 원문 줄: 23~29

````md
### The Big Picture to Details Rule
Korean sentences generally follow a logic of "zooming in" from a wide view to specifics. The instructor explains that we should arrange elements in the order of what needs to exist first. Scientifically, time is a broader concept than space, so time usually comes first. Within that time and place, people or objects must exist before an action can take place. This results in a general template: **Time → Place → People/Objects → Action**.

While this isn't a strict rule that must always be followed—as emphasis or context can shift elements—it serves as a reliable "rule of thumb" to prevent thoughts from getting tangled. For example, when describing a scene in a park, one should think of the park first, then the people, then the action of talking. Even if particles are missing, saying `공원 사람들 얘기해요` (Park people talk) is immediately understandable to native speakers because the word order is natural.
````

#### Language Philosophy and Strategy

- 원문 줄: 51~55

````md
### Language Philosophy and Strategy
*   **Contextual Nature:** Korean is highly contextual. Ji uses the example **사랑해** (I love you) to show that both the subject (I) and object (you) are often dropped because they are understood through context.
*   **Word Order Flexibility:** Unlike English, the order of S, T, L, O, and H can be rearranged to change the emphasis or "vibe" of the sentence, provided the verb remains at the end.
*   **The "Input-Process-Output" Method:** Ji advises against translating English thoughts into Korean. Instead, the student should take a Korean sentence (Input), decipher its logic using STLOHV (Process), and then create original sentences using that same structure (Output).
````

#### Ji의 동사 끝·줄기/가지/잎 비유

- 원문 줄: 27~35

````md
## Teaching Points

*   **Verb Position and Emphasis:** In Korean, the verb always comes at the very end of the sentence. The information placed immediately before the verb is typically the most important or emphasized part of the statement.
*   **Descriptive Verbs (Adjectives):** Adjectives in Korean are treated as verbs when they appear at the end of a sentence. For example, `조용하다` (to be quiet) is conjugated exactly like `공부하다` (to study). Ji labels these "descriptive verbs."
*   **Verb Anatomy (The Plant Analogy):**
    *   **Stem:** The base form of the verb after removing `다`. It remains unchanged during conjugation.
    *   **Branch (Tense/Honorifics):** Indicates when an action happened (e.g., `었/았` for past tense) or adds a layer of respect (`시/셔`).
    *   **Leaf (Context Markers/Sentence Types):** Adds nuance, emotion, or indicates the type of sentence (Declarative, Inquisitive, Propositive, Imperative). Examples include `~거든` (you know), `~잖아` (as you see/can't you see), and `~구나` (realization).
    *   **Fruit (Speech Level):** The final ending that determines the level of politeness (Casual `반말` vs. Polite `존댓말`).
````
