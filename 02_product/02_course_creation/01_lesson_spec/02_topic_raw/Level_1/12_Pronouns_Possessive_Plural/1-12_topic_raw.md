````md
**Introduction**

So far, we've learned how to say "I", and “this and that”.

And now it’s time to learn other pronouns.

But before we start: Pronouns in Korean do not change depending on whether they're used as a subject or an object—like "I" changes to "me" in English.

**I & My & Mine**

We learned that the standard version of "I" is 나, and 저 is the humble version.

Example: 나는 학생이야. / 저는 학생이에요.

So how do you say "my"?

Simple. You just add the possessive marker 의 after the pronoun. Think of it like apostrophe-S in English.

So you get 나의 and 저의.

의 is known to be one of the hardest sounds to make, but when it's used as a possessive marker, it's pronounced "에"—which is a lot easier.

But Koreans almost never say 나의 and 저의. We contract them to 내 and 제.

Examples:

이건 내 핸드폰이야.
저건 제 가방이에요.
그건 제 커피예요.

**"Mine"**

To say "mine," all you need to do is add 것.

But most of the time, 것 becomes 거—yes, that's the same word in 이거, 저거, and 그거.

When 거 comes after possessives, it's always pronounced "꺼."

So you get 내 거 (꺼) and 제 거 (꺼).

Example: 이건 내 거야. / 저건 제 거예요.

**We & Our & Ours**

"We" also has two versions: standard and humble.

우리 and 저희 for the humble version.

저희 is pronounced "저히."

Example: 우리는 학생이야. / 저희는 학생이에요.

You can also add the plural marker 들, which is equivalent to "s" or "es" in English, and say 우리들 and 저희들.

But 들 is optional, so 우리 and 저희 will do in most cases.

**Plural in Korean**

There something you need to know about plurals in Korean. Korean doesn't emphasize plural as much as English does.

For example, in English, you say "Cats are cute."

But in Korean, we just say 고양이는 귀여워요.

So unless you want to emphasize that there are multiple units, you don't need to attach 들 every time.

In fact, if you say 고양이들, it has a nuance similar to "THE cats" in English.

By the way, Korean doesn't have articles like "a" or "the."

**"Our" and "Ours"**

For "our," you can optionally attach 의 and say 우리의 and 저희의, but 우리 and 저희 are perfectly fine as is.

Example: 이 분은 우리(의) 엄마예요.

To say "ours," you just add 거.

Example: 이 그림은 우리 거야.

**"My" vs. "Our" in Korean Culture**

Now that we know how to say “my” and “our”, let’s talk about something Koreans do that confuses the hell out of non-Koreans.

우리/저희 남편 – “OUR” husband?

우리/저희 아내 – “OUR” wife?

First of all, no, we don’t share our spouses. What we really mean is “MY husband” and “MY wife”.

This is a very cultural thing. Korean culture tends to prioritize the group over the individual, and this really affects the way we use 우리.

The most classic example is 우리 나라 (our country). 제 나라 (my country) sounds really unnatural.

So we use 우리 for things or people that are part of a community—like home, company, school—or family members. For example, "my mom," "my brother," and "my dog" would be "our mom," "our brother," and "our dog."

And this is why we say 우리 남편 and 우리 아내 because husband and wife are a part of OUR family.

But this doesn’t happen until marriage. It’s MY girlfriend and MY boyfriend like 제 여자친구 or 제 남자친구.

So, 우리 and 저희 are for community and 내 and 제, on the other hand, are used for things that are more personal and exclusive.

**Your & Yours**

In the last lesson, we learned about 너—the casual "you."

"Your" in Korean is 너의, which is contracted to 네, but it's pronounced "니" to avoid confusion with 내.

Most 한글 is pronounced as written, but this is an exception to remember.

"Yours" is 네 거 (꺼).

Examples:

이거 네 물이야?
저거 네 거야?

**Plural "You" - Casual**

There are quite a few plural forms of 너—like "you guys" in English.

We have 너희(들) and 너네(들). They're interchangeable. But 너네 is more common than 너희 in spoken Korean. By the way 너희 is pronounced “너히”

You might also hear 니들, 니네(들), but these are only used informally.

The Plural Marker 들 is optional except for of course, 니들.

So 너희 and 너희들 are the same thing

**About 네 in 너네**

네 in 너네 is not "yes," but another plural suffix meaning "people of the same kind, class, or group."

While 들 can be used for all nouns like 연필들 - pencils, 네 can only come after pronouns.

네 can also function as a possessive marker instead of 의.

And 네 as the possessive marker has that same “your community” or “your group” type of feeling like

너네 가족 – Your family, 너네 집 – Your house, 너네 회사 – Your company

**Plural "You" - Polite**

In the last lesson, we learned about “title + 님”. So, you can simply attach 들 at the end—like 선생님들 (the teachers), 교수님들 (the professors).

But what if these people don’t have the same title?

You only have word to remember – 여러분(들).

Unlike singular **‘you,’ ‘여러분’ by itself is inherently polite and respectful.**

* **‘여러’** means **‘several’ or ‘multiple’**
* **‘분’** is the honorific form of **‘명’**, the counter for people.
* Together, they form **‘여러분’** to address **a group of people respectfully.**

**3rd person Pronouns in Korean**

Let’s talk about **3rd-person pronouns now.**

Pronouns in Korean are gender-neutral.

Because of this, most pronouns follow 이/저/그 + people format

Just like it’s rude to misgender people in English these days, in Korean, it’s crucial to **distinguish people from objects.** So, we should say “This person is my boss” instead of “This is my boss”

**3rd person (He, his & She, her)**

First things first, some textbooks will teach you that “he” is 그 and “she” is 그녀. But we never use these words in real life.

They’re only used in written texts, especially in a poetic way like in a song. Using them in speech will make you sound cringey. So, please don’t use them.

Use these instead.

**Casual Third Person**

If you're referring to a close friend, you use:

얘 (this person)
쟤 (that person)
걔 (that person)

**얘는 제 친구예요 / 이 펜은 쟤 거에요 / 거기는 걔네 집이에요.**

These come from 이, 저, 그 plus 아이, which means "kid.". But you can use 얘 쟤 걔 for adults too.

**Honorific Third Person**

For honorific, We simply add 분 after 이, 저, and 그. It’s the same 분 from 여러분.

So we get 이 분, 저 분, 그 분.

이 분은 제 선생님이에요.
저 분은 제 상사예요.
그 분은 제 교수님이에요.

**3rd person – plural (They, their)**

**"They" - Casual**

For casual "they," all you have to do is add the plural marker.

So we get:

얘들, 쟤들, 걔들

Or optionally: 얘네(들), 쟤네(들), 걔네(들)

Example: 얘들은 내 친구야. / 쟤네는 학생이야.

As you can see, you can use either 네 or 들, or both, to indicate plurality. The order is 네들, not 들네.

When you’re using 네, 들 is optional. 얘네(들) is slightly more commonly used than 얘들, by the way.

**"They" - Polite**

For polite "they," you can only add 들—not 네—after 분.

So it goes like: 이 분들, 저 분들, 그 분들.

Examples:

이 분들은 제 동료(들)입니다.
이 차는 저 분들 거야.
그 분들은 여러분(의) 손님(들)이에요.

Optional words are in parentheses.

Similarly, to say "their" you just add 의

and for "theirs," you add “의 거” or just “거”. So, 의 is optional for “theirs”.

**이들, 저들, 그들**

We also have 이들, 저들, and 그들. These are very neutral—neither casual nor polite. It’s sounds like “I don’t know them, they don’t know me and I don’t care.”

So, you can hear these words in formal settings like on the news or in a book, but they're not common in everyday speech.

이들은 한국 사람입니다.

**Wrap-Up**

That's it for pronouns!

In our next lesson, we're going to learn how to say "A is NOT B."
````

#### The Basic Plural Marker 들 / When to Use 들 with Nouns

- 원문 줄: 23~39

````md
### The Basic Plural Marker 들

The most basic way to make a noun plural in Korean is by attaching the particle 들 directly to the noun. Unlike English, which has complex pluralization rules (adding -s, changing vowels like mouse to mice, or having irregular forms like fish), Korean is consistent: you simply add 들. For example, 강아지 (dog) becomes 강아지들 (dogs), and 쥐 (mouse) becomes 쥐들 (mice).

However, the instructor emphasizes that 들 is usually optional. In Korean, plurality is typically understood through context. If you say 강아지 좋아해요 (I like dogs), the plural meaning is clear without the marker. Using 들 when the context already implies plurality—such as when using numbers or words like 많다 (to be many)—can actually sound awkward. For instance, saying 펜 열 개 (ten pens) is natural, while 펜들 열 개 sounds redundant. Similarly, 펜이 많아요 (There are many pens) is preferred over 펜들이 많아요.

---

### When to Use 들 with Nouns

While often unnecessary, 들 is used when a speaker wants to be explicitly clear that they are referring to multiple items, especially if the context might be vague. For example, in the sentence 이 도시에 편의점이 있어요 (There is a convenience store in this city), adding 들 to make it 편의점들이 있어요 clarifies that there are multiple stores.

Another common usage is when speaking about a category of things in general without caring about a specific number. The instructor notes that adding 들 in these cases can imply "all of those things" or "those things in general." For example, 편의점들은 (convenience stores in general) signals that the speaker is talking about the whole class of stores rather than one specific location.

A significant exception to the "optional" rule involves people. It is very common and natural to add 들 to nouns referring to people, such as 사람들 (people), 분들 (people - honorific), 선생님들 (teachers), 학생들 (students), and 친구들 (friends). While still not strictly required by grammar, it is the standard way to speak about groups of people.

---
````

#### Advanced Usage: 들 After Adverbs and Phrases

- 원문 줄: 41~45

````md
### Advanced Usage: 들 After Adverbs and Phrases

In spoken Korean, 들 can be attached to adverbs or even entire phrases. This is an intermediate-level concept and is strictly for speech, not formal writing. When 들 is used this way, it indicates that the subject of the sentence is plural, even if that subject isn't explicitly stated.

The instructor demonstrates this with the greeting 안녕하세요 (hello). By saying 안녕하세요들, the speaker is signaling that the greeting is addressed to everyone in the room individually. It carries the nuance of "Hello to each of you." This can also be inserted into the middle of the phrase: 안녕들 하세요. Other examples include 안녕히 가세요들 (Goodbye to each of you) and 잘들 했어요 (Good job, everyone). In these cases, 들 acts as a "pluralizer" for the action or expression, pointing it toward multiple recipients.
````

#### The Collective Marker 네 / Casual Plurals with 네

- 원문 줄: 49~63

````md
### The Collective Marker 네

The particle 네 is used after a person's name or a pronoun to indicate a group of people associated with that person, typically their family, group, or organization. For example, 철수 네 means "Chulsu and his family" or "Chulsu and his group."

This particle often functions similarly to the possessive marker 의 (pronounced 에), but with a collective nuance. While 철수의 집 means "Chulsu's house" (implying he owns it), 철수 네 집 means "Chulsu's family's house" or "the house where Chulsu and his family live." This is particularly common when referring to buildings or entities where the person is a member rather than the sole owner, such as 마크 네 회사 (Mark's company/the company Mark works at) or 철수 네 학교 (Chulsu's school). It is also used for family pets, like 철수 네 고양이 (Chulsu's family cat).

---

### Casual Plurals with 네

The instructor notes several casual and emphatic forms using 네. The word 너네 is a very casual way to say "you guys" or "you and your group/family." While 너희 is the standard plural "you," 너네 is common in informal speech. To add emphasis, speakers might say 너네들, which essentially means "all of you guys."

Similarly, the demonstrative pronouns 얘 (this person), 걔 (that person), and 쟤 (that person over there) can combine with 네 to form 얘네, 걔네, and 쟤네. These mean "this group," "that group," and "that group over there." Like 너네, these are very casual and should not be used with strangers or in polite situations. They can also take the 들 marker (e.g., 얘네들) for extra emphasis on the plurality of the group.

---
````

#### The Basic Possessive Marker 의 / Pronunciation and Omission

- 원문 줄: 23~35

````md
### The Basic Possessive Marker 의

The possessive marker is created by taking any noun and attaching the particle 의. This particle functions similarly to the apostrophe-s ('s) or the word "of" in English. Its primary purpose is to connect two nouns where the first noun possesses or is related to the second noun. For example, the instructor writes on the board that "Seoul's weather" or "the weather of Seoul" is expressed by placing the possessive marker between the two nouns: `서울의 날씨`. Another example provided is `철수의 가방`, meaning "Chulsoo's bag" or "the bag of Chulsoo." The instructor emphasizes that "possession" in this context is broad; it can mean literal ownership, like a bag, or a relationship, such as `철수의 친구` (Chulsoo's friend), where Chulsoo does not "own" the friend but is associated with them.

---

### Pronunciation and Omission

While the syllable is written as 의, it has a special pronunciation rule when used as a possessive marker. In this specific grammatical role, it is most commonly pronounced as 에. The instructor demonstrates this by saying `철수의` as "Chulsoo-e." He notes that while pronouncing it as "ui" is technically possible, it is much less common in natural speech. 

In everyday conversation, the marker 의 is frequently removed entirely if the relationship between the two nouns is clear and not vague. This often happens when creating compound nouns. For instance, `김치의 맛` (the flavor of kimchi) can simply become `김치 맛` (kimchi flavor). Similarly, "Korean class" is usually expressed as `한국어 수업` rather than `한국어의 수업`. The rule of thumb is that if the words sound natural together without "of" or an apostrophe-s in English, the marker can likely be omitted in Korean.

---
````

#### Pronoun Contractions and "Mine"

- 원문 줄: 37~49

````md
### Pronoun Contractions and "Mine"

When the possessive marker 의 is attached to the first-person and second-person pronouns, they often contract into shorter forms for easier speech. The instructor lists these transformations on the board:
- `저의` (my - formal) becomes `제`
- `나의` (my - casual) becomes `내`
- `너의` (your - casual) becomes `네`

A specific note is made regarding the pronunciation of `네` (your). To distinguish it from `내` (my), which sounds very similar, Koreans often pronounce `네` as 니. 

To express that something is "mine," "yours," or "his/hers," the contracted pronoun is combined with the word `것` (thing), often shortened to `거` in speech. The instructor points out a pronunciation change: when `제`, `내`, or `니` are followed by `것`, the `ㄱ` sound becomes a double consonant `ㄲ`. Thus, `제 것` is pronounced as "je-kkeot," `내 것` as "nae-kkeot," and `니 것` as "ni-kkeot."
````

#### The Concept of "Our" 우리

- 원문 줄: 50~57

````md
### The Concept of "Our" (우리)

In Korean, the word for "we" or "our" is `우리` (casual) or `저희` (formal/humble). Unlike other pronouns, it is extremely rare to add the possessive marker 의 after these words; `우리` already functions as "our" on its own. 

The instructor highlights a cultural nuance: Koreans use `우리` instead of "my" when referring to things that are shared or part of a collective identity. This includes family members, schools, companies, and homes. For example, instead of saying `제 아빠` (my dad), it is much more natural to say `우리 아빠` (our dad). This applies to `우리 학교` (our school), `우리 집` (our house), and `우리 회사` (our company). For pets, one can say `우리 강아지` (our dog) or `우리 집 강아지` (our house's dog).
````

#### Forming Plurals with 들

- 원문 줄: 24~28

````md
### Forming Plurals with 들
To make any noun plural in Korean, you simply attach the suffix 들 to the end of the word. Unlike English, there are no spelling changes or irregular forms to memorize. The instructor provides several examples of this transformation: 고양이 (cat) becomes 고양이들 (cats), 강아지 (dog) becomes 강아지들 (dogs), 집 (house) becomes 집들 (houses), 노래 (song) becomes 노래들 (songs), 도시 (city) becomes 도시들 (cities), and 아이디어 (idea) becomes 아이디어들 (ideas). This rule applies universally to all nouns regardless of whether they end in a vowel or a consonant.
````

#### Context and Optionality

- 원문 줄: 29~33

````md
### Context and Optionality
The instructor emphasizes that most of the time, it is not necessary to use plural nouns in Korean because the quantity is understood from the context. For example, in the sentence 저는 고양이를 좋아해요 (I like cats), it is unnecessary to say 고양이들 because the listener naturally understands you are talking about the category of cats in general. Similarly, 그 쿠키가 맛있어요 can mean either "That cookie is delicious" or "Those cookies are delicious" depending on the situation. The plural marker 들 is generally reserved for situations where it is strictly necessary for clarity.
````

#### When Plurals Are Necessary: General Statements

- 원문 줄: 34~38

````md
### When Plurals Are Necessary: General Statements
One specific situation where plural nouns are required is when talking about a noun in general or as a whole category. The instructor compares two sentences to illustrate this. The sentence 강아지는 똑똑해요 (Dogs are smart / The dog is smart) can be vague; a listener might wonder if you are talking about a specific dog or all dogs. To clarify that you mean dogs as a general species, you should use the plural form: 강아지들은 똑똑해요 (Dogs are smart / As for dogs in general, they are smart). Using 들 in this context removes the ambiguity and signals that the statement applies to the entire group.

---

````

#### When Plurals Are Used: Clarification / Note on Articles

- 원문 줄: 39~47

````md
### When Plurals Are Used: Clarification
Plural nouns are also used when a speaker wants to explicitly clarify that there is more than one of something. The instructor uses the example 집이 있어요 (There is a house / There are houses). If this is said in the context of a city, 도시에 집이 있어요, it is grammatically fine. However, if the speaker wants to be completely clear that they are referring to many houses rather than just one, they can say 도시에 집들이 있어요 (There are houses in the city). This explicit use of 들 ensures the plural meaning is captured without any doubt.

---

### Note on Articles
The instructor concludes with a note on articles, explaining that Korean does not have equivalents for the English words "a," "an," or "the." A single Korean noun can represent any of these depending on the context. For instance, 저도 쿠키를 먹고 싶어요 can mean "I also want to eat a cookie" or "I also want to eat the cookie." While speakers can specify "one apple" using numbers (which are covered in a later lesson), there is no need to insert articles into every sentence as one does in English.
````

#### Function and Usage of 의

````md
### Function and Usage of 의
The particle 의 connects two nouns together to show that the first noun possesses or is related to the second noun. It attaches directly to the end of a noun without any spaces. The instructor notes that 의 cannot stand alone; it must always follow a noun to be meaningful. The basic formula shown on screen is **Noun + 의**. This structure allows speakers to create possessive forms similar to "Billy's book" or "my homework."
````

#### Pronunciation Rules for the Particle 의

- 원문 줄: 27~31

````md
### Pronunciation Rules for the Particle 의
A critical point of the lesson is the pronunciation of 의. When 의 is a regular syllable within a word (like in the word 의사 for doctor), it is pronounced as 의. However, when it functions as the possessive particle, it is most commonly pronounced as 에. While it is technically possible to pronounce it as 의 even when it is a particle, the instructor explains that this is much less common and can be difficult to say quickly in conversation. Therefore, a phrase like 빌리의 책 (Billy's book) is actually pronounced as 빌리에 책.
````

#### The Standard Possessive Marker 의

- 원문 줄: 23~27

````md
### The Standard Possessive Marker 의
The instructor starts by reviewing the marker 의, which is typically pronounced as 에 when used as a particle. This marker functions similarly to the apostrophe-s ('s) in English, indicating that one noun possesses another. For example, in the phrase `철수의 집 (철수's house)`, the house is possessed by the individual named 철수. This marker is versatile and can be used with any person, animal, or object to show simple ownership.
````

#### Defining the Group Possessive Marker 네

- 원문 줄: 28~37

````md
### Defining the Group Possessive Marker 네
The particle 네 is defined as a group possessive marker. It literally refers to "people of the same kind" or "people of the same class" as the noun it follows. In this sense, it acts as a pluralizer for people, similar to the particle 들, but with a specific focus on a shared group identity. When you say `철수 네`, you are implying "철수 and those like him," which typically refers to 철수's family.

The instructor highlights the distinction between three markers:
- **의**: Shows simple ownership by anyone or anything.
- **들**: Simply indicates that a noun is plural (there are multiple of them).
- **네**: Specifically shows that a group, organization, or family owns something, or that the item is a part of that group's collective identity.
````

#### Grammatical Restrictions and Pronoun Usage

- 원문 줄: 47~60

````md
### Grammatical Restrictions and Pronoun Usage
There is a strict rule regarding first-person pronouns: do not use 네 with `저` or `나`. You should never say `저 네` or `나 네`. This is because Korean already has a dedicated word for "we/our," which is `우리`. To say "my school" or "my house" in a group sense, you use `우리 학교` or `우리 집`. For pets, the most common expression is `우리 집 고양이 (our house cat)`.

However, 네 is frequently used with the casual second-person pronoun `너 (you)`. The expression `너네` means "your family" or "your group." The instructor cautions that because `너` is casual (반말), `너네` should only be used in informal situations with people you know well.

Finally, 네 is used with the shortened demonstrative pronouns for people:
- **얘네**: These people (plural of 얘).
- **걔네**: Those people (plural of 걔).
- **쟤네**: Those people over there (plural of 쟤).

In these cases, the marker serves to turn an individual reference into a group reference, reinforcing the idea of a collective "they" or "these guys."
````

#### The Possessive Marker 의 and Basic Forms of "My"

- 원문 줄: 22~32

````md
### The Possessive Marker 의 and Basic Forms of "My"
To say "my" in Korean, one typically starts with the pronoun for "I" or "me" and attaches the possessive marker **의**. The choice of pronoun depends on the level of politeness required. For close friends, peers, or those younger than the speaker, the casual pronoun **나** is used. In all other polite or formal situations, the humble pronoun **저** is used. When the possessive marker is attached, it creates the forms **나의 (casual my)** and **저의 (polite/humble my)**.

The instructor notes a specific pronunciation rule: while **의** is usually pronounced as "ui," it is more often pronounced as "e" (에) when used as a possessive marker. On-screen examples demonstrate these forms in full sentences:
> **저의 손은 깨끗해요.** / My hands are clean. (Polite)
> **나의 손은 깨끗해.** / My hands are clean. (Casual)

In natural, everyday conversation, these forms are almost always shortened. **저의** becomes **제**, and **나의** becomes **내**. These shortened versions are much more common than the full forms.
````

#### Using 우리 Our Instead of "My"

- 원문 줄: 33~44

````md
### Using 우리 (Our) Instead of "My"
A critical nuance in Korean is that **저의/제** and **나의/내** are strictly reserved for things the speaker actually owns as personal property, such as a hand, a book, or a wallet. Using these words for people—like family members—sounds unnatural and implies a sense of literal ownership that does not exist in Korean culture.

Instead, Koreans use the word **우리**, which means "we" or "our," to mean "my" when referring to people or shared entities. This reflects a collective mindset. For example, when talking about a younger sibling, one should say **우리 동생** rather than **저의 동생**. The instructor provides an example sentence:
> **우리 동생은 귀엽고 똑똑해요.** / My younger sibling is cute and smart.

This rule also extends to pets. Even though a pet might be considered personal property in a legal sense, Koreans treat them as family members and use **우리**. To emphasize that a pet belongs to the entire household, the expression **우리 집 (our house)** is frequently used before the animal's name.
> **우리 집 고양이가 맨날 쓰레기를 먹어요.** / My cat eats trash every day.
> **우리 집 원숭이 이름은 찰스예요.** / My monkey's name is Charles.
````

#### The Humble Form 저희

- 원문 줄: 45~52

````md
### The Humble Form 저희
Just as there are polite and casual versions of "I," there is a humble version of "our." The word **저희** is the humble/polite equivalent of **우리**. It is used when speaking to someone to whom you need to show respect, such as an elder or a superior, to refer to your own group or family.
> **저희 아버지도 거기서 일하고 계세요.** / Our (my) father is working there, too.

The instructor concludes by emphasizing that choosing between **우리** and **저희** follows the same general politeness rules as other Korean pronouns, ensuring the speaker remains appropriately humble in formal contexts.
````

````md
The instructor highlights how the possessive marker frequently contracts when used with common pronouns. These contractions are essential for natural conversation.
````

#### Dictionary Pronouns vs. Spoken Alternatives / Informal Third-Person Pronouns

- 원문 줄: 24~39

````md
---

### Dictionary Pronouns vs. Spoken Alternatives
The instructor notes that while a dictionary will list `그 (he)`, `그녀 (she)`, and `그들 (they)`, these terms are almost never used in spoken Korean. Using them in conversation can sound overly serious, dramatic, or "too romantic," like lyrics in a song or lines in a novel. Instead, spoken Korean uses combinations of demonstrative modifiers and nouns. The core modifiers are `이 (this)`, `그 (that/the - out of sight)`, and `저 (that - over there/within sight)`.

---

### Informal Third-Person Pronouns (반말)
For casual situations, the modifier is combined with `애 (child/kid/person)`. These forms are often abbreviated for ease of speech.
- **이 애** becomes **얘** (this person/he/she nearby)
- **그 애** becomes **걔** (that person/he/she mentioned/out of sight)
- **저 애** becomes **쟤** (that person/he/she over there)

The instructor emphasizes that `걔` is the most common way to say "he" or "she" when referring to a peer or someone younger who has already been mentioned in the conversation. An example of this is the K-pop song title `제가 개야`, which translates to "That person over there is the one [we talked about]."
````

````md
### Plural Pronouns (They)
To make these pronouns plural, the particle `들` is added to the end of the singular form.

- **Polite:** `이분들`, `그분들`, `저분들`
````

- 원문 줄: 60~67

````md
### Flexibility and Cultural Nuance
The instructor explains that Korean is very flexible with third-person references. Beyond standard pronouns, speakers often use specific titles or descriptions as pronouns.
````

````md
## Teaching Points
*   **Pronoun Variety and Usage:** Korean pronouns are categorized by both formality (Low vs. Honorific) and usage (Proper vs. Real Life). Words like 니 (informal 'you') are common in speech but avoided in writing.
*   **Plurality with 들:** The marker 들 indicates plurality (like 's' in English) but is often dropped when the context or the pronoun itself (e.g., 너희, 여러분) already implies a group.

*   **Topic Marker (은/는) vs. Subject Marker (이/가):** Ji introduces these as "markers" rather than "particles" to clarify their roles. 은/는 marks the topic (what the sentence is about), while 이/가 marks the subject of the specific verb.
````

````md
Korean Particle ~들 and using 몇 with a counter

You have probably been asking yourself ‘how can I make something plural?’ Up to now, I haven’t mentioned anything about plural words in Korean. The reason for this is Korean people rarely distinguish between singular and plural. For example, if I say:

Play[나는 사과를 샀어](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/Lesson-12-s1.mp3)

This could mean “I bought an apple” OR “I bought apples.” This seems crazy to English speakers, but this is just how it is done in Korean. In most cases, the context can make it clear if you bought ‘an apple’ or if you bought ‘apples.’ If you really want to make it clear that you bought one apple, you could say:

Play[나는 사과 한 개를 샀어](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/Lesson-12-s2.mp3) = I bought one apple

When dealing with the ambiguity of singular/plural sentences in Korean, you could also use the word 몇 which can replace a number in these examples: 두 개/두 명/두 번. When 몇 replaces a number in these cases (몇 번/몇 명/몇 개), it has the meaning of “some \_\_\_\_.” For example, instead of saying: “Play[나는 사과 한 개를 샀어](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/Lesson-12-s2.mp3)”, you could say:

Play[나는 사과 몇 개를 샀어](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Lesson12-4.mp3) = I bought SOME apples

More examples (remember that 펜 한 개 and 한 개의 펜 have the same meaning):
Play[나는 몇 개의 펜을 샀어](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Lesson12-5.mp3) = I bought some pens
Play[나는 몇 명의 사람을 만났어](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Lesson12-6.mp3) = I met some people
Play[나는 학교에 몇 번 갔어](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Lesson12-7.mp3) = I went to school a few/some times (not sometimes)
*(remember,*번*acts as an adverb – so it doesn’t need a particle to be attached to it).*

Anyways, back to what I was trying to say earlier. ~들 can be attached to a noun to make that noun plural. However, adding ~들 to a noun that is not referring to a person is usually unnatural. Therefore, it would be unnatural to say something like this:

저는 사과들을 샀어요

Instead, ~들 is usually only attached to the word “person” (사람) or other words with the meaning of people (for example: actors, workers, doctors, etc…).

Play[의사들은 돈이 많아](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Lesson12-8.mp3) = doctors have a lot of money
Play[선생님은 내일 학생들을 만날 거야](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Lesson12-9.mp3) = The teacher will meet the students tomorrow
Play[배우들은 그들의\* 영화를 보통 좋아하지 않아](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Lesson12-10.mp3) = actors usually don’t like their movies
*\*By adding the possessive particle*의*to*그들*(them) it becomes*그들의*(their)*

````

#### We, Us, and Our (우리)

- 원문 줄: 301~343

````md

© HowtoStudyKorean.com – Unit 1

6


We, Us, and Our (우리)

At this point I would also like to introduce you to the word “우리” which you can see
from the vocabulary list of this lesson translates to “us” or “we.” In English, even though
they are technically the same word, the usage of “us” or “we” depends on its location
within the sentence it is used in. Just like “I” and “me”, if the word is the subject of a
sentence, “we” is used. For example:

I like you
We like you

However, if the word is the object in a sentence, the word “us” is used. For example:

He likes me
He likes us

In Korean, they do not make this distinction, and “우리” is used in both situations. For
example:

우리는 너를 좋아하다 = We like you
(우리는 너를 좋아해)
I deliberately didn't include a formal version of the sentence above because it is usually
awkward to say the word "you" politely in Korean. We'll get to this in a later lesson.

선생님은 우리를 좋아하다 = The teacher likes us
(선생님은 우리를 좋아해 / 선생님은 우리를 좋아해요)

By placing the possessive particle “의” after “우리” we can create the meaning of “our”.
While this can be done, I feel it is much more common to omit this particle when it is
used with “우리.” In fact, the particle “의” is very commonly omitted from words other
than “우리” as well. However, I don’t suggest thinking about doing this until you have a
better grasp of the language. At this point, I only suggest that you do this with “우리.”
For example:

우리 선생님은 남자이다 = Our teacher is a man
(우리 선생님은 남자야 | 우리 선생님은 남자예요)
````
