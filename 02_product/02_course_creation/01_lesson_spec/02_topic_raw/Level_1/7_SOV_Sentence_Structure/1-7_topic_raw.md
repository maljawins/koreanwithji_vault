````md
**Introduction**

In this lesson, we're going to learn everything about the SOV sentence structure.
This is the basic foundation of the STLOHV Framework I showed you in the "How Korean Works" lesson.

Let's look at these two example sentences:

저(는) 치킨(을) 좋아해요 - "I like chicken."

저(는) 영화(를) 봤어요 - "I watched a movie."

As you can see, Korean puts the verb at the very end.

We covered the word order in detail in the "How Korean Works" lesson, so if you need to, you can go back and review it.

**Object marker**

We have a new particle to learn. 을/를 - called the object marker.

And what it does is very straight forward as the name suggests. All it does is tell you what the object is in a sentence.

If you don’t know what an object is, it’s simply something or someone that receives the action.

Let’s look at this sentence, 저(는) 치킨(을) 좋아해요 – I like chicken.

The object in this sentence would be 치킨 (chicken) because it’s the thing that is “liked” by you.

Now, 을/를 is 받침-sensitive.

So, you use “을” for words that end in 받침 like in “치킨**을**”

and “를” for words that do not end in 받침 like in “영화**를”**.

**Can You Omit Particles?**

The reason I put parentheses around the particles in the example sentences is that they can be omitted. You'll hear Koreans dropping not only object markers, but also other markers like this all the time.

Again, Korean is a very contextual language, and we do this when the role of the noun is obvious through context.

So it's true that sometimes 저 치킨 먹어요 sounds more natural than 저는 치킨을 먹어요.

But I say you, as a learner, first need to understand how to USE these markers properly before you can feel when it's natural to drop them.

Because the meaning of the particle is still in the sentence even though you don't hear it.

**를 Contraction**

One more thing before we move on to verbs: In everyday speech, when the object ends in a vowel, namely, without a 받침, 를 is often shortened to just ㄹ.

나를 becomes 날 - "me"

저를 becomes 절 - "me" (humble)

너를 becomes 널 - "you"

것을 becomes 거를 - "thing" Then 거를 becomes 걸.

If you remember, 거 is just shortened 것.

This applies not only to pronouns but also to regular nouns.

For example, 친구를 (friend) is shortened to 친굴 in speech. But when writing, you have to spell it properly as 친구를.

Now, let’s talk about verbs.

**Action Verbs & Descriptive Verbs**

There are two main types of verbs in Korean: action verbs and descriptive verbs.

Action verbs are pretty straight forward. They are verbs that indicate actions, like "to live," "to go," "to make."

Descriptive verbs are another name for adjectives because they're verbs that describe the state of a noun.

Any verb that is NOT an action would be a descriptive verb.

In fact, we've already learned the two most basic descriptive verbs: 이다 and 아니다.

They don't indicate any sort of action. They simply describe what something is and what it's NOT.

**We talked about why they’re called “descriptive verbs” in the lesson “How Korean Works.” And** that's because they behave exactly like verbs in Korean.

Let me show you more examples as a reminder.

In English, adjectives remain the same in both of these cases: "a small cat" or "The cat is small."

But unlike English, descriptive verbs in Korean change their form depending on the usage.

작다 means “to be small” and it’s a descriptive verb.

And “a small cat” in Korean is “작은 고양이”

And “A cat is small.” Is “고양이는 작아요”

As you can see, when it directly modifies a noun, we get 작은, but when used as the predicate, we get **작아요.**

We'll learn about how to make “작은” in Level 4, but this is just to show you briefly how it's done.

And second, the way they both conjugate is almost identical.

Let’s use ‘살다’ – to live and make “A cat lives” in Korean.

Both action verbs and descriptive verbs end in 다 in the dictionary form. By the way, I strongly encourage you to learn the verbs in the dictionary form, not in their “already conjugated forms”.

I will leave a link to a free tool that can conjugate or “unconjugate” any verb for you.

Okay, "A cat lives" would be 고양이는 살아요.

As you can see, 작아요 and 살아요 both conjugate the same way with the 아요 ending.

This is why Korean adjectives are called "descriptive verbs"—because they behave like verbs.

**Important: Descriptive Verbs Cannot Have Objects**

And we’re just about to learn how to make this 아요 ending in the next lesson.

But before we do, I wanted to talk about this super common mistake. You cannot use the object marker with a descriptive verb because descriptive verbs, by nature, cannot have objects to begin with.

An object can only receive an **action,** not a state. So there is no place for an object for any descriptive verb.

For example, you can “SHINE” something but can’t “SHINY” something. You can “taste” something but you can’t “tasty” something. See where I’m going with this?

Let me show you an example of an incorrect sentence.

“김밥을 맛있어요”. This doesn’t make any sense because you can’t “tasty” a Gimbab.

The correct sentence would be “김밥은 맛있어요.”

This is why I made sure to cover the difference between action verbs and descriptive verbs first.

This will become move relevant when we learn about the two particles - 은는 and 이가.

But remember, “Descriptive verbs cannot have an object”

Alright, in our next lesson, we’re going to learn how to conjugate present tense in Korean.
````

````md
### Dropping Particles in Casual Speech
In casual Korean, the object, topic, and subject markers are often removed entirely. The instructor demonstrates this with the sentence `밥을 먹어요 (I eat a meal/rice)`. #### The Object Marker: 을 and 를
````

- 원문 줄: 28~40

````md
### The Object Marker: 을 and 를
The object marker identifies the noun that is receiving the action of a verb. It is used exclusively with action verbs. The instructor illustrates this with English examples like "I like cheese" or "He reads a book," where "cheese" and "book" are the objects. In Korean, these must be marked.

The conjugation rules are:
- **을** is added after a noun ending in a consonant.
- **를** is added after a noun ending in a vowel.

For example, `치즈 (cheese)` ends in a vowel, so it becomes `치즈를 좋아해요 (I like cheese)`. The instructor explains that hearing the marker `를` alerts the listener that something is about to happen to the cheese—it might be eaten, kicked, or liked.

There are important exceptions for verbs of communication. Verbs like `말하다 (to talk/speak)`, `전화하다 (to call)`, and `물어보다 (to ask)` do not use the object marker for the person being addressed. Instead, they use "to" markers like `에게` or `한테`. For instance, one should say `친구한테 말해주세요 (Please tell [to] your friend)` rather than using an object marker on "friend."
````

````md
### Basic Mechanics: 을 vs 를
The object marker attaches directly to a noun. The choice between 을 and 를 depends entirely on whether the noun ends in a consonant or a vowel. If the noun ends in a consonant, you attach 을. If the noun ends in a vowel, you attach 를. The instructor demonstrates this on the whiteboard with the symbols "C + 을" and "V + 를." This marker identifies the "object" of a sentence, which is the person, place, or thing that an action verb is directly affecting.
````

#### Action Verbs vs. Descriptive Verbs

- 원문 줄: 28~40

````md
### Action Verbs vs. Descriptive Verbs
A crucial rule emphasized throughout the lesson is that the object marker is used with action verbs only. It cannot be used with descriptive verbs (adjectives), such as "to be funny," "to be hungry," or "to be tall," because these verbs describe a state rather than performing an action on an object.

The instructor highlights several common verbs that learners often misuse:
- **있다 (to exist/to have):** You cannot "exist" something. In Korean, saying "I have a pen" is structured as "As for me, a pen exists." Therefore, you use the subject marker (이/가), never the object marker, with 있다.
- **좋다 (to be good):** This is a descriptive verb. You cannot use an object marker with it. If you want to say "I like [something]" using an action verb that takes an object, you must use 좋아하다.
- **필요하다 (to be necessary/to need):** While translated as "to need" in English, in Korean it functions as "to be necessary." Thus, "I need water" is expressed as "Water is necessary" (물이 필요해요), using the subject marker.

---

### Verbs of Communication and Giving
The instructor warns against using the object marker with verbs involving communication, such as "to call," "to ask," or "to speak." In these cases, the person you are talking to is not the direct object being "affected" in the Korean grammatical sense; rather, the "object" is the information or words being conveyed. Instead of the object marker, you must use the particles 에게 (formal/written) or 한테 (casual/spoken), which mean "to." For example, "Call me" should be 저에게 전화해 주세요 (Call to me), not 저를 전화해 주세요. Similarly, with the verb 주다 (to give), the object marker marks the item being given (e.g., 선물을 주다 - to give a present), while the recipient takes the "to" particle.
````

#### Omitting and Shortening the Marker

- 원문 줄: 43~54

````md
### Omitting and Shortening the Marker
In casual spoken Korean, the object marker is often omitted if the context makes the relationship clear. However, the instructor cautions that removing it can sometimes create ambiguity. For instance, 저 좋아해요 could mean "I like it" (assuming 저는) or "He/she likes me" (assuming 저를). If there is any risk of being misunderstood, the marker should be kept.

Common contractions used in speech include:
- **거슬** (thing + object marker) becomes **걸**
- **저를** (me) becomes **절**
- **나를** (me, casual) becomes **날**
- **너를** (you, casual) becomes **널**

Shortening 를 to just the letter ㄹ and attaching it to a vowel-ending noun (like 어디를 to 어딜) is common but can sound somewhat slangy.
````

#### Object Markers with 하다 Verbs

- 원문 줄: 56~59

````md
### Object Markers with 하다 Verbs
Many Korean verbs are formed by combining a noun with 하다 (to do), such as 공부하다 (to study) or 요리하다 (to cook). These can be split into [Noun] + [Object Marker] + [하다]. For example, 공부를 하다 literally means "to do studying." When the marker is used, there must be a space before 하다. If the marker is removed, the noun and 하다 must be joined without a space (공부하다). To avoid repetitive sounds, it is often more natural to remove the marker from the 하다 verb if another object is already present in the sentence, such as 파스타를 요리해요 (I cook pasta) instead of 파스타를 요리를 해요.
````

````md
### What is the Object Marker?
The object marker is a particle that attaches directly after a noun, which can be a person, place, or thing. Its primary function is to mark the object of an action verb. In linguistic terms, the object is whoever or whatever is receiving the action or being directly affected by the verb. For example, in the English sentence "I eat pizza," the word "pizza" is the object because it is the thing being eaten. In Korean, the object marker is placed after the noun for pizza to clarify its role in the sentence. It essentially shows who or what a verb is doing something to.
````

#### Conjugation Rules: 을 vs 를

- 원문 줄: 27~39

````md
### Conjugation Rules: 을 vs 를
The choice between the two forms of the object marker depends entirely on whether the preceding noun ends in a consonant or a vowel. If the noun ends with a consonant (meaning it has a 받침 or bottom consonant), the marker **을** is attached. If the noun ends in a vowel (meaning there is no bottom consonant), the marker **를** is attached. This distinction ensures smooth pronunciation when transitioning from the noun to the particle.

The instructor provides a practice list to demonstrate these rules in action:
- **피자 (pizza)** ends in a vowel, so it becomes **피자를**.
- **팝콘 (popcorn)** ends in a consonant, so it becomes **팝콘을**.
- **영화 (movie)** ends in a vowel, so it becomes **영화를**.
- **티비 (TV)** ends in a vowel, so it becomes **티비를**.
- **김치 (kimchi)** ends in a vowel, so it becomes **김치를**.
- **한글 (Hangeul)** ends in a consonant, so it becomes **한글을**.
````

#### The Object Marker: 을 and 를

- 원문 줄: 24~30

````md
### The Object Marker: 을 and 를
The object marker is used to identify the noun that is being directly affected by a verb. The instructor notes that this marker is strictly used with action verbs. Because descriptive verbs (adjectives) describe a state rather than performing an action on something, they cannot take an object and therefore never use 을 or 를.

The choice between the two forms depends on whether the preceding noun ends in a consonant or a vowel. Use 을 after a consonant and 를 after a vowel. The instructor demonstrates this with the verb 좋아하다 (to like). If you like cake, you take the word 케이크 (cake), which ends in the vowel ㅡ, and add 를 to create 케이크를 좋아해요. If you are eating the cake, the verb 먹다 (to eat) affects the cake, so cake remains the object: 케이크를 먹어요. Essentially, any time an action verb is doing something to a person or thing, that target must be marked as the object.
````

#### The Function of the Object Marker

- 원문 줄: 24~31

````md
### The Function of the Object Marker

The primary use of the object marker is to mark the object of a verb. In any given sentence, the object is the person or thing that is receiving an action. The instructor provides a clear comparison between English and Korean structures. In the English sentence "I kicked the ball," the word "ball" is the object because it receives the action of kicking. In the Korean equivalent, **저는 공을 찼어요**, the noun **공 (ball)** is followed by the marker **을** to signify its role as the object.

Another example used is **제가 과자를 먹었어요 (I ate the snacks)**. Here, the verb is **먹다 (to eat)**, used in the past tense. Since the snacks are what is being eaten, they receive the action and must be marked. The instructor emphasizes that identifying what is receiving the action is the key to knowing where to place the marker.
````

#### Choosing Between 을 and 를

- 원문 줄: 32~42

````md
### Choosing Between 을 and 를

The object marker has two forms depending on whether the preceding noun ends in a consonant or a vowel. This is a common pattern in Korean particles to ensure smooth pronunciation.

- Use **을** if the noun ends in a consonant (has a 받침).
- Use **를** if the noun ends in a vowel (no 받침).

The instructor demonstrates this with several examples. In **공을 (the ball)**, the noun **공** ends in the consonant **ㅇ**, so **을** is attached. In **과자를 (the snacks)**, the noun **과자** ends in the vowel **ㅏ**, so **를** is used. Further examples include **공부를 (study)**, where **공부** ends in a vowel, and **시간을 (time)**, where **시간** ends in a consonant.
````

#### Important Exceptions: Communication and Giving Verbs

- 원문 줄: 60~67

````md
### Important Exceptions: Communication and Giving Verbs

A critical point of the lesson is that not every English object is a Korean object. In English, verbs like "say," "tell," "call," or "give" often attach the receiver of the action directly as an object (e.g., "I told **him**"). However, in Korean, these receivers are often marked with the "to" particle **에게** (or **한테**) rather than the object marker.

For example, in the sentence "I gave him the ball," the ball is the direct object (**공을**), but "him" is the indirect receiver. In Korean, this is expressed as **나는 그에게 공을 주었다**. The instructor notes that verbs related to communication and giving generally do not use the object marker for the person being addressed or given to.
````

#### Basic Sentence Structure: SOV vs. SVO

- 원문 줄: 23~27

````md
### Basic Sentence Structure: SOV vs. SVO
The most fundamental difference between English and Korean is the word order. While English follows a Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) pattern, such as "I love you," Korean follows a Subject-Object-Verb (SOV) pattern. To say "I love you" in Korean, the conceptual order is "I you love." Using the dictionary forms of these words results in `나 (I)`, `너 (you)`, and `사랑하다 (to love)`. However, simply placing these words in order is grammatically incorrect and sounds unnatural. A proper sentence requires the addition of particles and verb conjugation: `나는 너를 사랑해`.
````

#### Bestowal Verbs (수요동사)

- 원문 줄: 54~70

````md
### Bestowal Verbs (수요동사)
Bestowal verbs imply giving or bestowing something to someone. In English, these often have a direct and indirect object. In Korean, only the item being given (the direct object) is marked with 을/를. The person receiving the item is marked with 에게 or 한테 (meaning "to").

The instructor lists seven key bestowal verbs:
- **주다 (to give)**
- **보내다 (to send)**
- **가르치다 (to teach)**
- **말하다 / 말해주다 (to tell)**
- **보여주다 (to show)**
- **사주다 (to buy someone something)**
- **가져다 주다 (to bring someone something)**

Example:
> **저는 선물을 제 친구한테 줬어요.** / I gave a gift to my friend.
(선물 is the object marked with 을; 친구 is the receiver marked with 한테).
````

#### Special Cases: Calling and Summoning

````md
### Special Cases: Calling and Summoning
The verb **전화하다 (to make a phone call)** treats the person being called as a receiver, not a direct object. Therefore, you use **한테/에게**, not **을/를**. Saying "엄마를 전화하다" would imply you are treating your mother as the physical telephone.

However, the verb **부르다** has two meanings that *do* take object markers:
1. **To call someone by a name:** The person being called is the object.
   > **저희 가족은 저를 '귀염둥이'라고 불러요.** / My family calls me a "cutie pie."
2. **To summon someone:** The person being told to come is the object.
   > **당장 지수를 불러.** / Call (summon) Jisu right now.
````

#### 상태·동작 동사와 목적격 조사

- 원문 줄: 23~24

````md
*   **Descriptive vs. Action Verbs:** Descriptive verbs (adjectives like 좋다) cannot take objects. Therefore, the object marker 을/를 cannot be used with them. Instead, the thing being described is marked with the subject marker 이/가.
*   **The Object Marker 을/를:** Used only with action verbs. Like other particles, it follows the 받침 rule: 을 for words ending in a consonant, 를 for words ending in a vowel.
````
