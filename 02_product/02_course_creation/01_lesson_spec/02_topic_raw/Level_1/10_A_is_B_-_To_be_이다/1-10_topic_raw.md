````md
**A is B**

In this lesson, we’re going to learn about “이다” - “To be”.

Before we get started, I’m going to need you to start thinking of the concept of “Be” in a different light because the word “Be” in English is very versatile.

Let’s look at these sentences

I am a man – 이다 (A is B)

저는 남자예요

I am (at) home – 있다 (be, exist)

저는 집에 있어요

I am kind – 친절하다 (To be kind)

저는 친절해요

As you can see, “am” in each sentence has completely different functions.

And Korean expresses these three meanings with different words.

And what we need here is the first one is **이다**.

**A is B – 이다 Conjugation**

이다 is a unique verb, which means it doesn’t follow the “regular” conjugation rules.

So, this is something you have to memorize.

**이다 Conjugation**

이다 is 받침-sensitive, which means the way it conjugates *changes* depending on whether the noun being described ends in a 받침 or not.

Here’s how to say “I am a student” in 3 different speech levels.

Casual: 나는 학생이야.

Polite: 저는 학생이에요.

Formal: 저는 학생입니다.

As you can see, 학생 meaning student ends in a 받침. So it’s 이야 in 반말, 이에요 in polite speech, and 입니다 in formal speech.

Both ‘나’ and ‘저’ mean “I” but “저” is the humble, polite version. So, ‘저’ goes hand in hand with 존댓말 instead of ‘나’.

는 is called the "topic marker." It marks who or what the sentence is about.

We also have 은 for words that end in a 받침—like 이 사람은, meaning "this person."

Note that there's absolutely no way to translate this because it simply doesn't exist in English.

There are other particles you can add after the subject of the sentence, and we're going to learn about them very soon.

For now, just know that 은 and 는 are the most commonly used particles when introducing yourself.

And let’s look at when there’s no 받침.

“I am a singer”

나는 가수**야**저는 가수**예요**저는 가수**입니다.**

이다 becomes 야 in casual speech, 예요 in polite speech and 입니다 in formal speech.

But in Koreans actually say these like this

1. We say 에요 instead of 예요
- 에요 is easier to say than 예요 right? So we’d say 가수에요 not 가수예요.

2. Secondly, instead of 가수입니다, we’d say 가숩니다

This makes it flow better. But you’d have to use the correct spelling in writing.

**This and That**

Now let’s learn how to say **“this”** and **“that”** in Korean to say **"This is A. That is B."**

In English, **“this”** and **“that”** can be both **nouns and adjectives**. However, in Korean, they are **always adjectives**.

So, if you **must** add a noun **‘것’ (thing) or 거 -the shortened version. And we almost always just say “거” in spoken Korean rather than “것”.**

So we have 이거 저거 and 그거

이 means "this." Anything close to the speaker, you'd say 이거. You can also say 요거, but this only happens in speech, not writing. Fun fact - 요거 comes from the 전라 regional dialect, but now it's used everywhere in the country.

And we have 2 version for “that”.

저 is for something you can see but is far away from both the speaker and the listener like “that tree over there” - “저 나무” If the tree is really far away, you could say “쩌어~ 나무”.

And 그 is used in 2 cases.

First, we use it for anything that’s not close enough to be called “this” like in this picture (show a picture of a staff holding a product).

And second, when you’re talking about something that is not in your sight like “that guy I met yesterday” You could also say “고거”. And just like “요거”, this is only used in informal situations.

**Here and There**

I want you to pay attention to the consonants of these words because the same logic applies to “here and there”.

So, here is 여기, Over there is 저기 and 거기 would be just “there”.

This is a very important concept to remember so make sure to make a note of this.

**This and That**

Back to This and that. We just learned that 것 can shortened to 거.

And when combined with the topic marker, **것은 becomes 거는 and 거는 becomes 건**.

Here are some examples:

**이것은 의자예요 / 이거는 의자예요 / 이건 의자예요**

**저것은 책상이에요 / 저거는 책상이에요 / 저건 책상이에요**

**그것은 공책이에요 / 그거는 공책이에요 / 그건 공책이에요**

**What is your name?**

Before we learn how to say “My name is”, let’s first learn “What is your name?”

We have two versions to say this in Korean.

The first one is “성함이 어떻게 되세요?” This is the politest way to ask someone their name.

And the second one is “이름이 뭐예요?” 이 표현은 두 가지 조건이 충족되지 않으면 다소 무례하게 느껴질 수도 있다. 첫번째는 처음 만나는 사람의 나이가 네 나이와 비슷해보일 때이다.

두번째는 만난 장소가 파티나 지인을 통해 누군가를 소개받는 자리와 같이 사적인 자리일 때이다.

So, you would never say this to someone like a 60 year old 아저씨 or your customer even if they look younger than you.

Why do they look completely different? That’s because the first one is an idiom which makes no sense in English when translated literally.

The second version though, is pretty straight forward. 이름 means name. And 뭐 means what. And we just learned 예요. Altogether, “The name what is?”.

Now, let’s look at the first phrase.

[Show 성함이 어떻게 되세요?]

성함 is the honorific version of 이름. And “어떻게 되세요?” literally means “How does it become?”

So, “how does the name become?” See, it makes no sense in English.

The best way to think of 어떻게 되세요 is to simply regard it as the polite form of "what is your [noun]...? So, yes, you’re going to have to learn this phrase as a whole chunk.

Here’s a few more examples

나이가 어떻게 되세요? – 나이 means age. So, this means “How old are you?”

In 반말 though, it would be “너(는) 몇 살이야?” By the way, do you see the irony here? Asking for someone’s age in 반말?

I mean, age is the most important information to speak 반말 in the first place.

그래서 누가 봐도 너보다 어린 꼬마 아이들을 빼고는 네가 누군가에게 반말로 나이를 묻는 일은 없을 것이다.

직업이 어떻게 되세요? – 직업 is job or profession. So, this one means “What do you do for a living?”

In 반말, you’d say “너(는) 직업이 뭐야?”

**My name is**

Now, here’s how you say “My name is” Note you’d never use 성함 to refer to your own name. It’s always 이름.

Polite – 제 이름은 Name 이에요 / 예요.

Casual – 내 이름은 Name 이야 / 야.

Formal – 제 이름은 Name 입니다.

**Closure**

In this lesson, we learned how 이다—"to be"—changes according to each speech level, and we learned about 이, 저, and 그.

We're going to cover the topic marker in detail in Level 2, so you don't need to know it deeply yet.

In our next lesson, we're going to start learning pronouns, starting with "How to Say You."

See you there!
````

````md
### Sentence Structure: "I Am [Name]"

To state your name politely, the instructor introduces the formula: **저는 [Name] 이에요/예요.** This structure consists of three parts. First, **저는** comes from **저 (I/me)** and the topic marker **는**, literally meaning "as for me." Second is the person's name. Third is the polite ending **이에요** or **예요**, which both come from the verb **이다 (to be)**. This verb functions like an equal sign, essentially saying "I equal [Name]."

The choice between the two endings depends on the final sound of the name:
- Use **이에요** if the name ends in a consonant (e.g., **석진이에요**).
- Use **예요** if the name ends in a vowel (e.g., **빌리예요**).

The instructor notes that while **예요** is the correct spelling after a vowel, it is often pronounced simply as **에요**. Additionally, while casual versions like **나는** exist, they are discouraged for first-time meetings to avoid being accidentally rude.
````

````md
### Refresher: The Verb 이다 (To Be)
Before diving into demonstratives, the instructor reviews the copula 이다 (to be). In polite spoken Korean, this verb takes two forms: 이에요 and 예요. The choice depends on the preceding sound: use 이에요 after a consonant and 예요 after a vowel. Note that while written as 예요, it is often pronounced as 에요.

The instructor explains that 이다 functions like an "equals sign" in a sentence. For example, in the sentence 저는 빌리예요 (I am Billy), the structure implies "I = Billy." This understanding is crucial because when saying "This is a movie," the Korean sentence structure will literally be "This thing = movie."

---

### Demonstrative Adjectives: 이, 그, and 저
Korean uses three distinct words to express "this" and "that" as adjectives. These words must always be followed by a noun and a space.

**이 (This)** is used for objects close to the speaker. Examples include:
- **이 영화** / this movie
- **이 티비** / this television
- **이 음악** / this music

**그 (That)** is used for objects close to the listener, but far from the speaker. Examples include:
- **그 영화** / that movie
- **그 티비** / that television
- **그 음악** / that music

**저 (That over there)** is used for objects far from both the speaker and the listener. It is important not to confuse this adjective 저 with the pronoun 저 (I). Examples include:
- **저 영화** / that movie (over there)
- **저 티비** / that television (over there)
- **저 음악** / that music (over there)

---

### Demonstrative Nouns: 이것, 그것, and 저것
When you want to use "this" or "that" as a standalone noun (e.g., "This is a movie" or "I want that"), you combine the adjectives above with the word 것, which means "thing."

- **이것** (This thing): Close to the speaker.
- **그것** (That thing): Close to the listener.
- **저것** (That thing over there): Far from both.

Unlike the adjectives 이, 그, and 저, these are full nouns and can take particles like the topic marker (은/는) or the object marker (을/를). The instructor provides several examples of these nouns used with the topic marker:
- **이것은 영화예요.** / This (thing) is a movie.
- **그것은 티비예요.** / That (thing) is a television.
- **저것은 음악이에요.** / That (thing over there) is music.
````

#### Usage Notes and Spoken Contractions

- 원문 줄: 80~92

````md
---

### Usage Notes and Spoken Contractions
The instructor emphasizes the grammatical difference: 이, 그, and 저 are adjectives and cannot stand alone with particles (e.g., "이는" is incorrect). They must always modify a noun. Conversely, 이것, 그것, and 저것 are nouns and can be used freely as subjects or objects.

In casual spoken Korean, the noun forms are frequently shortened. The final consonant 'ㅅ' is dropped, resulting in:
- **이것** → **이거**
- **그것** → **그거**
- **저것** → **저거**

While both the full and shortened versions are correct and widely used, learners will encounter the shortened versions more often in daily conversation.
````

#### Identity and Equality: 이다 and 아니다

- 원문 줄: 24~38

````md
### Identity and Equality: 이다 and 아니다

The verb 이다 means "to be" in the sense of "to be equal to." It is used when you are identifying what something is. For example, if you want to say "That is a cat," you use 이다 because you are stating that "that" is equal to the category of "cat." The instructor provides the sentence 그것은 고양이에요 (That is a cat) to illustrate this. A key grammatical point is that 이다 does not require a subject marker (이/가) on the noun it follows.

The opposite of 이다 is 아니다, which means "to not be" or "to not be equal to." Unlike 이다, 아니다 requires the use of the subject marker 이 or 가 to mark the noun that is being negated. The instructor demonstrates this with the sentence 그것은 고양이가 아니에요 (That is not a cat), highlighting the inclusion of the marker 가. In casual or slang speech, this marker is sometimes omitted, as seen in the example 난 바보 아니야 (I'm not stupid), where 난 is a contraction of 나는. Another formal example provided is 저는 좋은 학생입니다 (I am a good student).

---

### Existence and Possession: 있다 and 없다

The verb 있다 means "to exist." While it often translates to "is" or "are" in English, its core meaning is "there is" or "something exists." This is the primary source of confusion with 이다, as both can translate to "is," but 있다 never means "is equal to." Instead, it is used to describe the location of something or the fact that something exists in a certain place. For example, 그는 학교에 있다 (He is at school) uses 있다 because it describes his existence at the location of the school (marked by the location particle 에). Using 이다 here would incorrectly imply that the person is equal to the school.

있다 and its opposite 없다 (to not exist/there is not) are also the standard way to express "to have" and "to not have" in Korean. The instructor explains that a sentence like 저는 돈이 많이 있어요 literally translates to "As for me, there is a lot of money," but it is the natural way to say "I have a lot of money." The adverb 많이 (a lot) is used here to modify the quantity. Conversely, 저는 돈이 많이 없어요 means "I do not have a lot of money." When using 있다 and 없다, the item that exists or is possessed is typically marked with the subject marker 이 or 가.
````

````md
### Present Tense Conjugation: 이에요 and 예요

In the present tense, 이다 conjugates into two polite (존댓말) forms depending on the final sound of the noun it follows. If a noun ends in a vowel (no 받침), the ending 예요 is used. If a noun ends in a consonant (받침), the ending 이에요 is used. The instructor notes that while 예요 is the correct spelling, it is often pronounced as 에요 in natural speech. For casual speech (반말), the forms are 야 (after a vowel) and 이야 (after a consonant).

The instructor provides several examples to demonstrate this:
- **저는 여자예요.** / I am a woman. (여자 ends in a vowel, so 예요 is used.)
- **저는 학생이에요.** / I am a student. (학생 ends in a consonant, so 이에요 is used.)
- **제 이름은 Vicky예요.** / My name is Vicky.
- **저희 부모님은 한국인이에요.** / My parents are Koreans.

A cultural note is included regarding the word "my." While 제 is the standard polite form of "my," Koreans often use 저희 (our) when referring to family members or their home, as seen in the example **저희 부모님** (our parents).
````

````md
### Past Tense Conjugation: 이었어요 and 였어요

To express "was" or "were," 이다 conjugates into past tense forms. Similar to the present tense, the choice depends on the presence of a final consonant. If the noun ends in a vowel, 였어요 is used. If the noun ends in a consonant, 이었어요 is used. For casual speech, the 요 is simply removed to create 였어 or 이었어.

Examples shown:
- **지수는 학생이었어요.** / Jisoo was a student.
- **은지는 알코올 중독자였어요.** / Eunji was an alcoholic. (중독자 ends in a vowel.)
````

````md
To be: 이다

Let’s start building sentences in Korean. In this lesson, we will start by making simple
sentences using the word 이다. The translation for “이다” is “to be.”  English speakers
````

````md
adjectives, but the rules for 이다 are often (but not always) different. I will teach you

Like adjectives, 이다 can not act on an object. Only verbs can act on objects. For
example:

I eat hamburgers (eat is a verb, the object is a hamburger)
She meets my friend (meet is a verb, the object is my friend)
They study Korean (study is a verb, the object is Korean)
We listen to music (listen is a verb, the object is music)

All of those sentences (can) have objects because the verb is the predicate of the sentence.
However, in sentences that are predicated by adjectives, there will not be an object:

I am pretty
She is beautiful
They are hungry
We are smart
````

````md
Unlike in English, 이다 is not used in these types of sentences. That is, we do not use
이다 to indicate that something “is” an adjective. The structure of sentences predicated
by adjectives is discussed in Lesson 3.

So, 이다 is not used in these types of sentences:

I am pretty
She is beautiful
They are hungry
We are smart

However, 이다 is used in these types of sentences:

I am a man
He is a man
They are men
I was a man
They were men

이다 is used to indicate that a noun is a noun. The basic structure for a sentence
predicated by “이다” is:

[noun 은/는] [another noun][이다]

For example:
I 는 man 이다 = I am a man

Now substitute the words for “man” and “I,” which are:

나 = I
남자 = man

The Korean sentence would look like this:

나는 남자이다 = I am a man
````
