## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor, Billy, addresses a common point of confusion for Korean learners: the difference between the negative markers **안** and **못**. Both words are placed directly in front of a verb to change its meaning into a negative one. However, they are not interchangeable, as they convey completely different reasons for why an action is not happening.

The lesson focuses on the semantic distinction between intentionality and inability. By the end of the video, learners should understand that **안** corresponds to "do not" (a choice), while **못** corresponds to "cannot" (an inability or external restriction).

---

### Placement and Verb Types
Billy begins by demonstrating on-screen how to use these markers with "pure Korean" verbs. To make a verb negative, you simply place **안** or **못** directly before the verb stem. He defines pure Korean verbs as those that do not originate from Chinese (Sino-Korean). While over 60% of Korean vocabulary is Sino-Korean—often ending in **하다**—Billy provides a quick trick for identification: if a verb has only two syllables in its dictionary form, such as **하다 (to do)**, **살다 (to live)**, or **가다 (to go)**, it is a pure Korean verb.

The instructor notes that while you can put **안** directly before the single-syllable verb **하다** to get **안 하다**, longer Sino-Korean verbs ending in **하다** usually require a different structure where the marker is placed between the noun and the verb. To keep the lesson focused, he sticks to pure Korean verbs where the marker always precedes the entire verb.

---

### 안: Intentional Negation ("Don't")
The marker **안** is used to indicate that an action is not being performed by choice or intention. It translates most closely to "do not" in English. When a speaker uses **안**, they are communicating that they have the capability to perform the action, but they are intentionally deciding not to do it.

Billy writes on his glass board that **안** equals "intention." For example, if someone says they are not speaking a language using **안**, it implies they might actually know the language but are simply refusing to speak it at that moment.

---

### 못: Inability Negation ("Can't")
In contrast, **못** indicates a lack of ability or the presence of an external obstacle that prevents the action from occurring. It translates to "cannot." Using **못** signals that the subject is incapable of performing the action, regardless of their desire to do so.

On the board, Billy labels **못** as "can't." This is the appropriate marker to use when you lack a skill (like speaking a foreign language) or when circumstances beyond your control (like an illness or a physical barrier) stop you from completing an action.

---

### Comparison Examples
The instructor provides two clear scenarios to illustrate the functional difference between the two markers.

**Scenario 1: Going to School**
Starting with the affirmative sentence **학교에 가요 (I go to school)**, Billy shows how the meaning shifts:
- **학교에 안 가요.** / I am not going to school (by choice).
- **학교에 못 가요.** / I cannot go to school (due to a reason/inability).

He jokes that if you tell a teacher **학교에 못 가요**, they will ask for a reason, such as being sick or having an emergency. If you say **학교에 안 가요**, it simply means you don't want to go.

**Scenario 2: Speaking English**
This example highlights how using the wrong marker can change the perceived meaning of your skill level:
- **영어를 안 해요.** / I don't speak English (I choose not to).
- **영어를 못 해요.** / I can't speak English (I don't know how).

If you want to tell someone you haven't learned English yet, you must use **못**. Using **안** would sound like you are being stubborn or intentionally silent despite knowing the language.
