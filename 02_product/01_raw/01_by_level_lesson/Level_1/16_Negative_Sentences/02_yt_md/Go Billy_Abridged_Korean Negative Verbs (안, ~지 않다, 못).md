## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the concept of negation in Korean, noting that while English simply places "don't" or "doesn't" before a verb, Korean offers multiple structures with specific grammatical rules. The lesson covers the two primary ways to say "don't" (the long form ~지 않다 and the short form 안), the ways to say "can't" (the long form ~지 못하다 and the short form 못), and special verbs that have their own built-in negative forms.

The learning arc begins with the most versatile and rule-free method (~지 않다), moves into the more nuanced and restricted short-form negation (안), explains the distinction between "don't" and "can't," and concludes with negative adverbs that emphasize the degree of negation. Throughout the video, the instructor uses a whiteboard to demonstrate conjugation mechanics and categorizes verbs into "Pure Korean" and "Sino-Korean" to explain why certain negation styles are restricted.

---

### Long Form Negation: ~지 않다
The instructor identifies ~지 않다 as the best form for beginners to learn first because it is simple, consistent, and has no special usage restrictions. To create this form, one takes the verb stem (the verb minus 다) and attaches 지 않다. For example, the verb 가다 (to go) becomes 가지 않다 (to not go). When conjugated into the polite 요 form, it becomes 가지 않아요. This form is slightly more common in formal situations compared to the short form.

---

### Short Form Negation: 안
The second method involves placing the word 안 directly before a verb. While simpler in structure, it is trickier because it cannot be used with every verb. The instructor explains that 안 is primarily used with "Pure Korean" verbs—words that do not originate from Chinese. A helpful trick to identify these is that most two-syllable verbs like 가다 (to go), 오다 (to come), 먹다 (to eat), 살다 (to live), and 놀다 (to play) are Pure Korean and work perfectly with 안, such as 안 가다.

The instructor highlights a major exception involving action verbs ending in 하다. Many of these are Sino-Korean (Chinese-origin) nouns combined with 하다 (to do), such as 공부하다 (to study) or 요리하다 (to cook). For these action verbs, you cannot place 안 at the very beginning. Instead, you must separate the noun from the verb and place 안 before the 하다 part. Thus, "to not study" is 공부 안 하다, not 안 공부하다. However, this separation rule only applies to action verbs. Descriptive verbs (adjectives) that end in 하다 but are Pure Korean, like 심심하다 (to be bored), do not split; one would say 안 심심하다.

---

### Verbs with Built-in Negatives
Some Korean verbs have unique negative counterparts, and using 안 with the positive version is considered incorrect. The instructor provides three key examples:
- **알다 (to know):** The negative is 모르다 (to not know). You cannot say 안 알다.
- **있다 (to exist/have):** The negative is 없다 (to not exist/not have). While 있지 않다 is grammatically possible, 없다 is the standard choice.
- **이다 (to be):** The negative is 아니다 (to not be). This requires the subject being negated to be marked with the subject marker 이 or 가. For example:
> **저는 빌리가 아니에요.** / I am not Billy.

---

### Expressing Inability: ~지 못하다 and 못
To express that one "can't" do something (incapability) rather than just "doesn't" do it (choice or general fact), Korean uses 못. Similar to the "don't" forms, there is a long form and a short form. The long form is created by adding 지 못하다 to the verb stem, such as 가르치지 못하다 (can't teach). The short form involves placing 못 before Pure Korean verbs, like 못 가르치다.

The instructor clarifies the semantic difference: 안 해요 means "I don't do it" (perhaps by choice), while 못 해요 means "I can't do it" (due to lack of ability or circumstance). For example, if asked if you speak English, saying 영어 안 해요 implies you are choosing not to speak it, whereas 영어 못 해요 correctly conveys that you are unable to speak it.

---

### Negative Adverbs
The lesson concludes with four adverbs that are used exclusively with negative verb forms to add emphasis or nuance:
- **절대로 (never):** Used for absolute negation. "I never study" is 저는 절대로 공부 안 해요.
- **별로 (not really/not very):** Indicates a low degree or frequency. It often carries a slight tone of disappointment. "I don't really like Kimchi" is 김치를 별로 안 좋아해요.
- **그다지 / 그다 (not so):** Similar to 별로 but used specifically with descriptive verbs to mean "not so [adjective]."
- **전혀 (not at all):** Indicates zero percent or "not one bit." "I don't like it at all" is 전혀 안 좋아해요.
