**Hello**

**[Show / Read 안녕하세요 in a natural tone]**

**안녕하세요 comes from the descriptive verb 안녕하다** which is **안녕** (meaning well-being, peace) and **하다** (which means "to be" in this context) combined.

So, it literally means **"to be well." Or “to be in peace”**

However, this word is **only used for saying Hello and Goodbye**.

And we also don’t change the way we greet depending on the time of the day like “Good morning” or “Good afternoon”. It’s always “안녕하세요”.

**Hello – Polite speech**

And here’s how we got to 안녕하세요 from 안녕하다.

“안녕하” is the stem and the “세요? ending” turns it into an honorific question so technically it’s actually a question which means “Are you well?”.

[Highlight 세]

However, you’re not supposed to take it literally and answer it - kind of similar to “How are you?” in English in that you’re not supposed to take it literally.

So, what are you supposed to say then?

It’s really simple. You’d just say it right back.

A: 안녕하세요

B: 네, 안녕하세요

That’s it. Pretty simple.

And as you probably noticed from the audio, you’re supposed to lower your intonation towards the end and elongate 요 at the end like “안녕하세요~” So, it doesn’t even sound like a question.

Now, let’s pay attention to "세". If you ever see this consonant ‘시옷’ at the start of a verb ending, it’s most likely an honorific speech. Here, I’m elevating “YOU” like “Are YOU well?, honorifically.”

And one more thing about 세요, 세요 without the question mark could also be a command. So, 안녕하세요 might as well mean just “Be well”. That’s why you’ll often see this written without a question mark. And both versions are correct with, or without the question mark.

Let’s move on to 반말 casual speech.

**Hello – Casual**

For “Hello” in 반말, most textbooks will say it’s 안녕.

However, in real life, nobody really says 안녕 to their friends. In fact, as a native speaker, I don’t think I’ve ever used “안녕” to my friends, not once in my life.

See, in Korea, friends don’t even bother with greeting unless of course, it’s been a long time. But normally, people just get straight down to business since they would have been texting each other beforehand anyways.

So, here are 2 phrases that are much more natural than 안녕

왔어? – It oddly sounds a lot similar to “What’s up?” but it actually means “did you come?” And obviously, it’s a rhetorical question.

왔네? – same thing as 왔어? but this 네 context marker makes it sound like you realized something like “oh, you’re here now.”

**Pattern Recognition Tip**

If you see a ㅆ 받침 in a verb, it's most likely a past tense. We'll learn how to make a past tense sentence in lesson 12.

Moving on to “Hello” in formal.

**Hello in Korean – Formal**

Audio: 안녕하십니까?

This is a VERY formal way to greet somebody like to the viewers on the news or to an audience at a presentation.

Notice the ‘ㅅ’ in ‘십. Again, this makes the verb an honorific speech like I mentioned earlier.

And formal questions always end in "니까?"

Interesting fact about formal speech, is that you can’t add context markers in this speech type. Not because it’s supposed to be formal but because it’s grammatically wrong to add them.

The ending for formal speech IS a context marker in and of itself, and you can’t have more than one context marker in a sentence, which means you can’t add emotions to your sentence when speaking in thus speech level. So, if you overuse this speech type, you will sound too stiff and unnatural, sort of like a robot. Just a little something to remember.

**“How are you?” in Korean**

In English, you normally say “How are you?” after saying “Hi”. But in Korea, we don’t really ask “How are you?” maybe except for these two cases:

1) When it’s been a long time – a few months, at least

2) When we want to know “how you are” literally. — like a doctor checking in on a patient.

Koreans don’t think about **“how they are” on a daily basis**. So, unless it’s been a few weeks at least, asking “How are you?” can sound really unnatural or it could even imply that something might be wrong with you.

**We ask “Did you eat?”**

Instead, it’s very common to ask **"Did you eat?"** as a casual way to check in. I know it sounds a bit strange, but there’s a reason.

Korea used to be extremely poor for centuries, and people would literally starve to death. Thankfully, we’re doing pretty well now, but our obsession with food has been ingrained in our culture and as a result, to our language.

So much so that we even have this idiom “먹고 살다”, which is equivalent to “to make ends meet” in English but what it actually means is “to eat and live” literally.

That’s how important food is in our culture.

So, here’s how you say “Did you eat?” in Korean in the order from the most common to the least common.

1. 식사하셨어요? – Polite (H)

2. 밥 먹었어? – Casual low

3. 식사하셨습니까? – Formal (H)

**First of all, 식사하다** means “to have a meal” where 식사 is a Sino-Korean word, which means it comes from Chinese characters. Koreans tend to use Sino-Korean words to sound formal and fancy formal similar to how English uses words of Latin origin to sound fancy.

So, you could say it kind of sounds like “To dine” instead of “To eat”.

**And 밥** means **"rice”, specifically “cooked rice”. And** “Raw rice” is **쌀 by the way. And** since rice is a huge part of Korean diet, we just casually use **밥** to refer to **"meal"** or **"food” in general.**

**And 먹다 simply means “to eat”**

Now, when you hear this, you might think “Oh, is this person going to give me some food or something?” But that’s not really the case.

If you respond with “안 먹었어요”, meaning “No, I didn’t eat”, most Koreans will just go “Oh, you shouldn’t skip your meal” or “You should eat something” and probably won’t offer any food.

So, I personally just say “네” or “네, 먹었어요” even if I haven’t eaten to avoid unnecessary interactions.

It’s just like saying “good” to “how are you?” even when you’re not so “good” really.

**How are you? – When it’s been a long time**

Again, you wouldn’t say “How are you?” to someone you saw like two days ago. And it goes like

**How are you doing? - Present**

어떻게 지내세요? – Polite

어떻게 지내? – Casual

어떻게 지내십니까? – Formal

**어떻게** means **"how,"** and **지내다** doesn’t have a direct English equivalent, but it means something like **“to spend time”** or **“to do” in a general sense like in “How are you doing?”**

You can also replace 어떻게 with 잘 which means “well” to say “Are you doing well?”

잘 지내세요? 잘 지내? 잘 지내십니까?

Again, there’s a ‘ㅅ’ in polite and formal speech, and you can tell right away that this is an honorific speech. I hope you’re starting to notice the patterns in these endings for different speech levels.

Let’s move on to “Nice to meet you”

**Nice to meet you – Descriptive Verb 반갑다**

When meeting someone for the first time, Koreans use the descriptive verb **반갑다** “to be glad to see someone.” And it’s an irregular verb, which explains why it looks a bit different when conjugated. We’ll learn irregular verbs in level 2. So for now, just learn the whole phrases.

반가워요 반가워 반갑습니다

You can also add **만나서** in front

만나다 means to meet and 서 adds the meaning of “so”. For now, you could associate 서 with “so” since they sound similar.

So, 만나서 반가워요 literally means (we) meet so (I’m) glad

만나서 반가워요 / 만나서 반가워 / 만나서 반갑습니다.

**Bye**

First of all, in 반말, you can say 안녕 for both “Hi” and “Bye”. And the meaning depends on

There’s 2 ways to say "goodbye" in Korean and it depends on who is leaving and who is staying.

If you are leaving, you’d say "Stay well" and if you’re staying, you’d say "Go well."

And here’s what you say when you are leaving.

**Stay (Be) well – When you’re leaving**

안녕히 계세요 – Informal high (H)

잘 있어 – Informal low

안녕히 계십시오 – Formal high (H)

The verb **있다** means "to exist," or in this context “to stay” and **계시다** is the honorific version of **있다**. Notice that it’s not 있으시다. In most cases, you can just add an extra ‘ㅅ’ to make a verb honorific but every language has exceptions to a rule and we have a handful of words that use a completely different word for their honorifics like 계시다. We’ll talk about this in the lesson “Honorifics & Humble Speech” in level 2.

안녕히 is the adverbial form of 안녕하다. Most adverbs in Korean end with ‘히’ or ‘게’ like the ly in English. So, whenever you see a word like this, you’d know that’s an adverb.

Here, you can see the 세요 ending indicating a command.

And formal high command ends in 십시오.

**Go well – When you’re staying**

**And you’re staying and they’re leaving, you say**

안녕히 가세요

잘 가

안녕히 가십시오
