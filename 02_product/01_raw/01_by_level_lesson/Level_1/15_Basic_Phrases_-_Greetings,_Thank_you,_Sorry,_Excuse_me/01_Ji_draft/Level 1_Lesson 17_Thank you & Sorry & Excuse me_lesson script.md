**Intro**

Welcome. In this lesson, we’re going to learn how to say “thank you”, “You’re welcome” “Sorry” and “Excuse me”.

Let’s get right into it and start with “Thank you.”

**Thank you**

고맙다 and 감사하다 are both descriptive verbs, which mean "to be grateful" or "to be thankful."

고맙다 is pure Korean, and 감사 is Sino-Korean, meaning "gratitude."

Again, Sino-Korean has a more formal nuance than pure Korean, so 감사하다 feels like a deeper expression of gratitude, while 고맙다 is a bit more casual.

You'll usually use 감사하다 in 존댓말. For 반말, you only use 고맙다.

This is somewhat similar to how we have "thank you," "thanks," and "I appreciate it" in English.

So you’ll usually use 감사하다 in 존댓말. And for 반말, you only use 고맙다.

Now, in general conversation, informal polite speech—the 요 form—is more commonly used.

But when it comes to expressing gratitude, formal speech is particularly common.

I'll show you the expressions in order of most commonly used to least commonly used:

감사합니다 / 고맙습니다 / 고마워 / 고마워요 / 감사해요

I recommend using 감사합니다 for 존댓말 and 고마워 for 반말.

**You’re welcome**

In English, "you're welcome" is your standard go-to phrase to respond to "thank you."

But Korean doesn't have one response that everyone uses like "you're welcome."

Textbooks or translators will tell you it's 천만에요, but no one ever says this.

So forget about that phrase. Instead, use THESE phrases I'm about to show you.

I'm going to first tell you the literal meaning of each phrase, then the natural translation.

You might ask, "Why so many?"

Well, it's the same in English. You've got lots of different ways to respond to "thank you"—like "no problem," "anytime," "no worries," "of course," "my pleasure," and so on.

**1. 네**

Let's start with the easiest one: 네.

Yes, this means "yes," but like you learned in the Yes & No lesson, 네 can also mean "okay" or "alright."

You'd say it like 네~ with that elongated 네에에~ intonation.

**2. 별 말씀을요**

Next we have “별 말씀을요”.

별 means "special" or "particular," and 말씀 is an honorific form of 말, meaning "speech" or "word."

So 별 말 would be something you say that's out of the ordinary or surprising.

별 has a very nuanced usage, so let me bring up a simple phrase to help you get the idea.

별일—일 means "matter" (not the science term, but "matter" as in "what's the matter?").

This phrase means something like "anything special" or "anything new."

을 is a particle—the object marker. I'll talk about this in detail in the lesson "SOV Sentence Structure."

And 요 at the end indicates 존댓말, polite speech.

So altogether, this phrase implies "What you said is surprising"—meaning I only did what I was supposed to do, and the fact that you're thanking me comes as a surprise.

In other words, “That's nothing to be thankful for.”

**3. 아니야 / 아니에요 / 아닙니다**

아니야 is casual / 아니에요 is polite / 아닙니다 is formal.

They all mean "It's not." And “what it’s not” is implied. It’s like "It's not something to be thankful for."

**4. 고맙긴요 / 고맙긴**

As you might have noticed, 고맙긴 repeats 고마워요—the phrase we learned earlier.

Simply put: 기 after a verb stem turns the verb into a noun. So 고맙기 is like "thanking."

And this is a shortened version of 고맙기는요 with the topic marker 는. This particle deserves a whole lesson.

Just know that it indicates who or what the subject is ABOUT for now.

So it roughly translates to something like "What's there to be thankful ABOUT?" or "It's nothing to thank me for."

Koreans often say it in this tone: "에이~ 고맙긴요."

I hope you felt that.

**I’m Sorry**

Moving on, to “sorry”

Just like we had two words for "thank you," there’s two words for sorry as well. **죄송하다** and **미안하다**. So, just like with "thank you," the same logic applies here.

**죄송하다** is Sino-Korean so again, it’s more formal and it feels more.

And **미안하다** is more casual.

In **존댓말**, **죄송하다** is more commonly used than **미안하다**.

But in **반말 (casual speech)**, we only use **미안하다**.

Here’s how it goes, in the order of most common to least common

죄송합니다

죄송해요

미안해 You can also just say “미안” without the 해 part and it’s even more casual like “My bad”

미안해요

미안합니다

To keep things simple, I’d recommend using **죄송합니다** in 존댓말 and **미안해** in 반말.

**Koreans never say “I’m sorry to hear that”**

This is one of the most common mistakes by so many learners, especially English speakers.

In English, you say “I’m sorry to hear that” or “I’m sorry that happened to you” but in Korean, we don’t say “sorry” to comfort people. The closest thing we have would be “유감입니다”, but this is way too formal, diplomatic and even fake. I mean, the politicians say it all the time. So, don’t say “유감입니다”

So, **the phrases that we just learned are** strictly for apologies.

If you say **죄송합니다** to comfort someone, they’ll be like **“Why are YOU sorry?”**— or worse, they might think you’re the one to blame for what happened.

So, don’t say sorry in Korean unless it’s your fault. It’s as simple as that.

But instead, you can say

“괜찮아(요)? Or 괜찮으세요?” – Are you okay?

“어떡해(요)?”

If you’re a K-drama fan, you must have heard this a thousand times. 어떡해요 actually comes from “어떻게 해요” 어떻게 means “How” and 해요 is a conjugated version of 하다, to do. So “How do?”. In English, this could translate to “What do I do?” Or in this context, “Oh no, what are you gonna do?”

**Excuse me**

Moving on to “excuse me”

First, we have 실례합니다. This is the **dictionary definition** of excuse me.

실례 means **"bad manners or bad etiquettes"**.

◦ So, literally it means **"I'm sorry for my bad manners"** like "I hope you don’t mind my bad manners in an apologetic manner"

◦ It is a **very formal way of speaking** and is not entirely conversational.

◦ It can be used when trying to get people to move out of the way.

◦ A common example is when you have someone over to fix something in your house, like a plumber for example. And they might say “실례합니다” when they enter your home, like - **I'm gonna be “rude” for a moment by entering your home**

The next one is 죄송합니다, “Sorry”. This one’s pretty self-explanatory, so let’s move on.

잠깐만요 / 잠시만요

They’re interchangeable and what they literally mean is **"one moment"** or **"just a moment please".** It is used when trying to move through people in a crowded place, like on a subway.

Another usage of these two phrases would be to get people to pay attention to something you’re about to say like “Wait!”

지나갈게요

It means **"I'm passing"** or **"passing through".** So, you’re letting people know you’re coming through asking them to move out of your way.

저기요

저기 means “over there”. So, it’s like “hey you, over there”. This sounds a bit rude in English but 저기요 is perfectly fine and polite.

This is what you’d say in a restaurant to call a server or a waiter.
