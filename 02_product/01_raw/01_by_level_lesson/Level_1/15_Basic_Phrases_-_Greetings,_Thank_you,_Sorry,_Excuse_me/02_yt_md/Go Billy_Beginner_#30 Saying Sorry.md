### Apologizing for Your Actions

When you have done something wrong and need to apologize, Korean offers several levels of politeness. For close friends who are the same age or younger, the casual form **미안해** is used. When speaking to people you are close with but who are a similar age or older, you can use the polite forms **미안해요** or **죄송해요**. For formal situations or when speaking to strangers and superiors, the most formal options are **미안합니다** and **죄송합니다**.

The instructor highlights a nuance between the two main verbs: **죄송합니다** and **죄송해요** sound slightly more formal and polite than **미안합니다** and **미안해요**. Visually, the screen displays a hierarchy where **미안해** is at the top for close peers, followed by the polite **-요** endings, and finally the formal **-습니다** endings for general or high-stakes situations.

---

### Apologizing for Doing Something vs. Sympathy

A critical cultural and linguistic point is made regarding the use of these phrases: they specifically mean you are apologizing for an action you committed. The instructor provides an example: if a friend tells you their dog passed away, responding with **죄송합니다** would imply that you were responsible for the dog's death. In English, "I'm sorry" covers both apology and sympathy, but in Korean, these are distinct. To express "I'm sorry to hear that" or "Oh no," different expressions are required.

---

### Expressing Sympathy: "I'm Sorry to Hear That"

To show concern or sympathy without taking blame, Koreans use expressions derived from the verb **어떡하다 (to do what/how)**. For close friends, the casual expression is **어떡해**. For those requiring a polite level of speech, the form is **어떡해요**. These literally translate to "What will you (or I) do?" but function as "Oh no" or "I'm sorry to hear that."

The instructor demonstrates this through a conversation practice between two characters, Minwoo and Seoyoung. Minwoo says he wants to go to the park, but Seoyoung replies that she is very sad because she cannot go. Minwoo responds with **어떡해요?** to show he is sorry to hear about her situation.

---

### Additional Notes and Adverbs

The instructor provides further linguistic context by noting the root verbs for these expressions. **죄송합니다** and **죄송해요** come from the descriptive verb **죄송하다 (to be sorry)**, while **미안합니다**, **미안해요**, and **미안해** come from **미안하다 (to be sorry)**. 

To make an apology more sincere or intense, adverbs can be added before the phrase. Examples shown include:
- **너무 미안해.** / I am so sorry.
- **정말 죄송해요.** / I am really sorry.

For very minor mistakes among very close friends, a shortened version **미안** can be used, which is equivalent to saying "Oops," "My bad," or a quick "Sorry." Finally, for formal situations where one needs to say "I'm sorry to hear that" (such as in a professional or somber setting), the expression **유감입니다** is introduced as the appropriate formal choice.
