## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor explains that while English uses "Excuse me" as a catch-all phrase, Korean employs several distinct expressions depending on the specific social context. The lesson is divided into four primary scenarios: moving through a crowd, calling for service in a restaurant, getting a stranger's attention, and interrupting someone or asking for personal information. By understanding the literal meanings and nuances of these phrases, learners can navigate social interactions in Korea more naturally and politely.

---

### Squeezing Past People

When you are in a hurry and need to move through a crowded area, such as a busy subway station or a narrow sidewalk, the most common phrase is **잠시만요**. This literally translates to "just for a moment" and is used to ask people to yield space briefly. The instructor breaks down the word on screen: **잠시** (a moment), **만** (just), and **요** (polite ending).

To be more explicit about your intention to move, you can use **지나갈게요** or the more formal **지나가겠습니다**, both of which mean "I will pass by." These can be combined with **잠시만** to form **잠시만 지나갈게요** or **잠시만 지나가겠습니다** (I will pass by for a moment). If you are in an extreme hurry and find yourself having to lightly push or shove, the instructor recommends using **죄송합니다** (I'm sorry). This sounds the most apologetic and makes people more likely to be forgiving of the physical intrusion. These phrases are equally applicable on planes, public transportation, or any situation where someone is blocking your path.

---

### Calling Waiters and Staff

In a Korean restaurant, it is culturally acceptable and expected to call out to the staff when you are ready to order. The standard phrase is **여기요**, which literally means "here." By saying this, you are calling attention to your location and your need for assistance. A more respectful alternative is **사장님**, which literally means "boss." Even if the person serving you is not the actual owner, calling them **사장님** is a common way to show respect to service providers.

The instructor shares a "fun fact" about Korean dining: many large restaurants have a **호출벨** (summoning bell) on every table. Pressing this bell alerts the staff electronically, making it unnecessary to call out verbally. However, in smaller, more traditional restaurants, you will still need to use **여기요** or **사장님**. A typical interaction might begin with **여기요! 주문이요!** (Here! Order please!), to which a waiter might respond **네, 뭐 드릴까요?** (Yes, what can I get you?).

---

### Calling a Stranger

If you need to get the attention of someone you don't know—for example, if they dropped their phone on the street—the correct phrase is **저기요**. While **여기** means "here," **저기** means "over there." Using **저기요** is equivalent to saying "Excuse me" or "Hey you over there" in a non-rude way. It is the standard way to address a stranger whose name you do not know. Interestingly, **저기요** can also be used in restaurants as an alternative to **여기요**, since you are addressing a staff member whose name is unknown to you.

---

### Interrupting or Asking Personal Information

For situations that require a higher level of formal courtesy, such as interrupting someone's work or asking for a customer's personal details, the phrase **실례합니다** is used. The instructor explains the etymology using Hanja: **실** (lose) and **례** (courtesy/manners), together meaning a "lack of courtesy" or "disrespect." By saying **실례합니다**, you are proactively acknowledging that you are causing an inconvenience.

A common variation is **실례지만...**, which means "I'm sorry to bother you, but..." or "Excuse me, but..." This is frequently used in professional settings, such as a hotel staff member knocking on a door: **실례합니다. 주문하신 룸 서비스 식사 준비됐는데 들어가도 될까요?** (Excuse me. Your room service is ready; may I come in?). It is also used by retail staff when asking for a name to process a return: **실례지만 고객님 성함이 어떻게 되세요?** (Excuse me, but what is your name, customer?).
