## Teaching Points

### Overview

In this lesson, the instructor focuses on making noun-only responses polite using the markers 요 and 이요. While most textbooks prioritize full sentence construction with 예요 and 이에요, this lesson addresses the practical reality of daily conversation where speakers often give short, one-word answers. The instructor frames this as a way to remain "good-mannered" even when one is feeling "lazy" or wants to speak efficiently. Additionally, the lesson covers the demonstrative pronouns for "this," "that," and "it," explaining how they combine with the word for "thing" and how these combinations are shortened in natural spoken Korean.

---

### Making Nouns Polite with 요 and 이요

The instructor introduces 요 and 이요 as essential tools for making a standalone noun polite without needing a full verb conjugation. This is particularly useful for answering questions briefly. The rule for selection depends on the presence of a final consonant (받침) in the noun. If a noun ends in a vowel (no bottom letter), 요 is added. If a noun ends in a consonant (bottom letter), 이요 is added.

The instructor provides several examples to illustrate this distinction. When asked "Who is it?" or "Who are you?" (누구예요?), a speaker might want to simply answer "Me." In Korean, the polite word for "I/me" is 저. Since 저 ends in a vowel, the polite short response is 저요? (Me?). For nouns ending in consonants, such as 책 (book) or 컵 (cup), the speaker adds 이요, resulting in 책이요 (A book) or 컵이요 (A cup). This is contrasted with the full sentence forms like 책이에요 (It is a book) or 컵이에요 (It is a cup).

---

### Question Words and Conjugation

The lesson reviews the basic "5W1H" question words: 뭐 (what), 언제 (when), 어디 (where), 왜 (why), and 누구 (who). The instructor demonstrates how to conjugate these with the "to be" verb 예요/이에요. For example, "What is it?" becomes 뭐예요?, "Where is it?" is 어디예요?, and "When is it?" is 언제예요?. A notable exception is 왜 (why), which typically takes just 요 to become 왜요? rather than using the full 예요 conjugation. The instructor encourages learners to use 뭐예요? frequently in contexts like traditional markets to learn the names of new objects.

---

### Demonstratives: This, That, and It

The instructor explains the three-way distinction for demonstratives in Korean: 이 (this - near the speaker), 그 (it/the - near the listener or previously mentioned), and 저 (that - far from both). In Korean, these words cannot stand alone as pronouns like "this" does in English; they must be followed by a noun. To say "this thing," "that thing," or "it/that thing," they are combined with the word 것 (thing).

- **이것** / this thing
- **그것** / it/that thing (near you)
- **저것** / that thing (over there)

When these terms function as the topic of a sentence, they take the topic marker 은, becoming 이것은, 그것은, and 저것은.

---

### Shortened Forms in Spoken Korean

A major focus of the lesson is how these demonstrative phrases are shortened for natural speech. Just as English speakers use "wanna" instead of "want to," Korean speakers almost always use contracted forms of demonstratives in conversation.

- **이것은** becomes **이건** (This thing is...)
- **그것은** becomes **그건** (It/That thing is...)
- **저것은** becomes **저건** (That thing is...)

The instructor demonstrates these in sentences: 이건 뭐예요? (What is this?) and 그건 책이에요 (That is a book). He also notes that if you are referring to a person rather than a thing, you replace 것 (thing) with 사람 (person), resulting in 이 사람 (this person), 그 사람 (that person/the person), and 저 사람 (that person over there). These can also take the topic marker to become 이 사람은, 그 사람은, and 저 사람은.
