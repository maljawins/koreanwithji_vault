## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces the two primary ways to ask questions in Korean. The first type involves asking whether something is true or not, which are essentially yes/no questions. The second type involves using specific question words such as what, who, when, where, why, and how. This lesson focuses exclusively on the first type: yes/no questions.

The instructor highlights that forming questions in Korean is significantly simpler than in English because the word order does not change. In English, one must often swap the subject and verb or add auxiliary verbs, but in Korean, a statement is transformed into a question simply by changing the sentence ending and raising the intonation at the end of the sentence. The video covers six distinct types of question endings, ranging from formal to informal and direct to indirect.

---

### Basic Question Formation: Raising Intonation

The most fundamental way to ask a question in both 존댓말 (polite) and 반말 (casual) speech is to take a standard declarative sentence and raise the pitch at the end. The instructor demonstrates this with several examples. In the polite level, the statement `미국에서 왔어요 (I am from the U.S.)` becomes the question `미국에서 왔어요? (Are you from the U.S.?)` simply by raising the tone. Similarly, `한국을 좋아해요 (I like Korea)` becomes `한국을 좋아해요? (Do you like Korea?)`, and `한국어 잘해요 (I am good at Korean)` becomes `한국어 잘해요? (Are you good at Korean?)`.

In casual speech (반말), the same principle applies. The statement `점심 먹었어 (I ate lunch)` becomes `점심 먹었어? (Did you eat lunch?)`. Adjectives follow this rule as well; `피곤해 (I am tired)` becomes `피곤해? (Are you tired?)`, and `배고파 (I am hungry)` becomes `배고파? (Are you hungry?)`. The instructor notes that `배고파` comes from the adjective `배고프다`.

---

### Formal Questions with 습니까

The instructor explains that `습니까` is the question form of the highly formal `습니다` ending. While `습니다` is used for formal statements, `습니까` is used for formal questions. For example, the formal statement `아침을 먹었습니다 (I ate breakfast)` transforms into the question `아침을 먹었습니까? (Did you eat breakfast?)`. Similarly, `한국을 좋아합니다 (I like Korea)` becomes `한국을 좋아합니까? (Do you like Korea?)`.

The instructor clarifies that this form is rarely used in daily conversation because it sounds overly formal and stiff. It is typically reserved for professional settings like job interviews, press conferences, or news broadcasts. However, learners will frequently encounter it in Korean dramas or movies during formal scenes.

---

### Direct Questions with 니, 나요, and 가요

A more specific set of question endings includes `니` for casual speech and `나요/가요` for polite speech. These are direct questions asking for a specific answer. To form a `니` question in 반말, you take the verb stem and add `니`. For example, `미국에서 왔어?` can be asked as `미국에서 왔니?`. For `한국어 잘해?`, the stem of `하다` is used to create `한국어 잘하니?`. For adjectives like `배고프다` and `피곤하다`, the `다` is replaced with `니` to form `배고프니?` and `피곤하니?`.

In the polite 존댓말 version, the choice between `나요` and `가요` depends on the part of speech and tense. `가요` (specifically `ㄴ가요`) is used for present-tense adjectives. For example, `배고프다` becomes `배고픈가요?` and `피곤하다` becomes `피곤한가요?`. `나요` is used for present or past-tense verbs and past-tense adjectives. For example, `왔다` (past of `오다`) becomes `왔나요?`, and `잘하다` becomes `잘하나요?`.

The instructor provides a special rule for words containing `있다` or `없다` (such as `재미있다` or `재미없다`). Even though these function as adjectives, they always take the `나요` ending. Therefore, "Is Korean fun?" is asked as `한국어는 재미있나요?`.

---

### Indirect and Wondering Questions: ㄹ/을까, 가, and 나

The endings `ㄹ/을까`, `가`, and `나` are used for indirect questions where the speaker is "wondering" about something rather than directly demanding an answer from someone else. For example, `엄마는 아침을 먹었을까?` translates to "I wonder if mom ate breakfast?" This is distinct from the direct question `엄마는 아침을 먹었나요?` (Did mom eat breakfast?).

The choice between `ㄹ까` and `을까` depends on whether the verb stem ends in a consonant (받침). `먹다` becomes `먹었을까`, while `하다` becomes `할까`. Similarly, `가` is used for adjectives and `나` for verbs in this wondering sense. Examples include `학생들은 피곤한가?` (I wonder if the students are tired?), `아빠는 자나?` (I wonder if dad is sleeping?), and `날씨가 좋은가?` (I wonder if the weather is good?).

---

### Affirmative Questions with 지 and 조

The ending `지` (casual) or `지요/죠` (polite) is used when the speaker is asking for confirmation of something they already believe to be true. It is equivalent to adding "...right?" to the end of an English sentence.

- `너 한국인이지?` / You are Korean, right?
- `피곤하지?` / You are tired, right?
- `한국어 재미있지?` / Korean is fun, right?

To make these polite, you add `요` to get `피곤하지요?`. The instructor notes that `지요` is very frequently abbreviated to `죠`, resulting in `피곤하죠?`, `잘 지내죠?`, and `한국어 재미있죠?`.

---

### Suggestions with ㄹ/을까 and 래

The endings `ㄹ/을까` and `래` (or `래요/까요` in polite speech) carry a second meaning: making a suggestion or invitation. This is equivalent to "Shall we...?" or "Do you want to...?" in English.

- `공부할까?` or `공부할래?` / Shall we study? / Do you want to study?
- `갈까?` or `갈래?` / Shall we go? / Do you want to go?
- `만날까?` or `만날래?` / Shall we meet? / Do you want to meet?

The instructor explains that `래` is slightly more "inviting" than `까`, but they are largely interchangeable in a suggestive context. She also mentions the popular (and slightly aggressive but often cute) phrase `죽을래?` which literally means "Do you want to die?" but is used among friends when someone is being annoying.
