Let’s make this really clear.

Look at these two sentences:

**저는 동물이 좋아요. 하지만 (저는) 벌레는 싫어요.**

First, notice that **좋다** and **싫다** are both descriptive verbs (adjectives). Since **동물** is the subject of **좋다**, you need the subject marker **이** to indicate that. That’s all the subject marker does—it marks the subject of the verb. Simple.

But 은/는? That’s a little different.

1️⃣ **Topic Marking**
In the first sentence, **저는** introduces "me" as the topic of the conversation. That’s what **는** does—it marks the topic.

So, in the second sentence, you don’t need to repeat **저는** because it’s already clear that you’re still talking about yourself. If you did repeat it, it would sound unnatural and redundant.

2️⃣ **Contrast**
Another key function of 은/는 is to show contrast. And remember, **은/는** can be attached to any noun.

In **벌레는 싫어요**, the **는** in **벌레는** highlights a contrast—you like animals, but you don’t like bugs. That’s a nuance 은/는 naturally brings into the sentence.

Now, if you switch it around and say:

**저는 동물은 좋아요. 하지만 벌레가 싫어요.**

It still means the same thing, but now there’s more emphasis on **벌레** rather than **동물**, shifting the focus slightly to what you dislike.

And if you only say **저는 동물은 좋아요** without adding the second part, it would sound incomplete. You’d be expected to follow up with more information because 은/는 implies contrast.

Now, let’s look at these two sentences:

**저는 동물을 좋아해요. 하지만 (저는) 벌레를 싫어해요.**

Here, **좋아해요** and **싫어해요** are both action verbs, meaning "to like" and "to dislike." Since **동물** and **벌레** are objects, you need to use the object markers **을** and **를**.

Both sentences are perfectly fine as they are. However, you can make them sound more natural by replacing **를** with **는** in one of the sentences to show contrast.

So, you can say:
✔ **저는 동물은 좋아해요. 하지만 벌레를 싫어해요.**
✔ **저는 동물을 좋아해요. 하지만 벌레는 싫어해요.**

Both versions emphasize contrast but focus on different parts of the sentence.

Now, sometimes you can drop particles. And Koreans do this all the time. For example, you already know that you can omit **저는** in the second sentence because it's clear you're still talking about yourself.

But you can also drop just **는** in **저는** in the first sentence and simply say **저** because it's still obvious that you're the topic.

Likewise, you can drop **을** from **동물을** because it's clear that **동물** is the object.

So, instead of:
**저는 동물을 좋아해요.**

You can naturally say:
✔ **저 동물 좋아해요.**

Another example:
✔ **밥 먹었어?** ("Did you eat?")

Here, **밥** is obviously the object, so saying **밥을 먹었어?** would sound unnatural, almost like you're specifically asking, *"Is rice what you ate?"*

Now, let’s go back to our sentence:

✔ **저 동물 좋아해요. 하지만 벌레는 싫어해요.**

Notice that **는** in **벌레는** is showing contrast—it emphasizes that while you like animals, you don’t like bugs. So **는** cannot be omitted.

Likewise, if you use **은** in the first sentence:
✔ **저 동물은 좋아해요. 하지만 벌레를 싫어해요.**

This time, you do need this 를 in 벌레를 because **동물은** suggests contrast, so in the second sentence, you must specify what you dislike in contrast to what you like.

That’s why you can’t drop **를** in this case.

Unlike topic and object markers, **이/가** is a subject marker that highlights the subject of the verb. It answers **who**, **what**, or **which** in relation to the verb.

Since it marks the subject of the **VERB**, it’s generally not removable without changing the meaning of the sentence although there are times when you can drop 이/가, we’re not there yet. So for now, just play if safe.

So, to conclude, unless the particle is needed for emphasis or contrast, you can drop it to sound more natural.

So, we can say

저 동물 좋아해요 하지만 벌레**’는’** 싫어해요.

저 동물은좋아해요 하지만 벌레**’를’** 싫어해요

We’ll revisit particle omission after covering more particles in future lessons. For now, just remember: **if the noun’s role is obvious from context, the particle can often be omitted.**
