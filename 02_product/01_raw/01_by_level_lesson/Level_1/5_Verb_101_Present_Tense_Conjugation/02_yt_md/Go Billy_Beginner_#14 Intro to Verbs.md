## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces the fundamental concept of verbs in the Korean language, drawing parallels and highlighting key differences between English and Korean grammar. The lesson begins by defining a verb as an action or state of being—essentially anything someone or something does. While both languages use conjugation to change the form of a verb based on its usage in a sentence, Korean verbs follow a more streamlined system regarding subjects while maintaining complex rules for tense and politeness. The goal of this lesson is to familiarize students with the "dictionary form" of verbs and provide a basic set of conjugated verbs to begin forming simple sentences.

---

### The Dictionary Form and Action Verbs

In English, verbs in their base form are often preceded by the word "to," such as "to go" or "to eat." In Korean, every verb in its base or dictionary form ends with the syllable 다. This is a critical marker for learners; when searching for a word in a dictionary, if it ends in 다, it is almost certainly a verb. The instructor provides five essential examples of these base forms: 가다 (to go), 먹다 (to eat), 좋아하다 (to like), 보다 (to see), and 이다 (to be). Most of these are classified as "action verbs" because they describe a physical or mental activity. However, the instructor notes that 이다 (to be) is technically a "descriptive verb" because it does not describe an action, a distinction that becomes more important in later lessons.

---

### Conjugation Basics and Subject Consistency

Conjugation refers to changing the form of a verb to reflect how it is used. In English, verbs change based on the subject—for instance, "I go" versus "he goes." Korean verbs are unique because they do not change based on the person performing the action. Whether the subject is "I," "he," "she," or "Billy," the verb conjugation remains identical. For example, the conjugated verb 수영해요 (swim/swims) is used for both 저는 수영해요 (I swim) and 빌리는 수영해요 (Billy swims). While Korean verbs do not change for the subject, they do conjugate for different tenses, such as the past tense 수영했어요 (swam), the future tense 수영할 거예요 (will swim), or the present progressive 수영하고 있어요 (am swimming).

---

### Standard Present Tense Conjugations

For this introductory lesson, the instructor provides the standard polite present tense forms for the five core verbs introduced earlier. Learners are encouraged to memorize these specific transformations before the underlying grammatical rules are explained in future videos. The transformations are as follows:

- **가다 (to go)** becomes **가요**
- **먹다 (to eat)** becomes **먹어요**
- **좋아하다 (to like)** becomes **좋아요**
- **보다 (to see)** becomes **봐요**
- **이다 (to be)** becomes **이에요** or **예요** (depending on whether the preceding word ends in a consonant or vowel)

---

### Sentence Structure and the Object Marker

The instructor emphasizes that Korean follows a Subject-Object-Verb (SOV) sentence structure, which differs from the English Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) pattern. To say "I eat pizza" in Korean, the literal word order must be "I pizza eat." Placing the verb before the object, such as saying "저는 먹어요 피자," is grammatically incorrect. Furthermore, when a verb acts upon an object, an "object marker" is attached to that object. This marker is 을 if the word ends in a consonant and 를 if it ends in a vowel. The instructor demonstrates this with several practice sentences:

- **저는 가요.** / I go.
- **저는 피자를 먹어요.** / I eat pizza.
- **저는 김치를 좋아해요.** / I like kimchi.
- **저는 빌리를 봐요.** / I see Billy.
- **저는 빌리예요.** / I am Billy.

In these examples, the syllables 를 and 을 (highlighted on screen in different colors) serve to identify the target of the action. While this is a brief introduction, the instructor notes that mastering the SOV order and the object marker is essential for building natural Korean sentences.
