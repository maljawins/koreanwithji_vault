## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the fundamental rules for conjugating Korean verbs and adjectives into the polite present tense, commonly known as the ~요 form. The lesson is structured to move from basic mechanical steps to more complex vowel contractions and irregular patterns. By the end of the video, learners should understand how to transform a dictionary-form word into a natural spoken expression suitable for most social situations.

The core of the lesson revolves around identifying the "last vowel" of a word stem to determine which ending to attach. While the basic rules are straightforward, the instructor emphasizes that many common words undergo contractions or follow irregular paths, particularly those ending in specific vowels like ㅡ or consonants like ㅂ.

---

### Basic Conjugation Rules
The instructor outlines a three-step process for basic conjugation. First, remove the dictionary ending 다 from the verb or adjective. Second, check the last vowel of the remaining stem. If that vowel is ㅗ or ㅏ, add 아. If the vowel is anything else, add 어. Third, add the polite marker 요 to the end.

The use of 요 depends on the social context. It is required when speaking politely to strangers, elders, or those in higher positions. However, it can be omitted (leaving the word in 반말 or casual speech) when talking to close friends of the same age or younger, small children, and animals. The instructor demonstrates this with the verb 먹다 (to eat), which becomes 먹어 (casual) or 먹어요 (polite) because the vowel is ㅓ, and 좋다 (to be good), which becomes 좋아 (casual) or 좋아요 (polite) because the vowel is ㅗ.

---

### Vowel Contractions and Combinations
When a verb stem ends in a vowel without a bottom consonant (받침), the attached 아 or 어 often combines with the stem rather than staying separate. For example, 가다 (to go) technically conjugates to 가아, but because this sounds repetitive, it simplifies to just 가 (or 가요). Similarly, 오다 (to come) combines with 아 to become 와 (or 와요).

This rule also applies to stems ending in ㅜ or ㅣ. The verb 주다 (to give) combines with 어 to become 줘. Verbs ending in ㅣ, such as 마시다 (to drink) or 가르치다 (to teach), change the ㅣ to ㅕ when combined with 어, resulting in 마셔 and 가르쳐. These contractions are the standard way to speak and write in modern Korean.

---

### The ㅡ Vowel and 르 Irregulars
Words ending in the vowel ㅡ require looking back one syllable to determine the conjugation. For a word like 바쁘다 (to be busy), removing 다 leaves 바쁘. Since the stem ends in ㅡ, the instructor looks at the previous syllable 바. Because that syllable contains ㅏ, the ending attached is 아, resulting in 바빠 (or 바빠요). If the previous syllable does not contain ㅗ or ㅏ, such as in 예쁘다 (to be pretty), the ending is 어, resulting in 예뻐 (or 예뻐요). If there is no previous syllable, as in 쓰다 (to write/use), it defaults to adding 어 to become 써.

A special sub-rule exists for words ending in 르, like 모르다 (to not know). When the ㅡ is removed, the ㄹ doubles. One ㄹ stays at the bottom of the previous syllable, and the other starts the next syllable. Since the previous syllable 모 contains ㅗ, the ending is 아, resulting in 몰라 (or 몰라요).

---

### The ㅂ Irregular and Special Verbs
Many adjectives ending in the consonant ㅂ follow an irregular pattern. For a word like 부럽다 (to be envious), the ㅂ is removed and replaced with 우. This 우 then conjugates normally by adding 어, which combines to form 워. Thus, 부럽다 becomes 부러워 (or 부러워요). The instructor notes this typically applies to adjectives; action verbs ending in ㅂ, like 씹다 (to chew), often conjugate regularly as 씹어.

The lesson concludes with three high-frequency irregulars. First, 하다 (to do) always conjugates to 해 (or 해요). Second, 이다 (to be) becomes 이에요 after a consonant or 예요 after a vowel (이야/야 in casual speech). Third, 아니다 (to not be) always becomes 아니에요 regardless of the preceding sound (아니야 in casual speech).
