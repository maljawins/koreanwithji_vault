## Lesson Overview
This lesson focuses on three core pillars of Korean grammar: the nuanced use of particles, the structure of questions, and the foundational rules of verb conjugation. Ji begins by addressing common confusion surrounding subject markers (이/가) and topic markers (은/는), emphasizing that their usage is often a matter of "feeling" and intuition rather than rigid rules. The lesson explores how these particles change the focus and emphasis of a sentence, particularly through the "iPhone" example, and explains when particles can be naturally omitted in conversation.

The second part of the lesson covers question formation. Unlike English, Korean maintains a consistent Subject-Object-Verb (SOV) word order for both statements and questions. Ji introduces various question words (who, when, where, what, how, why) and explains their placement within a sentence. The lesson also touches upon question-specific context markers and the difference between formal and informal ways to say "yes" and "no."

Finally, the lesson introduces regular verb conjugation using a "plant analogy" (Stem, Branch, Leaf, and Fruit). Students learn how to conjugate verbs in the present tense based on two primary factors: the vowel of the verb stem (ㅗ and ㅏ vs. all others) and the presence or absence of a final consonant (받침). Through model verbs like 남다, 좋다, 가다, 보다, and 적다, Ji demonstrates how these rules apply across different speech levels, including informal low, informal high, and formal high.

### Slide 3~7 — Particles: Contrast and Verb Types
- **On screen:** Examples comparing descriptive and action verbs. 
    - **Slide 3-4:** 저는 동물이 좋아요 (I like animals) vs. 하지만 벌레는 싫어요 (But I don't like bugs). Color-coded: 는/은 in green (Topic/Contrast), 이/가 in yellow (Subject Marker). Arrows point from the descriptive verb box to the sentences.
    - **Slide 5-7:** 저는 동물을 좋아해요 (I like animals) vs. 하지만 벌레를 싫어해요 (But I don't like bugs). Color-coded: 를/을 in yellow (Object Marker). Arrows point from the action verb box.
- **Ji's teaching:** You still seem to have some confusion about particles. Like I mentioned before, even advanced speakers struggle with 은/는 and 이/가. So, don’t try to force yourself to memorize when to use which one. Instead, focus on listening to how they’re naturally used and let your intuition develop over time. Let’s make this really clear. Look at these two sentences: **저는 동물이 좋아요. 하지만 (저는) 벌레는 싫어요.** First, notice that **좋다** and **싫다** are both descriptive verbs (adjectives). Since **동물** is the subject of **좋다**, you need the subject marker **이** to indicate that. That’s all the subject marker does—it marks the subject of the verb. Simple. But 은/는? That’s a little different. 1. Topic Marking: In the first sentence, **저는** introduces "me" as the topic of the conversation. That’s what **는** does—it marks the topic. So, in the second sentence, you don’t need to repeat **저는** because it’s already clear that you’re still talking about yourself. If you did repeat it, it would sound unnatural and redundant. 2. Contrast: Another key function of 은/는 is to show contrast. And remember, **은/는** can be attached to any noun. In **벌레는 싫어요**, the **는** in **벌레는** highlights a contrast—you like animals, but you don’t like bugs. That’s a nuance 은/는 naturally brings into the sentence. Now, if you switch it around and say: **저는 동물은 좋아요. 하지만 벌레가 싫어요.** It still means the same thing, but now there’s more emphasis on **벌레** rather than **동물**, shifting the focus slightly to what you dislike. And if you only say **저는 동물은 좋아요** without adding the second part, it would sound incomplete. You’d be expected to follow up with more information because 은/는 implies contrast. Now, let’s look at these two sentences: **저는 동물을 좋아해요. 하지만 (저는) 벌레를 싫어해요.** Here, **좋아해요** and **싫어해요** are both action verbs, meaning "to like" and "to dislike." Since **동물** and **벌레** are objects, you need to use the object markers **을** and **를**. Both sentences are perfectly fine as they are. However, you can make them sound more natural by replacing **를** with **는** in one of the sentences to show contrast. So, you can say: **저는 동물은 좋아해요. 하지만 벌레를 싫어해요.** or **저는 동물을 좋아해요. 하지만 벌레는 싫어해요.** Both versions emphasize contrast but focus on different parts of the sentence.

### Slide 8~12 — When Can You Omit Markers?
- **On screen:** Examples of sentences with particles crossed out or highlighted. 
    - **Slide 8:** 저(는) 동물(을) 좋아해요 → 저 동물 좋아해요.
    - **Slide 9:** 밥(을) 먹었어? (Is rice what you ate?).
    - **Slide 10:** 저(는) 동물은 좋아해요. 하지만 벌레를 싫어해요.
    - **Slide 11-12:** Summary rules. Subject markers (이/가) can't be easily removed. 은/는 and 을/를 can't be removed when showing contrast.
- **Ji's teaching:** Sometimes you can drop particles. Koreans do this all the time. For example, you already know that you can omit **저는** in the second sentence because it's clear you're still talking about yourself. But you can also drop just **는** in **저는** in the first sentence and simply say **저** because it's still obvious that you're the topic. Likewise, you can drop **을** from **동물을** because it's clear that **동물** is the object. So, instead of **저는 동물을 좋아해요**, you can naturally say **저 동물 좋아해요.** Another example: **밥 먹었어?** ("Did you eat?"). Here, **밥** is obviously the object, so saying **밥을 먹었어?** would sound unnatural, almost like you're specifically asking, "Is rice what you ate?" Now, let’s go back to our sentence: **저 동물 좋아해요. 하지만 벌레는 싫어해요.** Notice that **는** in **벌레는** is showing contrast—it emphasizes that while you like animals, you don’t like bugs. So **는** cannot be omitted. Likewise, if you use **은** in the first sentence: **저 동물은 좋아해요. 하지만 벌레를 싫어해요.** This time, you do need this **를** in **벌레를** because **동물은** suggests contrast, so in the second sentence, you must specify what you dislike in contrast to what you like. That’s why you can’t drop **를** in this case. Unlike topic and object markers, **이/가** is a subject marker that highlights the subject of the verb. It answers who, what, or which in relation to the verb. Since it marks the subject of the verb, it’s generally not removable without changing the meaning of the sentence—although there are times when you can drop **이/가**, we’re not there yet. So for now, just play it safe. To conclude, unless the particle is needed for emphasis or contrast, you can drop it to sound more natural. If the noun’s role is obvious from context, the particle can often be omitted.

### Slide 13~18 — FEEL 은/는 & 이/가 (The iPhone Example)
- **On screen:** Six variations of the sentence "My friend's phone is an iPhone" with different particle combinations.
    - **Slide 13:** 제 친구(의) 핸드폰은 아이폰이에요. (General statement).
    - **Slide 14:** 제 친구(의) 핸드폰이 아이폰이에요. (Emphasis on "whose phone").
    - **Slide 15:** 제 친구는 핸드폰이 아이폰이에요. (Emphasis on "iPhone, not Samsung").
    - **Slide 16:** 제 친구가 핸드폰이 아이폰이에요. (Emphasis on "HE" has an "IPHONE").
    - **Slide 17:** 제 친구는 핸드폰은 아이폰이에요. (Suggests contrast, e.g., "but his PC is not a Mac").
    - **Slide 18:** 제 친구가 핸드폰은 아이폰이에요. (Emphasis on "My friend" + contrast).
- **Ji's teaching:** You need to *feel* the difference between these particles. That’s why I’ll show you multiple sentence examples so you can get a sense of their nuance and vibe. All of these sentences essentially mean the same thing, but because different particles are used, the nuance changes. The sentence we’re working with is: "My friend’s cell phone is an iPhone." By the way, this is a great example of how a sentence can sound completely natural in Korean but feel awkward when directly translated into English. Saying "My friend’s cell phone is iPhone" sounds strange in English, so a more natural translation would be: "My friend has an iPhone." I’ve capitalized certain words in the English translations to show where you would naturally raise your voice or emphasize words to match the nuance in Korean. **제 친구의 핸드폰은 아이폰이에요.** This is a neutral, general statement. No particular emphasis on any word. You can also drop **의** and say **제 친구 핸드폰** since it’s clear from context. **제 친구의 핸드폰이 아이폰이에요.** Here, **이** marks **핸드폰** as the subject, emphasizing *whose* phone is an iPhone. It answers the question: *Whose phone is an iPhone?* **제 친구는 핸드폰이 아이폰이에요.** Here, **이** marks **핸드폰** as the subject, emphasizing *what* kind of phone they have. This sentence subtly contrasts iPhone vs. other phones (e.g., not a Samsung). **제 친구가 핸드폰이 아이폰이에요.** This sentence is very similar to the previous sentence but here, you’re placing emphasis on my friend too. So it would sound like “My **FRIEND** here has an **IPHONE**.” **제 친구는 핸드폰은 아이폰이에요.** This one sounds like “My friend **DOES** have an iPhone.” **은** after **핸드폰** implies some sort of contrast. There’s a lot of possibilities here—they might have established that an Android is better than an iPhone, or maybe he has an iPhone for his phone but a PC for his computer, not a Mac. **제 친구가 핸드폰은 아이폰이에요.** Again, this sentence is very similar to the previous one but **가** after **제 친구** also places emphasis on my friend. So it sounds like “**MY FRIEND DOES** have an iPhone.” In English, you adjust intonation to emphasize words; in Korean, you use particles instead.

### Slide 19~22 — Questions in Korean
- **On screen:** 
    - **Slide 19:** Comparison of English (word order changes) vs. Korean (word order stays the same: SOV).
    - **Slide 20:** Changes for questions: Speech Type (Formal high: 하십니까?) and Question Endings/Context markers (지?, 니?, 나요?).
    - **Slide 21:** Examples: 고양이 좋아해? and 고양이 좋아해요? (Omission of "you" and object markers).
    - **Slide 22:** Answers: 반말 (응/어, 아니) and 존댓말 (네/예, 아니오). Note on 아니오 (Simple No) vs. 아니에요 (Verb conjugation).
- **Ji's teaching:** Unlike in English where the word order has to change to ask questions, the word order in Korean stays the same. Korean keeps the same S-O-V (Subject-Object-Verb) structure. So instead of "Do you like cats?", in Korean, it’s literally "You cats like?" Let’s start with simple Yes or No questions. The structure stays the same but there can be slight changes in the verb ending. There are two key factors: Speech type respect level and context markers. Korean has context markers specifically for questions like **니?**, **나요?**, **지?**, **ㄹ까?**. For example, **고양이 좋아하지?** means "You like cats, right?" We’ll go over context markers later. I’m also going to skip formal high speech types for questions and honorifics for now. **고양이를 좋아해** and **(아미라 씨는) 고양이(를) 좋아해요.** Familiar, right? The first one is informal low and the second one is informal high. All you need to do to change your sentence to a question is just add a question mark at the end and raise your tone. Since I’m asking “YOU”, I can omit **(너는)** or **(아미라씨는)** and also drop **(를)** because **고양이** is obviously the object. You would answer with **네** or **아니요** in 존댓말, or **응** or **아니** in 반말. By the way, there are two ways to say “No” in Korean: **아니오** is just plain “No,” while **아니에요** is the conjugated form of the verb **아니다** (to be not).

### Slide 23~25 — Question Words
- **On screen:** A table of question words: 누구/누가 (Who), 언제 (When), 어디 (Where), 무엇/뭐/무슨 (What), 어느 (Which), 어떻게 (How), 어떤 (Which kind), 얼마/얼마나/얼만큼 (How much), 왜 (Why).
    - **Slide 23:** Details for Who, When, Where.
    - **Slide 24:** Details for What, How, How much.
    - **Slide 25:** Details for Why. Note: Reason comes first in Korean. 왜 can be at the start or before the verb.
- **Ji's teaching:** Question words go exactly where they belong in a sentence—whether as a subject, object, adverb, or adjective. So instead of “Who do you like?” in English, in Korean, it’s “You who like?” **누구** means "who." It can be a subject or an object. **누구야? 누구세요?** means “Who are you?” or “Who is it?” When you want to ask "Who do you like?", it’s **누구(를) 좋아해?**. But when “who” is the subject, **누구** changes to **누가** (**누구** + **가**). **누가 울어?** means "Who is crying?" **언제** means "when" and is treated like a noun. **그게 언제야?** means “When is that?” Since it's a noun, it can be a subject: **언지가 좋아?** means “When is good?” **어디** is "where." You can add location particles: **어디에?** (At where?), **어디에서?** (From where?), **어디로?** (To where?). **무엇** means "what," but in conversation, it's usually shortened to **뭐**. **무슨** is an adjective, like "what movie" (**무슨 영화**). **어떻게** means "how" (method). Adverbs are typically placed right before the verb: **한국어 공부 어떻게 해?** **얼마** is "how much" (price). **얼마나** or **얼만큼** are adverbs for quantity: **얼마나 먹었어요?** (How much did you eat?). Lastly, **왜** means “why.” It can be at the very beginning (**왜 고양이를 좋아하세요?**) or right before the verb (**고양이를 왜 좋아하세요?**). In Korean, we typically say the reason first.

### Slide 26~29 — Verbs: Plant Analogy and 하다
- **On screen:** 
    - **Slide 26:** Regular verb conjugation rules. Stem + Branch + Leaf + Fruit. Considerations: Vowel of the stem (ㅗ/ㅏ vs. others) and 받침.
    - **Slide 27:** **하다** conjugation table. It is in its own category.
    - **Slide 28:** Diagram of a branch. Stem (**하**), Branch/Honorific (**시**), Leaf/Context (**여**), Fruit/Respect (**요**). **하 + 시 + 여 + 요 = 하셔요/하세요**.
    - **Slide 29:** **하셔요** (3rd person) vs. **하세요** (2nd person/You).
- **Ji's teaching:** We’re finally going to dive into verbs using the plant analogy: Stem, Branch, Leaf, and Fruit. We briefly touched on the **하다** verb. **하세요** is an honorific present tense of **하다** in informal high speech, a combination of **하+시+어+요**. This can be **하셔요** or **하세요**. Both are correct, but **하셔요** is more for the 3rd person, while **하세요** is more natural when addressing "YOU" directly. **하다** is the only verb that has **여** as its branch. Today, we’ll learn regular verbs. There are two things to look at: the vowel of the stem and the **받침** of the stem.

### Slide 30~34 — Verbs with ㅗ and ㅏ (남다)
- **On screen:** 
    - **Slide 30:** Rule: If the stem has ㅗ or ㅏ, the branch is **아**. If there is a **받침**, add **아**. If no **받침**, the branch merges.
    - **Slide 31-33:** Conjugation of **남다** (To stay/remain). Stem: **남**.
        - Informal low: **남 + 아 = 남아**.
        - Informal high: **남 + 아 + 요 = 남아요**.
        - Formal high: **남 + 습니다 = 남습니다**.
    - **Slide 34:** Other verbs like **남다**: **닫다** (close), **밟다** (step on), **찾다** (look for).
- **Ji's teaching:** For verbs that have **ㅗ** and **ㅏ** in the stem, the branch is **아**. **남다** means to stay or be left over. The stem is **남**, which has a **ㅏ** vowel and a **ㅁ 받침**. We remove **다** and add the branch **아**, so it becomes **남아**. **왜 너는 여기 남아?** (Why do you stay here?). For informal high, just add **요**: **남아요**. For formal high, the ending takes over everything. When the stem has a **받침**, you simply add **습니다**, regardless of the vowel: **남습니다**.

### Slide 35~38 — Verbs with ㅗ and ㅏ (좋다)
- **On screen:** 
    - **Slide 35-37:** Conjugation of **좋다** (To be good). Stem: **좋**.
        - Informal low: **좋아**.
        - Informal high: **좋아요**.
        - Formal high: **좋습니다**.
    - **Slide 38:** Other verbs like **좋다**: **놓다** (put down), **볶다** (stir-fry), **옳다** (be right).
- **Ji's teaching:** **좋다** follows the same rule as **남다** because it has the **ㅗ** vowel and a **받침**. **너는 누가 좋아?** (Who do you like?). **나는 네가 좋아.** (I like you). For formal high, it becomes **좋습니다**. **컴퓨터가 정말 좋습니다.** (The computer is really good).

### Slide 39~42 — Verbs with ㅏ and no 받침 (가다)
- **On screen:** 
    - **Slide 39-41:** Conjugation of **가다** (To go). Stem: **가**.
        - Informal low: **가 + 아 = 가** (redundant **아** is cancelled).
        - Informal high: **가요**.
        - Formal high: **가 + ㅂ니다 = 갑니다**.
    - **Slide 42:** Other verbs like **가다**: **차다** (kick), **자다** (sleep), **사다** (buy).
- **Ji's teaching:** The stem of **가다** is **가**. Since **가** and **아** are the same sound, the branch **아** is cancelled out. You’re left with just **가**. **어디 가?** (Where are you going?). For informal high, add **요**: **가요**. If the stem has no **받침**, the formal high ending is **ㅂ니다**. This **ㅂ** becomes the **받침** of the stem: **갑니다**.

### Slide 43~46 — Verbs with ㅗ and no 받침 (보다)
- **On screen:** 
    - **Slide 43-45:** Conjugation of **보다** (To see/look/watch). Stem: **보**.
        - Informal low: **보 + 아 = 봐** (merges).
        - Informal high: **봐요**.
        - Formal high: **봅니다**.
    - **Slide 46:** Other verbs like **보다**: **오다** (come), **나오다** (come out), **쏘다** (shoot).
- **Ji's teaching:** In **보다**, the **아** merges with the stem **보** and becomes **봐**. **어떤 영화를 봐?** (Which movie do you watch?). Informal high is **봐요**. Formal high is **봅니다** because **보** has no **받침**.

### Slide 47~51 — Verbs with other vowels (적다)
- **On screen:** 
    - **Slide 47:** Rule: If the stem has vowels other than ㅗ or ㅏ, the branch is **어**.
    - **Slide 48-50:** Conjugation of **적다** (To write down / To be little). Stem: **적**.
        - Informal low: **적 + 어 = 적어**.
        - Informal high: **적어요**.
        - Formal high: **적습니다**.
    - **Slide 51:** Other verbs like **적다**: **있다** (exist), **먹다** (eat), **입다** (wear).
- **Ji's teaching:** When the stem ends in a vowel other than **ㅏ** or **ㅗ**, the branch is **어**. **적다** can mean "to write down" or "little/few." **국이 너무 적어.** (The soup is too little). Informal high is **적어요**. Since **적** has a **받침**, formal high is **적습니다**.

## Teaching Points
- **Particle Nuance:** Particles like **은/는** and **이/가** are used to change emphasis rather than just marking grammar. **은/는** is primarily for topic marking and showing contrast. **이/가** marks the subject of the verb and answers "who" or "what."
- **Particle Omission:** Particles can be omitted when the context makes the noun's role obvious. However, they *cannot* be dropped if they are serving a specific purpose like showing contrast or emphasis.
- **Question Structure:** Korean questions maintain the same SOV word order as statements. Questions are indicated by rising intonation and specific verb endings or context markers (e.g., **니?**, **지?**).
- **Verb Conjugation (The Plant Analogy):**
    - **Stem:** The base of the verb (remove **다**).
    - **Branch:** The tense/honorific suffix. If the stem vowel is **ㅗ** or **ㅏ**, the branch is **아**. For all other vowels, it is **어**.
    - **Fruit:** The respect level (e.g., **요** for informal high).
    - **Formal High:** The ending **습니다** (with **받침**) or **ㅂ니다** (without **받침**) takes over the branch and fruit positions.
- **Question Words:** Words like **누구** (who), **어디** (where), and **뭐** (what) function as nouns or adverbs and stay in their natural position within the SOV structure. **누구** becomes **누가** when it is the subject of the sentence.
