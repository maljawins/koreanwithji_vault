## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor addresses a common point of confusion for Korean learners: the difference between `아니요` and `아니에요`. While they sound similar and both originate from the same root verb, `아니다 (to not be)`, they serve entirely different grammatical functions. The video aims to clarify when to use each by breaking down their structure, conjugation, and social usage.

The learning arc begins with the definition of the root verb and moves into the specific categorization of `아니요` as a standalone exclamation versus `아니에요` as a conjugated verb form. The instructor also covers essential grammar rules regarding particle usage and provides a practical dialogue example to demonstrate how both terms can appear in a single conversation.

---

### 아니요 — The Standalone "No"
The word `아니요` (or the casual version `아니`) is a complete phrase on its own. It functions as a full sentence meaning "No." Because it is an exclamation or a standalone response, it cannot be further conjugated. You cannot add verb endings to `아니요` or `아니`. It is simply used to negate a statement or answer a question before moving on to the rest of your sentence. For example, if someone asks if you are Korean, you might start your response with `아니요` to provide an immediate "No."

---

### 아니에요 — The Conjugated Verb
In contrast, `아니에요` is one of many possible conjugations of the descriptive verb `아니다 (to not be)`. It means "is not," "am not," or "are not." Unlike the standalone "No," this form is part of a sentence structure. Because it is a verb conjugation, it changes based on tense and politeness level. The instructor shows several variations on screen: `아니야` (casual present), `아니었어요` (polite past), and `아니네요` (polite spontaneous realization). While the casual form `아니야` contains the sound "ani," it is grammatically distinct from the standalone `아니`.

---

### Subject Markers and Particle Usage
When using `아니에요` or any conjugation of `아니다`, the noun that "is not" something should be marked with the subject marker `이/가`. The instructor recommends using `이` after a consonant and `가` after a vowel to make the sentence clear, though they are sometimes omitted in casual speech.

> **저는 한국 사람이 아니에요.** / I am not a Korean person.

The instructor also demonstrates that other particles like `도 (also/even/neither)` can be used with `아니다`. On screen, he shows a complex sentence: `그게 한국어도 아니고 영어도 아니야` (That thing is neither Korean nor English). Here, `아니다` appears in two forms: the connective `아니고` and the casual ending `아니야`.

A critical grammatical warning is provided: `아니다` is a descriptive verb, not an action verb. Therefore, you can never use the object marker `을/를` with it. The instructor visually crosses out the incorrect sentence `김치를 아니에요` and corrects it to:

> **김치가 아니에요.** / It is not kimchi.

---

### Social Usage: Humility and "You're Welcome"
Beyond its literal meaning of "is not," `아니에요` has a specific pragmatic use in Korean culture. It is the standard way to respond to compliments or expressions of thanks. When someone compliments your Korean skills by saying `한국어 잘 하시네요` (Your Korean is really good), responding with `아니에요` functions as a humble "No, no, I'm not that good." The instructor strongly recommends using this instead of a direct "Thank you" to appear more humble. Similarly, when someone says "Thank you," `아니에요` functions as "No problem" or "You're welcome."

---

### Practical Dialogue Example
The instructor concludes with a dialogue that uses both terms to highlight their different roles:

- **A: 한국어 잘 하시네요.** (Your Korean is really good.)
- **B: 하하 아니에요.** (Haha, no [I'm not/don't mention it].)
- **A: 한국 사람이에요?** (Are you Korean?)
- **B: 아니요. 저는 한국 사람이 아니에요.** (No. I am not a Korean person.)

In this exchange, the first `아니에요` is a humble response to a compliment. The `아니요` is a standalone "No" answering a direct question. The final `아니에요` is the conjugated verb meaning "am not."
