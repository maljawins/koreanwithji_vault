**Context Markers**

First, a quick reminder: these 'context markers' can only be used in informal speech, when you're using **반말 (banmal)**, and the **~요 form**.

The formal speech ending, **~ㅂ/습니다**, is a **context marker of its own.** The idea is that it already marks the “formal” context. **And** you **cannot** have more than one context marker at a time.

And this means you can’t add emotions to your sentences in formal speech. So, don’t overuse this form ‘cause you’ll sound like a robot.

**~네(요)**

Let’s start with 네요. **~네(요)** shows your **immediate reaction** to something you've *just* realized, or something that's *just happened*. It's a lot like saying 'Oh!' or 'Wow!

And the keyword here is “Just now” This is important because we have another form that also shows realization in a slightly different way.

The rule is simple. All you have to do is just attach 네요 after the stem.

One exception though with the ㄹ verbs, like 살다

You first remove ㄹ then add 네요. So 살다 to live becomes 사네요.

You can also use this with the past tense stem, so 하다 would be 했네요.

Now, with 이다, after a consonant, you’d use **이네요 (ineyo)**.

'Oh, we're home now!' – **'이제 집이네요.'** You just realized you’re almost home

**After a vowel:** you just use **네요 (neyo)**. 'Oh, she's a good friend!' – **'좋은 친구네요.' You can say this when** you just found out something good about a friend.

**~네(요) Comprehensible Input**

Let’s look at more examples

Son spots the fish first and goes 여기 물고기가 있네요 like “Oh, there’s fish here!”. He JUST saw them and he’s expressing that immediate realization.

Now, the dad looks, and he reacts to what his son just said and also sees the fish himself.

He replies: **'그렇네. 물고기가 많네**

First, **'그렇네' (geureone)**. This is a common way to agree with someone. It literally means “Yeah, it is so.” But used like “That’s right”

Then, he adds, **'물고기가 많네.'** He just realized, *in that moment*, that there are **a lot of them!**

So, the dad also has an immediate realization about the quantity of fish he's seeing right then and there.

Here’s another dialogue.

A says: **'와, 날씨 진짜 좋네!**

A has just noticed how beautiful the day is right at that moment.

Then B agrees '그렇네!’ and goes 우리 만날까?' 'Should we meet up?'

Now, A responds ‘좋은 생각이네.’ Expressing immediate realization that B's suggestion to meet is, in fact, a good idea. And says ‘이따 전화해’ Call me later

So you can see how **~네(요)** pops up naturally when people are reacting to things they've just observed, or quickly agreeing with something in the moment. It adds a very natural, conversational feel.

**~구나 / 군(요)**

Now, THESE are the context markers that also express realization. **~구나, ~구만 and ~군(요)**

The key difference though is that **this one sounds like you've figured something out *after giving it some thought.***

It's not an instantaneous, immediate reaction like **~네(요)**

In English, it has a similar vibe to “I see” or “Aha” rather than just a quick “Oh!”

Now, ~구나 ~구만 and 군. They’re all 반말, casual speech.

But ~구만 and 군 sound a bit more like you're **talking to yourself.** They’re pretty much used interchangeably.

And for 존댓말, polite speech, it’s only ~군요. 구나요 or 구만요 is incorrect.

The way you attach this ending is a little different depending on whether you have a **Descriptive Verb** or an **Action Verb**

**For descriptive verbs, you simply attach one of these endings directly to the verb stem.**

예쁘구나 / 재밌구나.

For action verbs, you need to insert **는 (~neun)** before **구나 / 군 / 군요**

So, you attach **~는구나 (~neunguna)**, **~는군 (~neungun)**, or **~는군요 (~neungunyo)** to the verb stem. And they’re not 받침 sensitive which is why you get 가는구나 and 먹는구나

Irregular ㄹ again. **Remove the ㄹ 받침 from the stem then attach 는구나**

So, **살다 (salda - to live)** becomes **사는구나**

~지 않다 (Negative Form)

For **Descriptive Verbs** in the negative (e.g., ~지 않다): You attach **~구나 (~guna)** directly to **않**. So, if a house is not big, and you realize it after thinking, you'd say: **'집이 그렇게 크지 않구나.' (Jibi geureoke keuji ankuna.)** – 'I see that the house is not so big.

For **Action Verbs** in the negative (e.g., ~지 않다): You attach **~는구나 (~neunguna)** to **않**. So, if you realize someone doesn't live in Seoul, you'd say: **'서울에 살지 않는구나.' (Seoure salji anneunguna.)** – 'I see that he/she doesn't live in Seoul.

But in the past tense, you **don't need to differentiate between action verbs and descriptive verbs**

You simply attach **~구나 (~guna) / ~군 (~gun) / ~군요 (~gunyo)** directly to the **past tense stem**.

**~구나 Comprehensible Input**

Now, let's look at this scenario that clearly distinguishes ~네(요) from ~구나 / ~군(요)

There’s two people entering a busy restaurant.

A goes: 이 식당은 사람이 많네요 Oh, there's a lot of people in this restaurant.

**He’s using 네요 to** express his **immediate observation** about the restaurant *right now like* – 'Wow, look at all these people!'"

Then B goes ‘저기 무슨 포스터가 있네요 – “Oh, they have some kind of poster over there.”

Again, Person B uses **~네요** he just noticed the poster in that moment.

Now, Person A processes this new information about the poster and connects it with their earlier observation and then says : “아~ TV에 나와서 그렇구나’ “Ah I see They were on TV, that’s why”

This is what 구나 does. He is **connecting two pieces of information** (many people + the poster, which presumably mentions a TV appearance) and coming to a **realization after thinking** about it. It's that 'Aha! Now I understand!' moment.

A: 제 친구는 키가 정말 커요. - My friend is really tall.

B: 오, 그래서 농구를 잘 하는군요! - Oh, I see! That's why he's good at basketball!

*In this dialogue, B takes A's statement about height, thinks about it, and connects it to a potential skill, leading to a realization about why the friend is good at basketball.*"

"Do you see the difference there? It's not just a spontaneous 'Oh!', but more of a 'Oh, *now* I get it,' after a little bit of thought or connecting pieces of information."

**~지(요)**

Next, we have 지(요).

~지 is 반말.

And in 존댓말, it’s ~지요 but you’ll hear ~죠 much more often.

Again, the rule is simple. You just add [~지(요)] after the stem. And you’re done.

Whether it's an action verb or a descriptive verb, and whether the stem has a batchim or not, it doesn't matter.

Now, the meaning of **~지(요)** changes a bit depending on whether it's used in a question or as a statement. However, in both cases, the **keyword for this form is 'confirmation.'** It's all about confirming something.

~지(요)? Question

When you use **~지(요)?** as a question to others - a Yes or No question - you're asking for the listener's **confirmation**. Like you’re trying to get the listener to agree with you like “Right?” or “Isn’t it?” in English.

한국 사람 이시죠? – You’re Korean, right?

**5W1H Questions (Intonation Change)**

But **~지(요)** can also be used with 5W1H questions (who, what, where, when, why, how) when “Right?” only works in Yes or No questions in English.

This is **untranslatable**, but in English, you use a different intonation.

Where did you put your card?

Where’d you put your card, again?

The second one implies that I expect you to know the answer. And I want you to confirm that. This is exactly what 죠 does in this situation

카드를 어디에 두셨어요?

카드를 어디에 두셨죠?

When used as a question to **yourself**, **~지** cannot be used in a simple Yes or No question. Logically speaking, it makes no sense to ask yourself for confirmation about your own idea.

So, it **can only be used in a 5W1H question**.

Like **'어떡하지?' (Eotteokaji?)** which comes from **'어떻게 하다 (eotteoke hada)**

And **이제 뭐 하지?' (Ije mwo haji?)** – 'What do I do now?

**Scenario 1: As a Question to Others - Yes or No Confirmation**

**(Dialogue)**

**A:** 수미 씨는 매운 음식을 좋아하지요? *(Sumi ssi-neun maeun eum-sigeul joahajiyo?)* **(Translation: Sumi likes spicy food, right?)**

*Here, A is asking B to confirm something they already believe or suspect about Sumi's preference for spicy food.*

**A:** 어제 파티에 누가 왔지?

A is using ~지 to ask for information that he thinks B might know and making the question softer or more reflective.

어디로 가지...? **Where should I go...?**

*Now he is speaking to himself, contemplating where to go.*

~지(요) Declarative

When you use **~지(요)** in a statement, you are essentially **confirming something yourself**, and it's less about getting the listener's agreement and more about stating something that's already clear or obvious to *you*.

This form often carries the feeling of 'Certainly,' 'Definitely,' or 'Absolutely' in English

Here, one character asks: **'여권 챙겼어?!'**– 'Did you get the passport?!

The other character confidently replies: **'당연하지! 여기 품속에~**

Here, **'당연하지'** is a perfect example of **~지** used as a declarative. It means 'Of course!' or 'Certainly!' He’s confidently stating that this is an **obvious conclusion** to him – He *definitely* brought the passport. He’s not asking for confirmation; He’s *giving* it.

Let's briefly look at the word **당연하다. It means ‘to be obvious’** but it’s used differently in Korean.

**당연한 결과 : 'A natural outcome' (meaning, no surprise there)**

**당연히 해야 해요 :** 'I'm supposed to do it' (it's the obvious thing to do).

**당연하게 생각하지 마 :** 'Don't take it for granted.'

And as we saw, **당연하지 (dangyeonhaji):** 'Of course / Absolutely' – a strong confirmation

Just a side note on **혹시.** It's an adverb meaning 'by any chance' or 'happen to.'

When you say **'혹시 몰라' (hokshi molla)**, it means 'You never know' or 'You can never be too sure.'

So, to recap: when you use **~지(요)** as a statement, you're confidently declaring or confirming something.

~지(요)? Declarative – Comprehensible input dialogue 3개

**Dialogue 1: Confirming with Certainty**

**A:** 한국어 시험이 정말 어려웠어요?

**B:** 네, 정말 어려웠죠. 제가 아는 문제가 거의 없었어요.

**(Translation: Yes, it was really difficult, indeed. I barely knew any of the questions.)**

*Here, B uses 어려웠죠 to strongly confirm that the test was difficult, stating it as the truth from his experience.*

**~지(요) As a suggestion**

Now, when 지요 comes after an honorific verb, it becomes a very polite and gentle way to suggest something**.**

It feels like a combination of 'Let's...' and 'Please' in English. It's softer and more respectful than a direct 'Let's.'

**Let's look at the text: '장모님, 오사카로 가시죠’**

**장모님 means ‘mother in law’. Now, if there’s one person you DO NOT want to disrespect, it’s your mother-in-law.**

가다 (gada - to go) becomes 가시다 (gasida) in its honorific form. And 가시다 becomes 가시죠.

So, '장모님, 오사카로 가시죠' means, 'Mother-in-law, let's please go to Osaka' It's a very polite way to invite her.

**Now, a reminder about making suggestions in Korean:**

**First, 자 is ONLY for 반말. You don’t want to say 가자 to your 장모님.**

There’s also ㅂ/읍시다 as in 갑시다! This is “supposed to be” formal but don’t use this form. This is something your boss would say to “politely suggest” something for you to do when what they’re actually doing is TELLING you to do it.

Instead, use “~으시죠”. It's the most natural and well-received option."

~지(요) Propositive – Comprehensible input

**A: 영화가 몇 시에 시작해요?**

**- What time does the movie start?**

**B: 7시에 시작해요. 늦기 전에 빨리 가시죠!**

**- It starts at 7. Let's go quickly before it's too late!**

~겠지(요)

Next, we have 겠지(요) Remember, **~겠** expresses intention or possibility, and here it’s combined with **~지(요)**

So, this one is all about guessing and then confirming that.

like **'Probably.' or 'I guess it would/will'**

**'그 사람은 지금 자겠지?' means, 'He's probably sleeping now, right?' You're guessing that he is sleeping, and you're also seeking a confirmation that your guess is correct.**

Let’s look at this one 내일은 내일의 해가 뜨겠지

This is a very poetic Korean saying, and it translates to something like, 'As for tomorrow, I guess the sun of tomorrow will rise.' or simply, 'I guess the sun will rise again tomorrow.

그러시겠지

그러시다 is the honorific form of 그렇다.

This literally means 'I guess your highness would do or say so,' or 'I guess that would be true for you, respectfully.'

But look at this face. This is how honorifics can be sarcastic sometimes, specifically in this context like 'Oh, *of course* you would say/do that'

This is a more advanced nuance but it’s good to know because you never know when you’re going to hear it. 혹시 몰라.

And there's a slightly different structure that uses **~겠지(요)**.

When you combine it with **야 ‘must’ or ‘should’ with 겠지,** means 'I suppose / guess it should'. You're making a guess about what the appropriate action is.

'늦었으니 이제 집에 가야겠지.' Since it's late, I guess I should go home now.

You're acknowledging that it's late and making a decision about the necessary action based on that.

~아/어야지(요)

While ~겠지(요), involved a degree of guessing, this form expresses something a sense of **obligation or firm intention. For now, think of 야 as “should” as in “got to’ or “have to”**

The basic structure is to take the stem, add **~아/어**, and then attach **~야지(요)**

So, 하다 becomes 해야지. And 먹다 becomes 먹어야지.

The meaning changes slightly depending on whether the subject is the first person (I / We) or the second/third person (You / He / She / They).

When the subject is **first person, it expresses a casual, but a firm decision you've just made for yourself and you’re saying it to yourself**

**It's like saying 'I'm just gonna...' or 'I intend to...'"**

This person is watching 먹방, and says: **'조금만 보고 자야지.'** This means, 'I'm just gonna watch only a little then sleep.'

**Usage with 2nd & 3rd Person Subject (You / He / She / They)**

**(Pointing to the "2nd, 3rd Person Subject" box)**

Now, when the subject is **second person (You)** or **third person (He / She / They)**, the meaning becomes closer to **'should' or 'got to.'** It implies an obligation or a necessity for someone else."

One is trying to wake the other up. The text says: **'일어나.. 일해야지.'**

**일어나 (Ireona)'** means 'Wake up

And then **'일해야지** means 'You got work to do,' or 'You should work.' This guy is reminding his friend of his duty that he *should* or *needs to* work.

So, the key takeaway for **아/어야지(요)** is that it expresses a stronger level of certainty or determination than ~겠지(요).

For first person, it's a personal resolve.

For second/third person, it's an obligation or a 'should do' for others.

~잖아(요)

Next, we have 잖아요.

**~잖아(요)** actually originated from **~지 않아요** which is the negative form, as you know. However, its **current usage is completely different**! So, don't confuse it with negation.

When you use **~잖아(요)**, you are essentially **insisting that you're right about something, and you assume the listener already knows that, or at least, *should* know that.**

**It's like you're stating an obvious fact or reminding them of shared knowledge. Kind of similar to starting a sentence with “You know” in English.**

The rule is super simple. You just add ~잖아(요) directly after the stem whether it’s a descriptive verb or an action verb and whether it has a batchim or not.

**You might occasionally hear ~잖습니까. This is the formal speech version of ~잖아(요). And this is the only context marker that can be used in formal speech**

The commentator is saying: **'시간이 많이 안 남았잖습니까?** You know there's not much time left.

He is stating a clear fact, assuming the viewers are aware of the limited time remaining in a game.

So, remember, all other context markers are informal but 잖습니까 is an exception.

~잖아(요) – Comprehensible input dialogue 3개

**A: 이 가게는 왜 이렇게 비싸요? - Why is this place so expensive?**

**B: 여기 강남이잖아요. - This is Gangnam, you know. (Implying: Of course it's expensive, it's Gangnam!)**

**강남 is like the rich neighborhood in Seoul, so** B is stating an obvious fact about the location to explain the price.

**A: 나 다이어트 해야 하는데... - I should go on a diet...**

**B: 이미 살 많이 뺐잖아! - You already lost a lot of weight, you know!**

**B is like “Come on, you know you lost so much already. Give yourself a break”**

**So, when it comes ~잖아(요), just think “I’m right and you know it”.**

**거든(요)**

I’ve briefly shown you 거든 before.

**~거든(요)** is used to **provide background information** like a reason for what you’re saying.

The key idea here is **'background information'** – it's like starting a sentence by saying “Hey, listen, let me explain something to you”.

So, you could think of it as meaning 'It's because~' or 'Just so you know,' or 'The thing is...’

The rule is simple: you just add **~거든(요)** directly after the stem of any verb, descriptive AND action And it doesn’t matter whether it has a batchim or not.

Let’s look at examples

You can use **~거든(요)** to provide the background, and then follow up with your main point like

지금 한국 너무 덥거든요? 그러니까 그냥 가을에 오세요.

Just so you know, it's super-hot in Korea right now. So, just come in the autumn.

Here, the first sentence with 덥거든요 provides the background information (it's hot), and the second sentence gives the advice based on that information.

And you can also use **~거든(요)** *within* the follow-up sentence itself, as a direct reason or explanation for something just stated.

오늘 기분이 안 좋네요. 친구랑 싸웠거든요

I just realized I'm not feeling good today. It's because I fought with a friend.

Here, the second sentence with 싸웠거든요 gives the reason for the bad mood stated in the first sentence.

**Let’s look at this guy**

**He’s saying 나 지금 바쁘거든?'** 'Just so you know, I'm busy right now.'

But clearly, he’s not busy. However, he IS providing a background information, that is false, of course, but this implies that he doesn’t want to be bothered.

And also, note that **~거든(요)** can sound simply **informative**, but it could also come across as a **little condescending and rude** depending on the context, your tone, and your delivery. So, be mindful of how you say it.

Again, the keyword to remember is “background information”.

**~더라고(요)**

Let’s look at 더라고(요).

For **반말 (banmal)**, casual speech, you can use either **~더라 (~deora)** or **~더라고 (~deorago)**. They are completely interchangeable.

However, in 존댓말, just remember that 더라요 is wrong. It’s only ~더라고요.

The core meaning of **~더라고(요)** is that **you are talking about something you've personally experienced, witnessed, or felt.**

It's about recalling something from your own direct observation.

It's like saying, 'I recall that...'

The rule is simple, take any verb stem and attach ~더라고(요) whether it has a batchim or not and that’s it.

이다 becomes 이더라고(요) after a 받침 and 더라고(요) after a vowel.

Now, here's where it gets a bit specific. For **Descriptive Verbs (adjectives)**, you can **only use the present stem**, not the past stem, before **~더라고(요)**

A: 이번 여름에 한국 어땠어요? – How was Korea this summer?

B: 좋았는데 날씨가 너무 덥더라고요. – It was good, but (I recall) the weather was too hot.

Notice B used 덥더라고요 (present stem of 덥다 - to be hot), even though they're talking about the past. In English, we would say 'it *was* hot.'

But Korean descriptive verbs with 더라고요 can only have the present stem because it’s still hot and it will stay hot in his memory.

If he were to say 더웠더라고요, with the past stem, which is wrong, it might imply that the heat *stopped* being hot after he left, which doesn't really make sense.

Since the characteristic (being hot) continues to be true in the speaker's experience, the present stem is used.

Now, what about “맛있다?” You ate the food and it’s gone now. So can you say “맛있었더라고요”? The answer is still **'No'** for the same reason."

A: 내가 말한 그 피자집 가봤지? – You’ve been to that pizza place I told you about, right?

B: 응, 맛있더라 – Yeah, (I recall) it was good.

Here, B says 맛있더라 (present stem) because

1. It will *always* be delicious in B's memory. The characteristic of being delicious is a permanent impression from their experience.

2. It is expected to be delicious *again* next time. The quality of the pizza place hasn't changed.

3. They will keep making delicious pizza. It's a continuous state or quality

So, by this logic, you can **only use the present stem** before ~더라(고)(요) for descriptive verbs because what you recall is still true in your memory.

Now, for **Action Verbs**, it's different. You **can use both the present stem and the past stem** with ~더라고(요).

Let's imagine the first scenario. Your Mom asks: **'오빠는 뭐하니?'** “What’s your brother doing?

So, you go to his room to check on him. You see him **working out**. Then, you would come back and tell your Mom: **'운동하더라고요.** – 'I recall he's working out.'

Even though your *experience* of seeing him work out happened in the past, the action itself is **ongoing or continuous** at the time you're reporting it. So, **~하더라고요 is used for an action that you observed, and it may or may not still be ongoing right now.** This is similar to the logic for descriptive verbs – the state or action is continuous or generally true.

But, what if you saw him when he **just finished working out**? Maybe he’s about to go take a shower.

That’s when you would say 운동했더라고요 – I recall he had worked out (and now he’s done)

So, **~했더라고요 (past stem)** is used for your personal experience where the action you observed **had already ended** at that point.

**But most of the time, [present stem + 더라고요] will be fine, as Korean doesn't always strictly adhere to complex timelines.** But knowing the nuance is definitely going to help, especially with your comprehensible input practice
