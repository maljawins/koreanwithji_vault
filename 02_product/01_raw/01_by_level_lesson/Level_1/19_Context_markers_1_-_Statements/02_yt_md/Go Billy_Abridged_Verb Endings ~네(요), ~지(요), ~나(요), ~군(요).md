## Teaching Points

### Overview

This lesson covers four distinct sentence endings that add specific emotional nuances or pragmatic functions to Korean sentences: ~네(요), ~지(요), ~나(요)/~가(요), and ~군(요). Unlike standard endings that simply convey information, these endings allow a speaker to express surprise, seek confirmation, show curiosity, or signal a new realization. The instructor emphasizes that while the core factual meaning of a sentence remains the same, these endings change how the listener perceives the speaker's intent and attitude.

---

### Expressing Surprise and Agreement with ~네(요)

The ending ~네 (casual) or ~네요 (polite) is primarily used to express surprise about something the speaker is experiencing or noticing right now. It is not used for past or future surprises, but for immediate, "wow" moments. To conjugate, you simply attach the ending to a verb stem. However, if a verb stem ends in ㄹ, that letter must be removed before attaching the ending. For example, 살다 (to live) becomes 사네요.

Beyond surprise, this ending is frequently used to show strong agreement with someone else's statement. In this context, it translates to English fillers like "certainly," "really," or "definitely." The instructor provides the example 맛있네요, which can mean "Wow, this is delicious!" (surprise) or "Yeah, it really is delicious" (agreement). Other examples include:
> **참 손이 작네요.** / Your hands are really small.
> **영화가 정말 재밌네요.** / The movie is really fun/entertaining.

---

### Requesting Confirmation with ~지(요)

The ending ~지 (casual) or ~지요 (polite) is used when the speaker is stating something they already believe to be true and wants the listener to confirm or agree with them. In speech, ~지요 is very commonly shortened to ~죠. This functions similarly to English tag questions like "right?", "don't you?", or "isn't it?".

The instructor illustrates this with the question 맛있죠?, which means "It tastes good, right?" or "It's delicious, isn't it?". It implies the speaker already thinks it tastes good and is just looking for the listener to nod along. Further examples include:
> **조금 비싸죠?** / It's a bit expensive, isn't it?
> **비타민을 매일 먹고 있지요?** / You are taking your vitamins every day, right?

---

### Showing Soft Curiosity with ~나(요) and ~가(요)

These endings are used in questions to show that the speaker is extra curious about the answer. This added curiosity has the social effect of making the question sound softer and more polite than a direct question. The conjugation rules for this form are more complex as they differ between action verbs and descriptive verbs.

For action verbs, you attach ~나(요) to the stem. If the stem ends in ㄹ, remove it first (e.g., 사나요?). For descriptive verbs, the ending depends on the final letter of the stem: if it ends in a consonant, attach ~은가요 (e.g., 작은가요?); if it ends in a vowel, attach ~ㄴ가요 (e.g., 큰가요?). There are specific exceptions for descriptive verbs: stems ending in ㄹ remove the ㄹ before adding ~ㄴ가요; stems ending in ㅂ remove the ㅂ and add 운가요 (e.g., 춥다 becomes 추운가요?); and stems ending in ㅎ remove the ㅎ before adding ~ㄴ가요 (e.g., 어떻다 becomes 어떤가요?).

The instructor notes two important practical points. First, in the past tense, both action and descriptive verbs always use ~나요 (e.g., 작았나요?). Second, in modern casual usage, it is increasingly common to use ~나요 even for descriptive verbs ending in a consonant (e.g., 작나요? instead of 작은가요?). Examples include:
> **맛있나요?** / Is it good? (Showing curiosity)
> **새 집은 어떤가요?** / How is the new house?
> **제 목소리가 작았나요?** / Was my voice quiet/too low?

---

### Realization and "I See" with ~군(요)

The ending ~군 (casual) or ~군요 (polite) indicates that the speaker has just realized or discovered something. It translates well to "Oh, I see," "I realized," or "So then...". For action verbs, the conjugation is ~는군(요), while descriptive verbs use ~군(요). Like other endings, ㄹ-stems remove the ㄹ before the action verb ending (e.g., 사는군요). In the past tense, both types of verbs use ~군요.

A very common casual variation is ~구나 (for descriptive verbs) or ~는구나 (for action verbs). This is more common than just ~군 in casual speech but cannot be made polite by adding 요. The most frequent use of this grammar is in the expression 그렇군요 (I see / That's right). Other examples provided:
> **맛있군요.** / Oh, I see, it's delicious.
> **드디어 눈이 오는군요.** / At last, it's finally snowing (I see it's snowing now).
> **원숭이도 나무에서 떨어지는군요.** / (I see) Even monkeys fall from trees. (Used to express that even experts make mistakes).
