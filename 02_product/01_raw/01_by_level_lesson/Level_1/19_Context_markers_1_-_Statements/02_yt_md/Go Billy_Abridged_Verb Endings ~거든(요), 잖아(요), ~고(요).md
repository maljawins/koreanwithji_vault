## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces three versatile verb endings used to provide context, emphasis, or additional information in Korean conversation: **~거든(요)**, **~잖아(요)**, and **~고(요)**. The lesson focuses on how these endings function pragmatically—how they change the "feeling" of a sentence rather than just its literal meaning. The instructor emphasizes that these forms are primarily colloquial and are used to manage the flow of information between a speaker and a listener, such as explaining a reason the listener might not know, insisting on a fact the listener should already know, or softening the delivery of a statement.

---

### Explaining with ~거든(요)

The ending **~거든(요)** is attached directly to a verb stem. It is used when the speaker is explaining something—either a reason or a fact—that they believe the listener does not already know. The instructor compares it to starting an English sentence with "I'm going to explain something to you" or "It's because..." Because it implies the listener is unaware of the information, it carries a nuance of "for your information" or "just so you know."

The tone of **~거든(요)** is highly dependent on intonation. A falling intonation makes the sentence sound informative and helpful. However, a rising intonation on the final syllable can make the speaker sound like they are talking down to the listener or being intentionally rude. This form is strictly for statements and cannot be used in questions, commands, or suggestions.

The instructor provides example sentences to demonstrate the informative use:
> **제가 가장 좋아하는 과일은 딸기거든요.** / My favorite fruit is strawberry (just so you know).
> **저도 한국어를 배우고 있거든요.** / I am also learning Korean (you know/for your information).

---

### Insisting with ~잖아(요)

The ending **~잖아(요)** is used when the speaker wants to emphasize that what they are saying is correct, often implying that the listener should already know or agree with the fact. It is a shortened version of **~지 않아요** (is it not?), but its usage is distinct. It functions similarly to the confirmation markers **~지** or **~죠**, but it is much stronger and more insistent.

In English, this feeling is captured by phrases like "Look," "See?", "As you know," or "I told you." Because it is an insistent form, it can sound upset or argumentative if used in the wrong context. To avoid sounding upset while still saying "as you know," the instructor suggests using the more straightforward expressions **알다시피** or **아시다시피**.

Example sentences for **~잖아(요)** include:
> **있잖아요.** / Look/Listen (used to start a conversation).
> **밤에 비가 올 거라고 했잖아요.** / I told you/You said it was going to rain tonight.
> **내일은 주말이잖아요.** / Look, tomorrow is the weekend (as you know).

---

### Softening and Adding with ~고(요)

The ending **~고(요)** is the sentence-final version of the connective particle **~고** (and). While **~고** usually connects two clauses in the middle of a sentence, using it at the end of a sentence signals that the speaker has more to say but is choosing to stop for a moment. This creates a "soft" feeling, making the statement sound less abrupt than a standard ending like **~어요**.

In colloquial speech, **~고(요)** is often pronounced as **~구(요)**, which sounds even less stiff and slightly "cuter" or more friendly. Regarding tense, the instructor notes that while you can use past tense stems (e.g., **~었고요**), you only need to do so if you want to specifically emphasize that an action was completed in the past.

The instructor demonstrates the softening effect with these examples:
> **해리포터 영화는 다 봤고요.** / I've seen all the Harry Potter movies (and, you know...).
> **여기에 이름을 적고 들어가세요.** / Please write your name here and then go in.
> **저는 스무 살이고요, 대학생이에요.** / I'm 20 years old, and (also) a college student.

---

### Advanced Note: ~거든 as a Conditional

The instructor briefly mentions an advanced usage of **~거든** (without **요**) used in the middle of a sentence. In this context, it functions as a conditional marker similar to **~면** (if/when). It is almost always followed by a command or a suggestion in the second clause. For example, "If/when [X] happens, then do [Y]." This is distinct from the sentence-final explanatory **~거든(요)** taught in the main lesson.
