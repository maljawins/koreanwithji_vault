## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor presents a comprehensive guide to seven common Korean verb endings that add specific emotional nuances or contextual information to a sentence. While the literal meaning of a sentence often remains the same as the standard 요 form, these endings allow speakers to express surprise, curiosity, confirmation, or even a desire to sound softer and more polite. The lesson is designed for learners ranging from beginner to low-intermediate levels.

The instructor emphasizes that Korean is a language of verb endings. By taking a verb stem—formed by removing the final 다 from the dictionary form—a speaker can attach various suffixes to completely change the "flavor" of the statement. For example, the verb 하다 (to do) can become 하네요, 하죠, 하나요, 하는군요, 하거든요, 하잖아요, or 하고요. Each of these conveys a different intent that would often require extra words like "wow," "right?", or "just so you know" in English.

---

### Expressing Surprise with ~네요

The ending ~네요 is used to express a feeling of surprise or a new realization about something happening in the moment. It is attached directly to the verb stem. For example, if you taste something and find it unexpectedly delicious, you would say 맛있네요 (It's delicious!). This carries the nuance of "Wow, I'm surprised at how good this is."

The instructor notes a few specific variations and rules for this ending. In casual speech (반말) with close friends, the 요 is dropped, leaving just ~네. A cute, slightly "cringy" variation used in texting or aegyo is ~네용. Regarding conjugation, if a verb stem ends in ㄹ, such as 길다 (to be long), the ㄹ is dropped before adding the ending, resulting in 기네요.

---

### Seeking Confirmation with ~지 and ~죠

The ending ~지 (casual) or ~지요 (polite) is used when the speaker wants to confirm something they believe to be true with the listener. In spoken Korean, ~지요 is almost always contracted to ~죠. This functions similarly to English tag questions like "isn't it?", "right?", or the Canadian "eh?".

There are no special conjugation rules for this ending; it attaches directly to any verb stem. For instance, asking 맛있죠? means "It's delicious, isn't it?". Using it with 예쁘다 (to be pretty) creates 예쁘죠? (It's pretty, right?). It signals to the listener that the speaker is interested in their feedback or agreement.

---

### Showing Curiosity with ~나요

The ending ~나요 adds a sense of genuine curiosity or a "soft" questioning tone to a sentence. Like ~네요, it follows the ㄹ-drop rule: for the verb 멀다 (to be far), the ㄹ is removed to form 머나요? (Is it far?). This sounds more inquisitive and polite than a standard question.

The instructor mentions a related casual ending, ~니, which is primarily used by females or by males in a jokingly feminine or "cute" way. An example provided is 뭐 하니? (What are you doing?). He references a Conan O'Brien music video where the comedian uses this form to sound intentionally cute.

---

### New Realizations with ~군요 and ~는군요

The ending ~군요 (polite) or ~군 (casual) is used when the speaker has just realized a fact they didn't know before. It translates roughly to "Oh, I see" or "I didn't realize that." For descriptive verbs (adjectives), one uses ~군요, as in 맛있군요 (Oh, I see it's delicious).

For action verbs in the present tense, the form changes to ~는군요, such as 잘 하는군요 (Wow, I see you are good at that). In the past tense, however, even action verbs use ~군요 (e.g., 했군요). A more emphatic casual version is ~구나 or ~는구나, which adds extra emphasis to the realization.

---

### Informing the Listener with ~거든요

The ending ~거든요 is used when the speaker is providing information that they believe the listener does not know. It functions like "FYI," "Just so you know," or "The reason is..." in English. Because it assumes the listener is ignorant of a fact, it can sometimes sound condescending or rude if used improperly.

The instructor highlights that intonation is critical for this ending. A rising intonation on the final syllable (~요↑) often sounds playful or like one is "talking down" to a friend in a friendly way. For example, 미국 사람이거든요! (Just so you know, I'm an American!). A falling intonation (~요↓) sounds more serious, factual, and distant. It is generally safer to use a falling intonation with people you are not close to.

---

### Asserting Correctness with ~잖아요

The ending ~잖아요 is used to insist that something is true, often when the speaker feels the listener should already know the fact. It translates to "See?" or "Look, I told you." It is a compressed version of ~지 않아요, but the meaning has shifted from a negative question to a strong affirmation.

While it ends in ~요, the instructor warns that this form is inherently casual and should not be used with strangers, teachers, or bosses. It is best suited for playful banter or close friends. For example, if a Korean friend says Korean is easy, you might say 한국 사람이잖아요! (Look, it's because you're Korean!).

---

### Softening Statements with ~고요

The ending ~고요 is derived from the connector ~고 (and). While ~고 usually connects two sentences, using ~고요 at the end of a single sentence has two main functions. First, it can indicate that the speaker's thought is not yet finished, implying there is more to say.

Second, and more commonly, it is used to make a sentence sound "softer" and less harsh. By ending with ~고요, the speaker sounds less certain or aggressive, leaving room for the listener to disagree. For example, instead of a blunt "I'm not Korean," one might say 아니고요... (I'm not Korean, and...). This makes the interaction feel more polite and less confrontational.
