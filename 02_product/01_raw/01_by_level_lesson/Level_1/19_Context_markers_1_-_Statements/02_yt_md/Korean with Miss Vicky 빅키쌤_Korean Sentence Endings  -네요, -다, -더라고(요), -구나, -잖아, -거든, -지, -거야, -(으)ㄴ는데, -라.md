### Overview

In this comprehensive live lesson, the instructor covers ten essential Korean sentence endings that add specific nuances, emotions, and pragmatic meanings to speech. The lesson is structured to move beyond simple dictionary definitions by comparing confusingly similar endings and demonstrating their usage through real-life clips from Korean dramas like *Strong Girl Nam-soon* and *A Time Called You*. The instructor emphasizes that these endings are the key to sounding natural and expressive in Korean conversation, as they signal the speaker's immediate reactions, past realizations, or relationship with the listener.

---

### The Immediate Reaction Marker 네요

The primary keywords for **네요** are "right now" and "immediate." It is used to express a realization or reaction to something happening at the very moment of speaking. The grammar rule is simple: attach **네요** directly to the stem of a verb, adjective, or the copula **이다**.

The instructor outlines several specific contexts for this ending. First, it is used for what you realized just now, such as looking out a window and seeing rain for the first time: **비가 오네요** (Oh, it's raining). Second, it serves as an immediate reaction to what you are seeing or hearing, or to what someone just said. Because it is an immediate reaction, it is frequently used for surprises, compliments, or agreeing with a friend's statement. For example, if a friend says Korean is hard, you might respond with **맞네** (That's true/I agree) as your immediate realization. When complimenting someone's language skills, you would say **한국어 잘하시네요** (You speak Korean so well).

---

### The Versatile 다 Ending

The **다** ending is famously known as the "written form" used in diaries and newspapers, but it also has significant spoken functions. It is strictly **반말** (informal speech), so it must be paired with other informal elements like **나** instead of **저**. The conjugation rules differ between verbs and adjectives in the present tense. For verbs, you add **-ㄴ/는다** to the stem (e.g., **간다**, **먹는다**). For adjectives, the form is identical to the dictionary form (e.g., **예쁘다**, **좋다**).

In spoken Korean, **다** has two main usages. First, it can be used interchangeably with **네요** to express an immediate reaction. For instance, seeing a friend's new haircut and saying **예쁘다!** (It's pretty!) or feeling the difficulty of a task and saying **어렵다** (It's hard). Second, it is often used in place of the future tense (**-을 거야** or **-을게**) to announce an intention. A common example is **나 간다** (I'm leaving/I'm off), which functions similarly to **갈게**.

---

### Recalling the Past with 더라고(요)

The ending **더라고(요)** is focused on the past, acting as the opposite of **네요**. It is used to convey something the speaker learned, realized, or felt in the past. The "더" in this ending is the same retrospective marker found in **-던**. The informal forms are **더라** or **더라고**, while the polite form is **더라고요**.

This ending is used when sharing a past experience with someone else. For example, if you went on a picnic yesterday and are describing the weather today, you would say **날씨가 진짜 좋더라고** (The weather was really nice). It can also be used with the past tense suffix **-았/었-** to describe a "past of a past." If you went into your son's room an hour ago and saw he had already finished his homework before you arrived, you would say **끝냈더라고** (I saw that he had [already] finished it).

---

### Realizing Facts with 구나

The ending **구나** (informal) or **군요** (polite) is used when realizing a new fact. Unlike **네요**, which is often emotional or reactive, **구나** is more focused on the objective realization of information. It is frequently used in the form **그렇구나** (I see/I realize it is so) when responding to new information from a listener.

The conjugation rules are distinct: verbs take **-는구나** (e.g., **사는구나**), while adjectives take **-구나** (e.g., **슬프구나**). A key nuance is that **구나** is rarely used for one's own immediate feelings because we don't usually "realize" our own emotions as new facts unless we are observing ourselves from a distance. However, it is perfect for acknowledging how someone else feels: **슬프구나** (I see that you are sad).

---

### Reminding and Proving Points with 잖아(요)

The keyword for **잖아(요)** is "the other person already knows this." It is used to remind the listener of a fact or to prove a point using shared knowledge. It often translates to "you know..." or "...right?" but with a more assertive nuance.

The instructor provides several examples: if a friend asks where the water is when it's right in front of them, you say **저기에 있잖아요** (It's right there [you know]). If someone asks your name after you already told them, you might say **아까 말했잖아요** (I told you earlier [remember?]). In drama clips, this is shown when a character says **너도 내 친구 밀었잖아** (You pushed my friend too, [you know/right?]) to justify an action.

---

### Providing Reasons with 거든(요)

The ending **거든(요)** is primarily used to provide a reason. It can be a response to a "why" question, a reason for something just said, or a "conversation opener" that provides background for what the speaker is about to say next. For example, if a teacher asks why you are late, you respond **늦잠 잤거든요** (It's because I overslept).

A second, more aggressive usage of **거든(요)** is for strong opposition or objecting to what someone else said. If someone spreads a false rumor that you are dating someone, you would sharply say **아니거든요!** (No, I'm not/That's not true at all!). Because of this assertive nuance, it can sound "cute" between very close friends but aggressive with strangers.

---

### Stating the Obvious with 지/죠

The ending **지** (informal) or **지요/죠** (polite) is used for statements that the speaker considers obvious, truthful, or a matter of common sense. The keyword is "obvious to me." While the question form of **-죠?** asks for confirmation, the declarative form asserts a truth.

For example, if asked if your girlfriend is pretty, responding **예쁘지** implies "Of course she's pretty/It's obvious she's pretty to me." It can also be used to suggest an action, particularly in the form **-시죠**, such as **가시죠** (Let's go) or **앉으시죠** (Please take a seat). The instructor notes that **-시죠** can sometimes sound rude if used by a lower-ranking person to a higher-ranking person, as it carries a directive tone.

---

### Dramatic Effect and Emphasis with 거야

The ending **-(으)ㄴ/는 거야** (informal) or **-(으)ㄴ/는 거예요** (polite) literally translates to "It is that..." and is used for dramatic effect or emphasis. In questions, it softens the tone: **왜 그런 거야?** (Why is it that [you did that]?) sounds less direct than **왜 그랬어?**.

In statements, it is used to make a story more intriguing or to highlight a specific point the speaker wants the listener to pay attention to. When telling a story about the past, speakers often switch to the present tense **-는 거야** to make the narrative more vivid: **갑자기 나를 무는 거야!** (Suddenly, [it's that] it bites me!). It is also used to convince someone or explain oneself by emphasizing a decided fact, such as **너 합격한 거야** (You [actually] passed).

---

### Background and Contrast with (으)ㄴ/는데(요)

The ending **-(으)ㄴ/는데(요)** is derived from the connective "but" or "and," and it carries the keyword "expecting more." It is used to provide background information or to signal that the speaker is waiting for a response or action from the listener.

One usage is responding to something contrary to one's knowledge: if someone calls you American and you are Canadian, you say **저 캐나다 사람인데요** (I'm Canadian, though...). Another usage is providing background: **제가 요즘 한국어를 배우는데요...** (I'm learning Korean these days [and/so]...). Finally, it is used to imply a request without saying it directly. If a stranger picks up your bag, saying **그거 제 건데요** (That's mine...) implies "so please put it back."

---

### Assertive Commands with 라

The ending **-라** is an imperative marker used only in **반말**. It makes a command more assertive and straightforward than a simple verb conjugation. It is often used when the speaker is annoyed or wants to be very firm.

Common examples include **조용히 좀 해라** (Be quiet!) or **저리 가라** (Go away!). While it is usually assertive, between very close friends it can sometimes sound playful. It is also the standard form for imperative sentences in written Korean, though the assertive nuance is specific to spoken language.
