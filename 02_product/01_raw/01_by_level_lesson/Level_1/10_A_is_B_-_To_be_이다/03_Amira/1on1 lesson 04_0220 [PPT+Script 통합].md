## Lesson Overview
In this fourth session, the focus shifts from the structural mechanics of the Korean language to practical application, specifically addressing speech levels, social phrases, and basic sentence construction. The lesson begins with a recap of the **STLOHV** acronym, word order flexibility, and the "plant analogy" for verbs (stem, branch, leaf, and fruit). A primary goal today is to standardize the terminology for speech types—Informal High, Informal Low, Formal High, and Formal Low—to resolve inconsistencies often found in various learning resources.

The core of the lesson covers essential social interactions: greetings, farewells, gratitude, and apologies. Ji emphasizes that while these should be memorized as units for now, they contain recognizable grammatical patterns, such as honorific markers (ㅅ) and tense markers (ㅆ). Cultural nuances are highlighted, particularly the Korean preference for asking "Did you eat?" (**식사하셨어요?**) over the Western "How are you?", and the specific rules for answering negative questions.

Finally, the lesson introduces the "A is B" sentence structure using the verb **이다** (to be). This includes learning subject particles (**은/는**), the distinction between the humble "I" (**저**) and casual "I" (**나**), and how to navigate the complexities of the word "you" in Korean culture. By the end of the lesson, students are prepared to construct a basic self-introduction and identify objects using "this" and "that" (**이것/저것/그것**).

### Slide 4 — Speech types simplified
- **On screen:** A table showing the verb **하다** (To do) conjugated across different formalities. 
    - **Informal High (Polite):** ends in **요** (highlighted in blue).
    - **Informal Low (Casual):** Nothing at the end (highlighted in blue).
    - **Formal High (Formal):** ends in **ㅂ니다** (highlighted in blue).
    - **Formal Low:** Only for books/monologues.
    - **존댓말** = High & **반말** = Low (highlighted in yellow).

- **Ji's teaching:** In the videos I sent you, you’ve probably noticed some inconsistency with terms like "formal" and "polite." Let’s standardize our terminology. We'll categorize the four main speech types as: Informal High (Polite - **해요**), Informal Low (Casual - **해**), Formal High (Formal - **합니다**), and Formal Low (**한다**). The terms High and Low indicate the level of respect toward the listener. High is **존댓말**, and Low is **반말**. I’ll use these terms often to help you get familiar with them. Note that "low respect level" does not mean disrespectful; it's just a classification. Formal Low is mainly used in writing—newspapers or academic papers—and sounds unnatural in conversation, so we won't focus on it. (H) stands for honorifics, used when referring to someone of higher status. To identify speech types easily: if it ends in "**요**", it's Informal High. If it ends in "**ㅂ니다**", it's Formal High. If it has neither, it's **반말**. If you see a syllable starting with "**ㅅ**" right after the stem (like **시, 셔, 세**), it’s honorific.

### Slide 5 — YES & NO
- **On screen:** A table for Yes/No.

    - **NO:** **아니오** (Informal High), **아니** (Informal Low), **아닙니다** (Formal High).

- **Ji's teaching:** **네** and **예** in **존댓말** are inherently polite, so there’s no distinction between formal and informal, and you don’t need to add any suffix. They’re interchangeable, but **네** is more common. **응** and **어** in **반말** are also interchangeable. For "No," **아니** technically means "not" rather than a direct "no," so you need to add a suffix to indicate politeness: **아니오** for Informal High, **아니** for Informal Low, and **아닙니다** for Formal High.

### Slide 6 — When No means Yes in Korean
- **On screen:** Comparison between English and Korean logic. 
    - English: "Yes" always means agreement with the fact (I am hungry).
    - Korean: You agree or disagree with the *negative* in the question.
    - Example: "Aren't you hungry?" -> **네** (Yes, I'm NOT hungry / I agree with your negative question). **아니오** (No, I AM hungry / I disagree with your negative question).
    - Visual: **-1 x -1 = +1** (highlighted in blue).
- **Ji's teaching:** In English, answering "Yes" means agreement regardless of whether the question is positive or negative. But in Korean, you respond to the negative in the question. So if someone asks "Aren't you hungry?" and you say **네**, you are saying "Yes, I am not hungry" because you are agreeing with their negative statement. If you say **아니오**, you are saying "No, I am hungry" because you are disagreeing with the negative. Think of it like multiplying negative numbers: **(-1) × (-1) = (+1)**.

### Slide 8 — Hello
- **On screen:** Table for **안녕하다** (To be well). 
    - **안녕**: Well-being, Peace. **하다**: To be.
    - Informal High (H): **안녕하세요?**
    - Informal Low: **안녕?** (Note: Only in Textbooks).
    - Formal High (H): **안녕하십니까?**
- **Ji's teaching:** **안녕하다** is a descriptive verb formed by combining the noun **안녕** (well-being/peace) with **하다** (to be). It literally means "to be well." However, this word is only used for Hello and Goodbye—it’s not used in other contexts.

### Slide 9 — Hello – 존댓말 Informal High
- **On screen:** **안녕하세요?** broken down. 
    - **안녕하** (Stem) + **세요요?** (Ending). 
    - **ㅅ** in **세** indicates honorific. 
    - **요** at the end indicates informal high. 
    - **세요?** indicates a question (or imperative without the question mark).
- **Ji's teaching:** **안녕하세요?** is technically a question meaning "Are you well?" "**안녕하**" is the stem and the "**세요?**" ending turns it into a question. "**세**" indicates an honorific, and "**요**" marks informal high speech. Although it’s technically a question, we don’t usually say it with a rising intonation; we just say **안녕하세요**. Repeat after me: **안녕하세요**~. Excellent! Note that **세요** without the question mark is an imperative, so it could mean "Be well." You don't answer this question; you just say it back.

### Slide 10 — Hello – 반말 Informal Low
- **On screen:** **안녕?** (No grammar). Note: Used only in textbooks; sounds "fake." In Korea, friends don't bother greeting unless it's been a long time.
- **Ji's teaching:** For **반말** informal low, textbooks will tell you it’s **안녕**, but we don’t really use this in real life. In Korea, friends don’t even bother with greetings; we just get straight to the point since we would have been texting anyway.

### Slide 11 — Alternatives – 반말 Informal Low
- **On screen:** **왔어?** / **왔네?** (Did you come? / Oh, you're here now). 
    - From verb **오다** (To come). 
    - **오** + **았** + Context marker = **왔어?** / **왔네?**
    - **ㅆ** **받침** in the first or second syllable indicates **PAST TENSE** (highlighted in yellow).
- **Ji's teaching:** Here are two phrases much more natural than **안녕**. Repeat after me: **왔어?** It sounds like "What's up?", but it actually means "Did you come?" Then there is **왔네?**, which has the same meaning but the **네** context marker makes it sound like "Oh, you're here now." Tip: if a verb has a **ㅆ** **받침** in the first or second syllable, it’s most likely past tense. Sometimes the "branch" merges with the stem, like in **왔**.

### Slide 12 — 오다 (To come) - Informal Low Past tense
- **On screen:** Plant analogy diagram for **오다**. 
    - **오** (Stem). 
    - **았** (Branch: Tense - Past). 
    - **어?** (Leaf: Context - Plain/General Statement). 
    - **네?** (Leaf: Context - Oh, now). 
    - Note: No Fruit (Politeness) because it is Informal Low.
- **Ji's teaching:** This diagram shows how the verb **오다** grows. The stem is **오**, the past tense branch is **았**, and the leaves are the context markers **어** or **네**. Because this is **반말**, there is no "fruit" (the **요** or **ㅂ니다** endings) to provide politeness.

### Slide 13 — 먹다 (To eat) Past tense – Informal high
- **On screen:** Plant analogy diagram for **먹다**. 
    - **먹** (Stem). 
    - **었** (Branch: Tense). 
    - **어** (Leaf: Context - Plain). 
    - **요** (Fruit: Respect). 
    - Result: **먹 + 었 + 어 + 요 = 먹었어요**.
- **Ji's teaching:** Now look at **먹다** in Informal High. We have the stem **먹**, the past tense branch **었**, the plain context leaf **어**, and finally the fruit **요**, which adds the respect needed for Informal High. Together it becomes **먹었어요**.

### Slide 14 — Hello – Formal High
- **On screen:** **안녕하십니까?** 
    - **십** indicates honorific. 
    - Questions in Formal Speech have **ㅂ니까?** ending. 
    - Formal High can't have context markers (sounds like a robot/little emotion).
- **Ji's teaching:** In formal high, it’s **안녕하십니까?** Repeat after me. You see the '**ㅅ**' in '**십**', right? That makes it honorific. Tip: Formal High speech type questions always end in "**ㅂ니까?**" An interesting fact is that you can’t add context markers in this speech type; it’s grammatically wrong. If you overuse this, you sound like a robot.

### Slide 15 — How are you? in Korean is awkward
- **On screen:** "is awkward" (highlighted in yellow). Only used when it's been a long time (months) or for literal health checks (doctor).
- **Ji's teaching:** In Korea, we don’t actually ask "How are you?" except when it’s been a long time or we literally want to know your state, like a doctor checking a patient. Koreans don’t think of "how they are" on a daily basis. Unless it’s been a few months, asking this can be unnatural or imply something is wrong.

### Slide 16 — We ask “Did you eat?”
- **On screen:** Why? Food is huge in Korean culture (highlighted in yellow). History of starvation. Idiom "**먹고 살다**" (To eat and live) means "To make ends meet."
- **Ji's teaching:** Instead, we often ask "Did you eat?" as a casual way to check in. Korea used to be extremely poor, and people would literally starve. Food still means a lot to us. We even have the idiom "**먹고 살다**" which literally means "to eat and live," but figuratively means "to make ends meet." That’s how important food is.

### Slide 17 — Did you eat? in Korean
- **On screen:** 
    - **밥**: Cooked rice / Meal / Food (highlighted in yellow). **쌀**: raw rice.
    - **먹다**: To eat.
    - **식사하다**: To have a meal (Sino-Korean, sounds fancy/formal).
    - Informal High (H): **식사하셨어요?**
    - Informal Low: **밥 먹었어?**
    - Formal High (H): **식사하셨습니까?**
- **Ji's teaching:** **밥** literally means "cooked rice," but it's also a casual way to say "meal." We have a separate word for raw rice, **쌀**. **식사하다** means "to have a meal." **식사** is a Sino-Korean word, which sounds formal and fancy—like "to dine" versus "to eat." That’s why using **식사하다** in **반말** would sound very weird. Repeat these after me: **식사하셨어요?**, **밥 먹었어?**, and **식사하셨습니까?**

### Slide 18 — How are you? When it’s been a long time
- **On screen:** 
    - **지내다** (v): Describes general state of being or passing time.
    - **어떻게**: How. Can substitute with **잘** (well).
    - Informal High (H): **어떻게 지내세요?** / **잘 지내세요?**
    - Informal Low: **어떻게 지내?** / **잘 지내?**
    - Formal High (H): **어떻게 지내십니까?** / **잘 지내십니까?**
- **Ji's teaching:** Again, you’d never say these to someone you saw two days ago. **어떻게** means "how," and **지내다** doesn’t have a direct English equivalent, but it means "to spend time" or "to get along." You can substitute **어떻게** with **잘** (well). Notice the patterns: '**ㅅ**' for honorific, '**요**' for informal high, and '**ㅂ니다/ㅂ니까**' for formal high.

### Slide 19 — Nice to meet you 반갑다 – Glad
- **On screen:** 
    - **반갑다**: Irregular verb (ㅂ **받침** pattern).
    - Subject "I" is omitted.
    - Can add **만나서** (To meet + so) in front.
    - Informal High: **반가워요**.
    - Informal Low: **반가워**.
    - Formal High: **반갑습니다**.
- **Ji's teaching:** When meeting someone for the first time, we use the descriptive verb **반갑다**, which is similar to "glad." Repeat after me: **반가워요**, **반가워**, **반갑습니다**. Tip: **반갑다** is an irregular verb. The '**ㅂ**' in the stem disappears when conjugated. You can add **만나서** in front. **만나다** means "to meet" and **서** means "so." Whenever you see **서**, think of "so"—they sound similar! So **만나서 반가워요** literally means "(We) meet, so (I am) glad."

### Slide 20 — Bye in Korean
- **On screen:** Depends on who is leaving ("Stay well" vs "Go well"). **안녕** is both but not used IRL.
- **Ji's teaching:** There are two ways to say "goodbye" depending on who is leaving and who is staying. If you're leaving, you say "Stay well." If you're staying, you say "Go well."

### Slide 21 — Bye: When they’re staying (you’re leaving)
- **On screen:** 
    - **있다**: To be/exist (Present). **있었다** (Past).
    - **계시다**: Honorific of **있다**.
    - **안녕히**: Adverbial form of **안녕하다** (Adverbs end in **히** or **게**).
    - Informal High (H): **안녕히 계세요**.
    - Informal Low: **잘 있어**.
    - Formal High (H): **안녕히 계십시오**.
- **Ji's teaching:** When you are leaving, you say "Stay well." Repeat after me: **안녕히 계세요**, **잘 있어**, **안녕히 계십시오**. The verb **있다** means "to be," and **계시다** is its honorific form. Don't be fooled by the **ㅆ** in **있**; that is the stem, not the past tense. **안녕히** is an adverb. Most adverbs end in **히** or **게**, like "-ly" in English. Here, **세요** is an imperative (command), and the formal high imperative ends in **십시오**.

### Slide 23 — Thank you in Korean
- **On screen:** **감사하다** > **고맙다**.

    - Both are descriptive verbs (To be grateful/thankful).

- **Ji's teaching:** There are two words for "thank you": **고맙다** and **감사하다**. **감사하다** feels a little stronger and more sincere, while **고맙다** is a bit more casual. You’ll usually use **감사하다** in **존댓말**. Because respect is so important, we actually use formal high speech for "thank you" more often than informal high.

### Slide 27 — Never say sorry to say “I’m sorry to hear that”

If you say **죄송합니다** to comfort someone, they’ll wonder why *you* are sorry, or they might think you are the one to blame! Don't say sorry unless it's your fault. There is no direct translation for "I'm sorry to hear that," though there are other ways to show empathy.

### Slide 33 — A is B: 이다 – To be

- **Ji's teaching:** The most important word for introducing yourself is "to be." In English, "be" has many uses, but in Korean, we use different words. To say "A is B" (like "I am a man"), we use **이다**.

### Slide 34 — Subject
- **On screen:** 
    - I am a student: **나는 학생이야** (Inf. L), **저는 학생이에요** (Inf. H), **저는 학생입니다** (For. H).

### Slide 35 — Subject Particle
- **On screen:** 
    - **은 / 는** particle marks the subject. Does not exist in English (highlighted in yellow).
    - **은**: for nouns with **받침** (ex: **제 이름은**).
    - **는**: for nouns without **받침** (ex: **저는**).
    - **받침** is important for grammar (highlighted in yellow).
    - **내/제**: My. **이름**: Name.
- **Ji's teaching:** In Korean, we use **은/는** after the topic of a sentence. English doesn't have this, so there's no direct translation. **는** is for nouns with no **받침**, and **은** is for nouns that have a **받침**. This is why **받침** matters so much—it changes the particles and the verbs!

### Slide 36 — 이다 Conjugation (With 받침)
- **On screen:** When there’s a **받침**, it’s followed with ‘**이**’.
    - **이야** (Low).
    - **이에요** (High).
    - **입(이+ㅂ)니다** (Formal).
    - Example: **학생** (ends in **ㅇ** **받침**).
- **Ji's teaching:** When the noun has a **받침**, like **학생**, we use **이야**, **이에요**, or **입니다**. Repeat after me: **나는 학생이야**, **저는 학생이에요**, **저는 학생입니다**.

### Slide 37 — 이다 Conjugation (No 받침)
- **On screen:** When there’s no **받침**, there’s no ‘**이**’.
    - **야** is short for **이야**.
    - **예요** is short for **이에요**.
    - Many Koreans say **에요** (eyo) (highlighted in yellow).
    - Verbal shortcut: **아미랍니다** (**아미라 + ㅂ니다**) (highlighted in yellow).
- **Ji's teaching:** When there is no **받침**, like **아미라**, we use **야**, **예요**, or **입니다**. If you say **이야/이에요** really fast, it sounds like **야/예요**, so think of them as shortened versions. Two tips: Koreans often say **에요** instead of **예요** because it's easier to pronounce. Also, instead of **아미라입니다**, we often say **아미랍니다** to make it flow better verbally.

### Slide 39~40 — This and That
- **On screen:** **이, 저, 그 + 것 (거)**. 
    - **것**: Thing. **거** is more natural (highlighted in yellow).
    - In Korean, these are always adjectives, so you must add **것/거** to make them nouns.
    - **이거**: This.
    - **저거**: That over there (far).
    - **그거#1**: That right there (close to listener).
    - **그거#2**: That thing/guy not in sight (mentioned before).
- **Ji's teaching:** In English, "this" and "that" can be nouns or adjectives. In Korean, they are *always* adjectives. To make them nouns, you must add **것** (thing) or **거** (which is more natural). There are two versions of "that." **저거** is for something far from both of us. **그거** is for something close to you (the listener) or something we are talking about that isn't in sight. Pay attention to the consonants (**ㅇ, ㅈ, ㄱ**) because they apply to "here and there" too.

### Slide 42 — This / That is Noun
- **On screen:** Abbreviations. **것은** → **거는** → **건**. **거 + 는 = 건** (highlighted in yellow).

**Closing Note: The Verb 하다**
Before we finish, you need to know about **하다**. Korean verb endings are hard, but if you know **하다**, you know half of all Korean verbs. Many verbs are just a **Noun + 하다**. For example, **안녕하다** (well-being + to be), **친절하다** (kindness + to be), or **말하다** (speech + to do/talk). **하다** turns words into verbs. It is the most important verb in the language.

## Teaching Points
- **Speech Levels:** Standardized into four categories: Informal High (**요**), Informal Low (No ending), Formal High (**ㅂ니다/ㅂ니까**), and Formal Low (Writing only). **존댓말** refers to High levels; **반말** refers to Low levels.
- **Identifying Patterns:** 
    - **ㅅ** (**시, 셔, 세, 십**) indicates an honorific.
    - **ㅆ** **받침** in the first or second syllable usually indicates past tense (e.g., **왔어**, **먹었어요**).
    - **요** at the end is the universal marker for polite, informal high speech.
- **Social Logic:** 
    - **Yes/No:** In response to negative questions, **네** means "I agree with your negative statement" and **아니오** means "I disagree with your negative statement."
    - **Greetings:** "Did you eat?" (**식사하셨어요?**) is the standard casual greeting. **밥** (cooked rice) is used for general meals.
    - **Apologies:** **죄송합니다** and **미안해** are for personal faults only; never use them for "I'm sorry to hear that."
- **Sentence Structure (A is B):** 
    - Uses the verb **이다**. 
    - **Conjugation:** If the noun has a **받침**, use **이야/이에요**. If no **받침**, use **야/예요**.
    - **Subject Particles:** **은** (with **받침**) and **는** (without **받침**).
- **Addressing Others:** Avoid the word "you" (**당신**). Use family titles (**오빠, 언니, 형, 누나**), names with **씨/님**, or professional titles. When in doubt, drop the subject entirely.
