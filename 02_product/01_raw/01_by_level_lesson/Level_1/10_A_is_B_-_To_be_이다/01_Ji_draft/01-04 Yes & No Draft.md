**Yes & No in Korean**

Okay. Let’s learn how to say yes and no in Korean.

네 and 예 are “yes” in 존댓말. And they’re inherently polite, so you don’t need to add “요”.

And they’re interchangeable, but **네** is more common than **예** as 예 feels a bit more formal than 네.

**Why 네 Sounds Like "De"**

Now, let me address a very common question while we're on the subject: Why does 네 sound like "de" to non-Korean ears?

One thing you have to understand is that Korean sounds are different from English sounds. This is why you need to pay attention to the actual sounds of 한글 rather than romanization.

Korean consonants are pronounced a lot more abruptly than English consonants. They have a shorter "holding time," so to speak, before you open your mouth and vibrate your vocal cords.

영어 자음은 부드럽게 흐르듯이 발음이 되지만 한국어의 자음은 발음할 때 입에서 “short burst”가 생긴다.

Now, when you say N sound in English you put your tongue behind your upper teeth. But ㄴ (ni eun) comes from between lower and upper teeth.

So when Koreans say 네 fast, it makes them slightly bite their tongue thereby adding abruptness on top of that, making it sound like 데.

**Yes in 반말**

Moving on to 반말, “Yes” in 반말 is **응** or **어**. “어” can sometimes feel a bit cold and slightly unwelcoming than “응”. They’re also interchangeable in most cases.

**No in Korean**

For "No," you do need to add 요 to indicate politeness:

**아니요** – Polite. (often shortened to 아뇨)

**아니** – Casual.

You might have heard “아니에요”. This means “It isn’t” rather than a simple “No” and it’s actually a conjugated form of the verb “아니다”, which means “to not be”.

We’ll get into this when we learn how to say “A is not B.” but just know that it’s 아니요 and 아니 are simple “No”.

**1) Responding When Someone Calls You**

This is similar to English—when someone calls your name, you could say "yes."

So in a situation like this, you can say “네” or “응” in Korean. For example, in school, when the teacher is taking attendance and you hear your name, you would respond with “here” in English. However in Korean, we respond with just “네”.

**2) Phone call**

The go-to phrase for answering a phone call in Korean is 여보세요, which means “Hello” but only for phone calls - for greeting.

But your phone always shows you the name or the number of the person that’s calling you. And most of the time, that person is someone you know.

And since you know this person, you don’t really have to bother with “여보세요”. Instead, you can just answer with "**네**" or **“응”**.

And you can also end a phone call with “네” and “응” like 네~ or 응~ with an elongated tone which signals the end of the call.

**3) Showing You're Listening**

In a conversation, if you stay too silent while the other person is talking, sometimes it might seem like you're not listening closely. So to show that you’re actually paying attention, you can say 네 or 응.

This is similar to "uh-huh" or "right" in English—it's just a sound to show you're listening.

You can also say 네네 or 응응 by repeating it twice.

**4) "Sorry?” “What?” “Pardon?”**

You can also use 네 to ask someone to repeat what they said.

There are two situations for this:

First, when you didn't hear what they said—like "Sorry?"

Second, when you're surprised or can't believe what they said—like "Really?"

In this case, the intonation goes up like a question: 네?

**5) To respond to a command / suggestion**

Sort of like “Okay” or “Alright” “Got it” in English. This one’s pretty straight forward.

**How Koreans use “아니”**

If you watch Korean shows, you might have noticed Koreans start their sentences with “아니” a lot - Even in situations where it’s not necessarily in response to a “yes or no” question.

“아니” does mean “No”. But in this specific context, they’re not trying to negate whatever it was said beforehand.

It’s rather used like a filler word at the beginning to add emotion to your sentence. We even have a slang that refers to “아니시에이팅” with “아니” and the English word “initiating” combined.

I’ll show you how 3 cases where 아니 can be used.

Note that I'm not going to cover the grammar in the example sentences that follow — So just focus on feeling how 아니시에이팅 works.

**1. To express surprise, doubt, or frustration**. - This usage is similar to saying "wait", or "But like" in English.

Ex: “아니, 거기를 네가 왜 갔어?” – “Wait, why did YOU go there?”

**2. To Make an Additional Explanation, Add an Excuse, or Offer a Denial**

This can be compared to "I mean" in English.

“아니, 방이 너무 더운거야” – “I mean, the room was too hot”

Then you would come up with some sort of follow up story after that. like “So, I opened the window.” or something like that.

**3. To Grab the Listener's Attention**

Similar to "Look," "Hey," or "By the way" in English.

Example: 아니, 근데 뭐 할까요?
→ "By the way, what should we do?"

I think this is one of those things that can’t really be “taught” logically. Instead, it is “learned” through your intuition. So, give yourself some time to get used to it. And I’m sure you’ll be able to “feel” it in time as you expose yourself to more input.

**When Yes means No**

Sometimes, in Korean, Yes means No and No means Yes. And this happens when you’re answering a negative question.

Let’s say someone asks you “Aren’t you hungry?”

In English, “Yes” would mean “Yes, I AM hungry” because **"Yes"** means “Yes” regardless of whether the question is negative or not.

But in Korean, **you respond to the negative in the question. So if you said,**

* **네** → This would mean "I’m NOT hungry" because it means “Yes, I agree with the negative”
* **And 아니요** would mean "I AM hungry" because it means “No, I disagree with the negative.”

This could take some time to get used to but to give you a tip, think of it like multiplying negative numbers: **(-1) × (-1) = (+1)**.
