### Basic Sentence Structure: SOV vs. SVO
The most fundamental difference between English and Korean is the word order. While English follows a Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) pattern, such as "I love you," Korean follows a Subject-Object-Verb (SOV) pattern. To say "I love you" in Korean, the conceptual order is "I you love." Using the dictionary forms of these words results in `나 (I)`, `너 (you)`, and `사랑하다 (to love)`. However, simply placing these words in order is grammatically incorrect and sounds unnatural. A proper sentence requires the addition of particles and verb conjugation: `나는 너를 사랑해`.

---

### Particles: Subject and Object Markers
Particles, also known as postpositions, are small functional words attached directly behind nouns to indicate their grammatical role in a sentence. The instructor focuses on two primary types: subject markers and object markers.

Subject markers indicate that the preceding noun is the subject of the sentence. The instructor introduces `은` and `는` as topic particles that function as subject markers. The choice between them depends on the final sound of the noun: if a noun ends in a vowel, like `나 (I)`, you use `는` to get `나는`. If a noun ends in a final consonant (받침), like `휴대폰 (cell phone)`, you use `은` to get `휴대폰은`. This rule exists to make pronunciation smoother. Other subject markers like `이` and `가` also exist, though their specific nuances are reserved for future lessons.

Object markers indicate that the preceding noun is the object receiving the action. The markers `을` and `를` follow the same phonetic rule as subject markers. `너 (you)` ends in a vowel, so it takes `를` to become `너를`. `휴대폰 (cell phone)` ends in a consonant, so it takes `을` to become `휴대폰을`. Interestingly, the instructor notes that in some contexts, subject markers like `은/는` can also mark objects to convey different nuances, and in casual speech, particles are sometimes omitted entirely, though beginners should practice with them included.

---

### Verb Conjugation: The Dictionary Form and the Stem
Every Korean verb has a base form, often called the dictionary form, which always ends in `다`. Examples include `사랑하다 (to love)`, `알다 (to know)`, and `먹다 (to eat)`. To use these in a sentence, they must be conjugated. Conjugation involves removing the `다` to find the "verb stem" and then adding a specific ending that carries meaning, such as tense or politeness level.

Unlike English, Korean does not have subject-verb agreement; the verb form does not change based on whether the subject is "I," "she," or "people." Instead, the form is determined by the last letter of the verb stem and the intended meaning or social context (formal vs. informal).

---

### Present Tense Conjugation Rules
The instructor outlines three primary patterns for present tense conjugation based on the verb stem:

For verbs ending in `하`, such as `사랑하다 (to love)` or `존경하다 (to respect)`, the `하` is replaced with `해`. Thus, `사랑하다` becomes `사랑해` and `존경하다` becomes `존경해`.

For verbs whose stem contains the vowel `아` and has a final consonant, such as `알다 (to know)` or `살다 (to live)`, the ending `아` is added. `알다` becomes `알아` and `살다` becomes `살아`. The instructor provides example sentences: `나는 방탄소년단을 알아` (I know BTS) and `나는 행복한 삶을 살아` (I live a happy life). Note that `행복한` is an adjective meaning "happy," and `삶` means "life."

For verbs whose stem contains vowels like `어` or `여` and has a final consonant, such as `먹다 (to eat)` or `열다 (to open)`, the ending `어` is added. `먹다` becomes `먹어` and `열다` becomes `열어`. Example sentences include `나는 야채를 먹어` (I eat vegetables) and `나는 문을 열어` (I open the door).

---

### Past Tense and Formality Levels
Past tense conjugation builds upon the present tense forms. To change a present tense verb to the past tense, the instructor demonstrates adding a double `ㅅ` (받침) followed by `어`.
- `사랑해` (present) → `사랑했어` (past)
- `알아` (present) → `알았어` (past)
- `먹어` (present) → `먹었어` (past)

Finally, the instructor introduces speech levels. The examples above are informal (반말). To make them polite/formal (존댓말), one simply adds `요` to the end: `사랑해요`, `알아요`, `먹어요`. For even higher formality, often used in business or official settings, the ending `습니다` is used: `사랑했습니다`, `알았습니다`, `먹었습니다`.
