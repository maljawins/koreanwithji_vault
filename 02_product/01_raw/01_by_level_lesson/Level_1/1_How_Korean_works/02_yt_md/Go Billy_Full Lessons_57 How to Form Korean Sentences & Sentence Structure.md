### The Core SOV Structure
The most significant difference between English and Korean is the placement of the verb. English follows a Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) order, such as "I kicked the ball." In this example, "I" is the subject, "kicked" is the action, and "the ball" is the object receiving the action. Korean, however, is a Subject-Object-Verb (SOV) language. This means the same sentence would be organized as "I the ball kicked." The instructor emphasizes that verbs must always come at the very end of the sentence.

This rule applies consistently to objects. For example, the English sentence "I eat snacks" becomes:
> **저는 과자를 먹어요.** / I snacks eat.

---

### Incorporating Locations
Just like objects, locations are placed before the verb in a Korean sentence. Whether the verb describes moving to a location or performing an action at a location, the place name precedes the final verb. The instructor provides two examples to show this consistency:
> **저는 학교에 가요.** / I to school go.
> **학교에서 공부해요.** / At school (I) study.

---

### Adding Time Markers
Time information, such as "today" or "tomorrow," is typically added at the very beginning of a Korean sentence or phrase. While English often places time at the end (e.g., "I study at school today"), Korean prioritizes it early. It is also grammatically acceptable to place the time marker immediately after the subject; doing so maintains the same meaning but adds a slight emphasis to the time itself.

When dealing with specific times, Korean follows a "large to small" logic. You must state the largest unit of time first, followed by smaller units. For example, to say "Today, I'm going to school at 2:30 p.m.," the order is: Today + p.m. + Hour + Minute + Location + Verb.
> **오늘 오후 2시 30분에 학교에 가요.** / Today p.m. 2:30 at school to go.

---

### Adverbs and Adjectives
Adverbs, which describe how an action is performed, must be placed directly before the verb they modify. Unlike English, where adverbs like "slowly" can sometimes move around, Korean requires them to stay fixed before the final verb.
> **내일 집에서 열심히 공부할 거예요.** / Tomorrow at home hard (I) will study.

Adjectives in Korean function similarly to English by appearing directly before the noun they describe. If a sentence requires multiple adjectives, they are connected together (often using the **-고** connector), and only the final adjective in the string is conjugated to modify the noun.
> **저는 더러운 방에서 공부할 거예요.** / I in a dirty room will study.
> **저는 크고 맛있는 케이크를 먹고 싶어요.** / I a big and delicious cake want to eat.
