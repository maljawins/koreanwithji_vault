## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor provides a comprehensive roadmap for students who have just finished learning Hangul and are unsure where to begin with grammar. The lesson is structured around seven fundamental pillars of the Korean language: word order, verbs, sentence endings, adjectives, adverbs, particles, and conjunctions. By understanding these seven areas, learners can form a "big picture" mental map of how the language functions before diving into specific conjugation rules.

The video emphasizes that Korean is a "verb-final" language, which is the most significant departure from English. Throughout the lesson, the instructor uses a handwritten sketchbook to visually demonstrate sentence structures, letter transformations, and the relationship between different parts of speech. The goal is not to memorize every vocabulary word shown, but to understand the underlying logic that governs Korean communication.

---

### Word Order: Subject-Object-Verb
The most fundamental rule of Korean grammar is the word order. In simple sentences consisting only of a subject and a verb, such as "I work" or "Mom cooks," the order is identical to English: Subject + Verb. However, as soon as an object is introduced, the structure changes. While English follows a Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) pattern (e.g., "I study Korean"), Korean follows a Subject-Object-Verb (SOV) pattern.

The instructor illustrates this by writing out the English sentence and then physically rearranging the components for the Korean equivalent. In Korean, the verb is always placed at the very end of the sentence. This means a sentence like "I study Korean" is structured as "I Korean study." This SOV structure is the foundation upon which all other grammar rules are built.

---

### Verbs and the Concept of the Stem
Korean verbs are characterized by having a "base form" (or dictionary form) that always ends in the syllable **다**. Examples shown include **먹다** (to eat), **자다** (to sleep), and **마시다** (to drink). The instructor explains that these base forms cannot be used directly in a sentence; they must be conjugated to reflect tense and politeness levels.

To conjugate a verb, one must identify the "stem," which is everything in the base form except for the final **다**. For instance, the stem of **먹다** is **먹**. Conjugation involves changing or adding to this stem based on specific rules, such as whether the stem ends in a vowel or a consonant (**받침**). The instructor notes that while most verbs follow regular patterns, there are irregular verbs that require rote memorization.

---

### Sentence Endings: Politeness and Formality
While the verb is the last "word" in a sentence, it is almost always combined with a sentence ending. These endings are crucial because they convey the relationship between the speaker and the listener. The instructor introduces the two primary categories: **반말** (informal/casual) and **존댓말** (formal/polite).

- **반말** is used with close friends, family members, or people younger than the speaker. In its plainest form, it often has no additional ending beyond the conjugated verb.
- **존댓말** is used with strangers, older people, or in professional settings. The most common polite ending is **-요**, which is why many Korean sentences end with this sound. A more formal version used in official settings is **-습니다**.

The instructor clarifies that "formal" in Korean doesn't just mean a black-tie event; it is the default way to show respect to anyone you aren't intimately close with.

---

### Adjectives as Descriptive Verbs
In Korean, adjectives function very differently than in English. They are often referred to as "descriptive verbs" because they behave exactly like action verbs. They have a base form ending in **다**, they have a stem, and they conjugate into present, past, and future tenses.

The instructor uses the example **예쁘다** (to be pretty). To say "Jisoo is pretty," the adjective must be conjugated into the present tense: **지수는 예뻐요**. However, if the adjective is used to directly modify a noun (e.g., "Pretty Jisoo"), it takes a different "modifier" form: **예쁜 지수**. This dual nature of adjectives—acting as a verb at the end of a sentence or as a modifier before a noun—is a key concept for beginners to grasp.

---

### Adverbs and Their Placement
Adverbs provide additional information about verbs, adjectives, or entire sentences. Because the verb must always come last in a Korean sentence, adverbs are typically placed immediately before the verb. For example, in the sentence "I sleep peacefully," the Korean structure is "I peacefully sleep" (**저는 평화롭게 자요**).

Many adverbs are derived from adjectives by adding specific endings, similar to adding "-ly" in English. The instructor points out that learning adjectives and adverbs often goes hand-in-hand. Additionally, phrases that function like adverbs (such as "at school" or "in Korean") are also placed before the verb in the sentence structure.

---

### Particles: Post-positions and Markers
Particles are perhaps the most unique element of Korean grammar for English speakers. They are "post-positions," meaning they are attached to the end of a noun to indicate that noun's role in the sentence.

- **Subject Marking Particles:** **이/가** or **은/는** are attached to the subject.
- **Object Marking Particles:** **을/를** are attached to the object.

The instructor explains that these markers allow Korean speakers to identify the subject and object regardless of word order, though the SOV order remains the standard. While these particles are sometimes dropped in casual conversation, they are essential for clarity and natural-sounding speech. Other particles function like English prepositions, such as **-에서** (at/in) and **-로** (by/using).

---

### Conjunctions and Sentence Merging
Conjunctions like "and" (**그리고**) and "but" (**하지만**) are used to connect sentences. However, Korean has a unique feature where these conjunctions can "merge" with the verb of the first sentence. Instead of having two separate sentences connected by a word, the verb ending itself changes to act as the connector. For example, the verb and the word for "but" can combine into a single conjugated form that carries the meaning of "but" while transitioning to the next clause.

---

### Context and Omission
A final, crucial "basic" of Korean is that the subject, the object, or both can be completely removed if they are obvious from the context. The most famous example is "I love you." In a full, grammatically "complete" sentence, it would be **저는 당신을 사랑해요** (I [subject marker] you [object marker] love). However, because it is obvious who is speaking and who is listening, Koreans simply say **사랑해** (Love). This reliance on context makes Korean sentences much simpler and more efficient in daily conversation.
