### What is a Sentence Connector?

A sentence connector is a piece of grammar used to join multiple independent sentences into a single, longer sentence. In English, these are words like "and," "but," "if," or "in order to." In Korean, these functions are performed by specific grammar suffixes or forms attached to verbs and adjectives. Common examples include **~고 (and)**, **~지만 (but)**, **~(으)려고 (in order to)**, and **~(으)면 (if)**. The instructor explains that mastering these connectors is the key to transitioning from a beginner level to a more natural, intermediate or advanced level of fluency.

---

### Basic Korean Sentence Structure (S.O.V.)

The instructor reviews the fundamental difference between English and Korean word order. While English follows a Subject-Verb-Object (S.V.O.) pattern, Korean follows a Subject-Object-Verb (S.O.V.) pattern. The verb must always come at the very end of the sentence.

> **저는 피자를 만들어요.** / I make pizza.

In this example, **저 (I)** is the subject, **피자 (pizza)** is the object, and **만들어요 (make)** is the verb. However, real-world Korean sentences are rarely this simple. They often include adjectives, adverbs, time markers, and locations. Billy demonstrates how adding a single adjective like **맛있는 (delicious)** changes the sentence:

> **저는 맛있는 피자를 만들어요.** / I make delicious pizza.

---

### Expanding the Sentence

To illustrate how sentences grow in complexity, the instructor adds more information to the base sentence, including time, location, and adverbs.

> **저는 오늘 집에서 맛있는 피자를 많이 만들 거예요.** / Today I will make a lot of delicious pizza at home.

Even this expanded sentence is considered "simple" because it lacks a sentence connector. By adding a connector like **~서 (because/so)**, the sentence becomes truly complex:

> **오늘 집에서 맛있는 피자를 많이 만들 거라서 치즈를 사야 돼요.** / I have to buy cheese because I'll make a lot of delicious pizza at home today.

The instructor notes that sentences can theoretically be infinite in length as long as you continue to chain pieces together with appropriate connectors.

### Detailed Sentence Formula and Word Order

For learners to understand where to place various parts of speech, the instructor provides a more detailed formula for standard Korean word order:

1.  **Time or Subject:** Usually the first part. If time includes multiple units (e.g., year, month, day, hour), it goes from the largest unit to the smallest.
2.  **Location:** Where the action happens.
3.  **Noun/Postpositional Noun:** People involved or spatial relationships (inside, behind, etc.).
4.  **Object:** The thing receiving the action.
5.  **Verb:** Always at the end.

Descriptions (adjectives for nouns and adverbs for verbs) always come immediately before the word they describe. For example, in the sentence **저는 내일 다른 도서관에서 한국어를 많이 공부할 거예요 (Tomorrow I'll study a lot of Korean at a different library)**, the adjective **다른 (different)** precedes **도서관 (library)**, and the adverb **많이 (a lot)** precedes the verb **공부할 거예요 (will study)**.

---

### Nuance and Emphasis through Reordering

While the S.O.V. structure is the standard, Korean is a relatively flexible language. Parts of a sentence can be moved around as long as the verb remains at the end. However, reordering words changes the nuance or emphasis of the sentence. The part of the sentence moved closer to the verb or placed in an unusual position tends to stand out more.

Comparison 1:
> **저는 도서관에서 한국어를 공부해요.** / I study Korean at the library. (Standard)
> **저는 한국어를 도서관에서 공부해요.** / At the library, I study Korean. (Emphasizes the location)

Comparison 2:
> **두 시부터 공부를 할 거예요.** / I'll study from two o'clock. (Standard)
> **공부를 두 시부터 할 거예요.** / From two o'clock, I'll study. (Emphasizes the time)

The instructor concludes by advising students not to stress over "perfect" structure initially, as the overall meaning usually remains clear even if the order is slightly non-standard. Reordering should eventually be done intentionally to convey specific nuances.
