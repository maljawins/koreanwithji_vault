## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor provides a comprehensive guide to Korean sentence structure, moving beyond the basic Subject-Object-Verb (SOV) model to explain how various parts of speech—including markers, adverbs, adjectives, and locations—are organized in real-world speech. The lesson emphasizes that while the SOV rule is the foundation, a natural Korean sentence involves a specific hierarchy of elements. The instructor uses a whiteboard to visually map out these components, demonstrating how to build complex sentences from scratch while warning against common beginner mistakes like dropping essential markers or misplacing adverbs.

---

### The Basic Foundation: SOV Structure

The most fundamental rule of Korean sentence organization is SOV: Subject, Object, and then Verb. The subject is the person or thing performing an action or being described. The object is the noun affected by the verb. Verbs, which always come at the very end of the sentence, include actions like `먹다 (to eat)` or `가다 (to go)`. For example, the English sentence "I like Korea" is reorganized in Korean as "I Korea like." The instructor writes this on the board as:

> **저는 한국을 좋아해요.** / I like Korea.

In this example, `저 (I)` is the subject, `한국 (Korea)` is the object, and `좋아해요 (like)` is the verb. While this simple structure is easy to learn, the instructor notes that real conversations are rarely this brief and require additional elements to function correctly.

---

### Understanding Korean Markers

Markers are essential particles placed directly after nouns to indicate their grammatical role. The instructor lists three primary types on the board: the object markers `을/를`, the subject markers `이/가`, and the topic markers `은/는`. A common point of confusion for beginners is the difference between the "Subject" in the SOV rule and the "Subject Marker." The instructor clarifies that the subject of a sentence can actually be marked by a topic marker or a subject marker depending on the context; the SOV "Subject" refers to the role, not necessarily the specific particle used.

The instructor strongly advises beginners against the common practice of dropping markers for the sake of "naturalness." While markers are sometimes omitted in casual spoken Korean, doing so as a learner can lead to vague or grammatically incorrect sentences. Keeping the markers helps the speaker and listener track the relationship between words as sentences grow longer.

---

### Adverbs and Adjectives

Adverbs and adjectives have strict placement rules in Korean. Adverbs, which describe how an action is performed, must always come before the verb or verb phrase. For example, to say "to eat quickly," the adverb `빨리 (quickly)` precedes the verb `먹다 (to eat)`, resulting in `빨리 먹다`. If multiple adverbs are used, such as "very quickly," they stack before the verb: `아주 빨리 먹어요`.

Adjectives must always be placed directly before the noun they describe. The instructor demonstrates this by transforming the descriptive verb `맛있다 (to be delicious)` into its adjective form `맛있는`. He writes:

> **저는 맛있는 치즈를 먹어요.** / I eat delicious cheese.

A critical warning is given regarding conjugation: you cannot use an adjective form at the end of a sentence or as an adverb. For instance, to say "Suzy smiled prettily," one cannot use the adjective `예쁜 (pretty)`; instead, it must be converted into the adverb `예쁘게 (prettily)`.

---

### Postpositions and Locations

In English, prepositions like "on," "under," or "inside" come before the noun. In Korean, these are "postpositions" because they follow the noun. The instructor illustrates this with a drawing of an airplane and a cloud, listing terms like `안 (inside)`, `박 (outside)`, `옆 (next to)`, and `위 (on top)`. To say "on the table," the order is "table on top" (`책상 위`).

When specifying a location for an action, the location usually appears toward the beginning of the sentence or right before the verb phrase. If you want to emphasize the location, place it at the very start. The instructor provides a complex example:

> **저는 집에서 미술을 공부하고 싶어요.** / I want to study art at home.

Here, `집에서 (at home)` is placed after the subject but before the object and verb. If an adverb like `천천히 (slowly)` were added, it would go directly before the verb, after the location and object.

---

### Combining Markers and Time Expressions

The instructor explains that markers can sometimes be combined, but they follow a strict hierarchy. For example, the particle `도 (also/too)` can be added to the dative marker `에게 (to)`, forming `에게도`. However, the order cannot be reversed. Topic markers `은/는` can follow many particles (like `에게는` or `에서는`) but can never be combined with subject markers `이/가` or object markers `을/를`.

Time expressions, such as `어제 (yesterday)`, are flexible but generally occupy two specific spots: at the very beginning of the sentence or immediately after the subject.

> **어제 저는 도서관에 갔어요.** / Yesterday I went to the library.
> **저는 어제 도서관에 갔어요.** / I went to the library yesterday.

---

### The Master Sentence Formula

To help students remember the order of all these elements, the instructor introduces a comprehensive (though humorously long) mnemonic: **T-A-S-M-L-P-O-A-V**. This stands for Time, Adjective (for subject), Subject, Marker, Location, Postposition, Object, Adverb, and Verb. While rarely used in its entirety, it serves as a guide for complex thoughts. He concludes by building a sentence using this logic: "Yesterday I saw a pretty monkey."

> **어제 저는 예쁜 원숭이를 봤어요.** / Yesterday I saw a pretty monkey.

- **Time:** `어제`
- **Subject:** `저는`
- **Adjective + Object:** `예쁜 원숭이를`
- **Verb:** `봤어요`
