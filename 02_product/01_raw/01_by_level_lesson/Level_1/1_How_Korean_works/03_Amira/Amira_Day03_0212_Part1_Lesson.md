## Teaching Points

### Compound Batchim (받침)
Ji explains that compound batchim consist of two different consonants at the bottom of a syllable. While there are 11 possible combinations, one (ㄹㅅ) is only used in Old Korean and can be ignored.
*   **Dominant Letter Rule:** In most pairs, the first letter is dominant. In three specific pairs (ㄹㄱ, ㄹㅁ, ㄹㅍ), the second letter is dominant.
*   **Pronunciation in Isolation:** Only the dominant sound is pronounced.
    *   **넋** (spirit): dominant is ㄱ → pronounced [넉].
    *   **앉다** (to sit): dominant is ㄴ → pronounced [안].
    *   **읽다** (to read): dominant is ㄱ → pronounced [익].
    *   **삶** (life): dominant is ㅁ → pronounced [삼].
    *   **읊다** (to recite): dominant is ㅍ (which becomes ㅂ) → pronounced [읍].
*   **Followed by Silent Consonant (ㅇ):** The second letter of the batchim moves over to the next syllable.
    *   **넋이** → [넉시]
    *   **앉아** → [안자]
    *   **읽어** → [일거]
*   **Followed by Non-Silent Consonants:** The dominant letter interacts with the following consonant using standard assimilation rules (amplification, aspiration, or nasalization).
    *   **Exception:** **닭** (chicken). Usually pronounced [닥], but when followed by a particle like **이**, it becomes **다기** [da-gi] rather than the expected [달기].

### The "STLOHV" Framework
Ji introduces an acronym to help organize Korean sentence components:
*   **S**ubject / **T**ime / **L**ocation / **O**bject / **H**ow / **V**erb.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** In Korean, the **Verb always comes at the end**.
*   **Particles:** These are markers attached to the end of nouns to indicate their role (e.g., 는/은 for subjects, 를/을 for objects, 에서 for location). Ji compares them to English prepositions but notes they are more essential for defining grammatical relationships.

### Language Philosophy and Strategy
*   **Contextual Nature:** Korean is highly contextual. Ji uses the example **사랑해** (I love you) to show that both the subject (I) and object (you) are often dropped because they are understood through context.
*   **Word Order Flexibility:** Unlike English, the order of S, T, L, O, and H can be rearranged to change the emphasis or "vibe" of the sentence, provided the verb remains at the end.
*   **The "Input-Process-Output" Method:** Ji advises against translating English thoughts into Korean. Instead, the student should take a Korean sentence (Input), decipher its logic using STLOHV (Process), and then create original sentences using that same structure (Output).
