## Lesson Overview

This recording continues the deep dive into Korean sentence structure, shifting focus from word order and particles to the complex world of Korean verbs. Ji explains that while word order is flexible, the verb must always remain at the end of the sentence. He introduces the concept of "descriptive verbs," explaining that in Korean, adjectives function like verbs when they are the predicate of a sentence. This conceptual shift is crucial for Amira to understand how to describe states of being versus actions.

The core of the lesson is dedicated to the anatomy of a Korean verb, which Ji breaks down using a plant analogy: the "Stem" (the root/base), the "Branch" (tense and honorifics), the "Leaf" (context markers and sentence types), and the "Fruit" (speech levels/respect). Ji emphasizes that while English verbs change based on the subject (e.g., "I dance" vs. "he dances"), Korean verbs remain the same regardless of the subject, but change drastically based on the relationship between the speaker and the listener.

Throughout the session, Ji uses visual aids, including a TikTok video he created, to illustrate these abstract concepts. Amira remains highly engaged, noting that the language feels very "vibe-based" and emotional rather than purely logical. ## Teaching Points

*   **Verb Position and Emphasis:** In Korean, the verb always comes at the very end of the sentence. The information placed immediately before the verb is typically the most important or emphasized part of the statement.
*   **Descriptive Verbs (Adjectives):** Adjectives in Korean are treated as verbs when they appear at the end of a sentence. For example, `조용하다` (to be quiet) is conjugated exactly like `공부하다` (to study). Ji labels these "descriptive verbs."
*   **Verb Anatomy (The Plant Analogy):**
    *   **Stem:** The base form of the verb after removing `다`. It remains unchanged during conjugation.
    *   **Branch (Tense/Honorifics):** Indicates when an action happened (e.g., `었/았` for past tense) or adds a layer of respect (`시/셔`).
    *   **Leaf (Context Markers/Sentence Types):** Adds nuance, emotion, or indicates the type of sentence (Declarative, Inquisitive, Propositive, Imperative). Examples include `~거든` (you know), `~잖아` (as you see/can't you see), and `~구나` (realization).
    *   **Fruit (Speech Level):** The final ending that determines the level of politeness (Casual `반말` vs. Polite `존댓말`).
*   **Speech Levels and Honorifics:**
    *   **Casual (`반말`):** Used with friends or those younger/lower in status.
    *   **Polite Informal (`요` style):** The most common form, used with strangers or in daily polite social interactions.
    *   **Polite Formal (`습니다` style):** Used in professional settings, news, or business.
    *   **Honorifics:** Specific word changes or additions (like `시`) used to show respect to the subject of the sentence (e.g., `먹다` becomes `드시다`).
*   **Particle Flexibility:** Particles change based on the speaker's intent. For example, changing the object particle `을/를` to `도` changes "I study Korean" to "I *also* study Korean."

## Lesson Flow

*   **Introduction to Verb Rules:** Ji starts by reinforcing that verbs must be at the end. He explains that the word right before the verb gets the most "shine" or emphasis.
*   **Adjectives as Verbs:** Ji shows a slide comparing `공부하다` (to study) and `조용하다` (to be quiet). He explains that because they both end in `하다`, they follow the same rules. Amira asks if this is the same for every word, and Ji confirms the stem remains unchanged while the endings do the work.
*   **The Verb Ending System:** Ji introduces his "Stem-Branch-Leaf-Fruit" analogy. He plays a video for Amira to visualize how a verb grows from a base stem into a complex expression of tense, mood, and respect.
*   **Context Markers and Nuance:** Ji provides several examples using the verb `먹다` (to eat). He demonstrates how `먹었잖아` can mean "I ate it, you know" or "Can't you see I ate it?" depending on the tone. Amira observes that Korean seems to express emotions through these endings that would require many extra words in English.
*   **Sentence Types:** Ji lists the four types: Declarative (`먹다`), Inquisitive (`먹니?`, `먹냐?`), Propositive (`먹자`), and Imperative (`먹어`, `먹으세요`). He notes that even within "asking a question," there are multiple ways to do it depending on the "vibe."
*   **Speech Levels (Respect):** Ji explains the hierarchical nature of Korean society and how it dictates language. He introduces `반말` (casual) and `존댓말` (polite), specifically highlighting the common `요` ending.
*   **Honorifics and Subject Dropping:** Ji shows how `읽다` (to read) changes to `읽으셔` when talking about someone of higher status. He explains that even if the subject (he/she) is dropped, the verb ending tells the listener exactly who is being spoken about.
*   **Practical Application (Hello):** Ji deconstructs `안녕하세요`. He explains it is actually a question ("Are you well?") based on the descriptive verb `안녕하다`.
*   **Q&A and Wrap-up:** Amira asks if object particles stay the same. Ji explains they change based on meaning (e.g., "Korean" vs. "Korean also" vs. "Only Korean"). Ji then outlines the homework: reading the textbook, watching videos, and writing lesson notes.
