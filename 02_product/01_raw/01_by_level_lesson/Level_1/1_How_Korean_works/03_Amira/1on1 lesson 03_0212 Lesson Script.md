**Compound 받침 인트로**

There are **11 compound 받침**, but don't worry—they're easier than they look.

ㄳ ㄵ ㄶ ㄺ ㄽ ㄾ ㅀ ㅄ ㄻ ㄼ ㄿ

First, you can ignore **ㄽ** because it’s only used in old Korean. I can't even think of any that has this 받침, so you can forget about it. That leaves us with **10 compound 받침**:

ㄳ ㄵ ㄶ ㄼ ㄾ ㅀ ㅄ ㄺ ㄻ ㄿ

I’ll make something clear. These only appear in **받침** positions, meaning you’ll never see them at the beginning of a syllable.

**Dominant letter of compound 받침**

Every compound 받침 has one **dominant letter**.

Except for **ㄺ, ㄻ, and ㄿ**, the **first letter** is the dominant one. And when there’s only one syllable, you only pronounce the dominant letter.

Here are some examples:

ㄳ - ‘넋’의 발음은 ‘넉’이다.
ㄵ - ‘앉’의 발음은 ‘안’이다.
ㄶ - ‘않’의 발음도 ‘안’이다.
ㄼ - ‘넓’의 발음은 ‘널’이다.
ㄾ - ‘핥’의 발음은 ‘할’이다.
ㅀ - ‘잃’의 발음은 ‘일’이다.
ㅄ - ‘값’의 발음은 ‘갑’이다.

For **ㄺ, ㄻ, and ㄿ**, the **second letter** is dominant:

ㄺ - ‘읽’의 발음은 ‘익’이다.
ㄻ – ‘삶’의 발음은 ‘삼’이다.
ㄿ – ‘읊’의 발음은 ‘읍’이다. 기억하는가? ㅍ 받침은 ㅂ 받침 소리가 난다는 것을?

**Consonant assimilation of compound 받침**

When these 받침 are followed by other consonants, the same consonant assimilation rules apply.

The only difference is that, no matter which letter is dominant, **all compound 받침 act as either amplifiers or aspirators**

1. **When followed by ‘ㅇ’ silent letter**

Let’s first look at the easiest rule. If the next syllable starts with ‘ㅇ’, the second letter in the compound 받침 moves to the next syllable.

넋이 > 넉시
앉아 > 안자
않아 > 아나 – Remember? ㅎ goes silent when followed by ‘ㅇ’ So, this is 아나
넓어 > 널버
핥아 > 할타
잃어 > 이러 – Again, ‘ㅎ’ followed by ‘ㅇ’ turns silent
값이 > 갑시

1. **When followed by non-silent consonants**

When followed by other consonants, all you need to do is just apply the usual consonant assimilation rules to the dominant letter. However, compound 받침 always act as either amplifiers or aspirators.

넋만 > 넉만 > 넝만
 - The dominant letter here is ㄱ. And remember ‘ㄱ’ 받침 met with nasal consonant becomes ‘ㅇ’ NG sound? So this is pronounced 넝만.

앉다 > 안따 – In this case, the dominant letter of ㄵ 받침 is ㄴ. But although ㄴ by itself, is not an amplifier 받침, ㄵ compound 받침 amplifies the 다 sound to 따. So, this is pronounced 앉따
않고 > 안코 – Remember ‘ㅎ’ 흐 sound? The ㅎ in ㄶ aspirates 고 to 코
넓다 > 널따 – Again ㄹ is the dominant letter but it amplifies the 다 to 따
넓혀 > 널펴
 - ㅂ sound combined with the aspirator ‘ㅎ’ in the following consonant, makes a ㅍ sound. So this is pronounced 널펴
핥다 > 할따 – Again, 다 is amplified to 따
잃지 > 일치 – Now again, ‘ㅎ’ in ㅀ aspirates 지 to 치 So this is 일치
값도 > 갑또 – ‘도’ is amplified to 또
값만 > 갑만 > 감만 – Remember when ㅂ 받침 is followed by ‘ㅁ sound becomes naturally turns into ㅁ? So this is 감만
읽는 > 익는 > 잉는 – Here, the dominant letter ‘ㄱ’ is followed by this ‘ㄴ’ sound, a nasal consonant, so this is 잉는.

Now the dominant letter of these compound 받침 is the second letter
읽다 > 익따 – So, this is 익따
삶다 > 삼따 – The dominant letter is ‘ㅁ’ but again ‘다’가 ‘따’로 amplify 된다.
읊다 > 읍따 – ‘ㅍ’ is the dominant letter and ㅍ in 받침 position turns into ㅂ sound. So, this is 읍따
On a sidenote, this is the only word that has ㄿ 받침.

The only exception is 닭. The ㄹ sound is completely silent. So this is pronounced 다기 not 달기.
And this is pronounced 다카나 not 달카나

**1) Introduction to Particles**

These words attached to the nouns (는, 에, 에서, 를) are called particles. They connect nouns and indicate their roles in a sentence.

Some particles act like prepositions in English (e.g., in, at, on), but these particles (**는, 을**) have no direct English equivalent.

But basically, they link words together to form sentences and indicate the roles of nouns.

**4) How verbs work in Korean**

**A) Adjectives in Korean are verbs**

First of all, in Korean, adjectives at the end of the sentence like “the room is quiet” rather than “a quiet room”, are treated exactly the same way as verbs.

공부하다: V. To study
조용하다: Adj. Quiet

하다 could mean to do or to be. By the way, 하다 is arguably the single most important verb in Korean. And we’re gonna learn 하다 really soon.

But as you can see in the example here both verb and adject ends with 하다.

공부하다 literally means “To do study”. And 조용하다 means “To be quiet” and we treat the act of being quiet itself as one verb, meaning adjectives and verbs follow the same conjugation rules. And we call these adjectives “descriptive verbs”.

**B) Verb Stem**

You might have wondered what I meant by **"Stem"** in the words I used as examples when we studied compound 받침 earlier.

In Korean, verbs are divided into two parts: **Stem** and **Ending**. Just like we attach particles to nouns, verb conjugation happens in the **Ending** part.

For example, the **dictionary form** of a Korean verb, before conjugation, has the **~다** ending, like **먹다** (to eat). If we remove **다**, we’re left with **먹**, which is the **stem** of the verb **먹다**.

The stem remains unchanged, and different endings can be attached to indicate various meanings, tenses, and levels of formality. This conjugation happens in the Ending part.

**C) Verb Ending - Sentence Types, Tense, Context marker, Speech Type**

The **ending** is where all the magic happens, and this is arguably the most difficult part of learning Korean. That’s because **Korean is incredibly expressive**, and the **nuance, tone, and subtle expressions** all depend on which **ending** is used. And these **subtle nuances** in verb endings may not feel intuitive to English speakers. That’s why I say the level of your proficiency is directly correlated to how **well you understand and use verb endings**. But to make things easier for you, I like to use this plant analogy to help you understand things visually.

The way endings change can be categorized into **five main types**, and since these types combine in different ways, there are **countless variations**. I’ll go through some examples to show you how this works.

By the way, you **don’t need to learn all of them right now**. Again, I’m just showing you these as a reference to help you understand **how Korean works as a language**. We'll break them down and learn them step by step later.

I’ll show you examples with the verb ‘먹다’ which means to eat.
 - Tense
 - Context marker (공식적인 grammar term은 Sentence ending이다)
 - Sentence Type (Statement, Question, Suggestion, Command)

**C-1) Tense**

Every language has a past, present, future tense.

These tenses come right after the stem. That’s why I call this part the branch.

Present Tense
- 읽어 – read (reed)

Past tense – The branch that has ㅆ 받침 indicates the past tense.
- 읽었어 - read (red)

Future tense – The branch that has ㄹ 받침 indicates the future tense
- 읽을 거야 – will read

**C-2) Context marker (Sentence endings)**
 - Although the official grammar term is sentence ending, I personally call this context marker because it literally marks the context of the verb. And this part comes after the branch so I also call this part Leaf. There are lots of different context markers but I’m just going to show you a few to help you get the idea what they’re for.

1) 먹거든 (Present)
 - I eat, you know
2) 먹잖아 (Past)
 - You know I eat (or can’t you see I’m reading when said aggressively)
3) 먹을래 (Future)
 - I’m reading it (in an affirmative tone like I’m DOING IT)
4) 먹지 (Present)
 - You see? I eat
5) 먹었구나 (Past)
 - Oh, you ate (Realization)
 > Mind you, there’s no subject here but it is clearly implied that the subject is a 2nd person because I would KNOW if I ate or not.

As you can see, you can express so much emotions and nuances with so much less words than English. And this is what I personally love about my language.

**C-3) Sentence Types**

And we also have 4 sentence types. And these all happen at the ending. I’ll show you

1) Declarative – For making statements
 - 읽어 : I read
2) Inquisitive – For asking questions
 - 읽어? : Do you read?
 - 읽니? : Kinder tone
 - 읽냐? : Playful tone (can be rude sometimes)
3) Propositive – Suggestion: “let’s”
 - 읽자 : Let’s read
4) Imperative – Telling someone to do it
 - 읽어(라) : Please read
 > You can say ‘읽어’ but adding 라 makes it sound stronger.

**C-4) Speech type (respect level)** - Polite speech (존댓말 - Formal & Informal) **-** Casual speech (반말 – spoken between friends)

Korean culture is highly **hierarchical**, and this is deeply reflected in the language. Because of this, speech is broadly divided into **polite speech** (aka **존댓말**) and **casual speech** (**반말**). Here, **말** means "speech."

Even within **존댓말**, there are two types: **formal** and **informal** polite speech.

 **Formal polite speech** is typically used in **news broadcasts** or **business settings**.

 **Informal polite speech** is what you commonly hear in **stores** or between **strangers**.

In terms of sentence structure, **speech type is reflected in the very last part of the verb ending**. That’s why I refer to this part as the **"fruit"** of the sentence.

1) 존댓말 (Formal)
 - 읽습니다 : I read
2) 존댓말 (Informal)
 - 읽어요 : I read
3) 반말 (Casual)
 - 읽어 : I read

**C-5) Honorifics**Because of this strong **culture of respect**, Korean has a system called **honorifics**. We use honorifics to to **humble ourself** while **showing respect to** the person being spoken to or about

In verbs, instead of using the **아/어 Branch** after the **stem**, you use **시/셔**.

Sometimes, completely different words are used to express the same meaning in a more respectful way.

We also have honorific versions of pronouns.양식의 맨 위

1) Honorific present
- 읽으세요: You read

2) Honorific past tense
- 읽으셨어요

3) Honorific future tense
- 읽으실 거예요

Even with just one verb, the number of possible variations is incredibly high due to the combinations of these five types of endings.

**But I have good news for you.** Unlike English, **Korean verbs do not change based on the subject.**
(For example: **I dance / He dances** – in Korean, the verb stays the same.)
