## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the concept of third-person pronouns (he, she, they) in Korean, known as 대명사. While English relies heavily on these words to avoid repetition, Korean handles them differently. The instructor explains that in natural spoken Korean, the most common strategy is to omit the subject entirely when the context is clear. When a pronoun is necessary, the formal dictionary terms are rarely used in conversation; instead, speakers use specific demonstrative-based alternatives that vary based on politeness levels and the physical distance of the person being discussed.

---

### The Strategy of Subject Omission
In Korean, if the speaker and listener both know who is being discussed, the subject is typically removed. The instructor provides examples where English requires "she," "he," or "they," but Korean simply proceeds to the description. For instance, instead of saying "She is smart," one says `똑똑해요 (is smart)` after mentioning the person's name in the previous sentence. This applies to all relationships, such as siblings or friends: `저는 남동생이 있어요. 13살이에요. (I have a younger brother. [He] is 13 years old.)`

---

### Dictionary Pronouns vs. Spoken Alternatives
The instructor notes that while a dictionary will list `그 (he)`, `그녀 (she)`, and `그들 (they)`, these terms are almost never used in spoken Korean. Using them in conversation can sound overly serious, dramatic, or "too romantic," like lyrics in a song or lines in a novel. Instead, spoken Korean uses combinations of demonstrative modifiers and nouns. The core modifiers are `이 (this)`, `그 (that/the - out of sight)`, and `저 (that - over there/within sight)`.

---

### Informal Third-Person Pronouns (반말)
For casual situations, the modifier is combined with `애 (child/kid/person)`. These forms are often abbreviated for ease of speech.
- **이 애** becomes **얘** (this person/he/she nearby)
- **그 애** becomes **걔** (that person/he/she mentioned/out of sight)
- **저 애** becomes **쟤** (that person/he/she over there)

The instructor emphasizes that `걔` is the most common way to say "he" or "she" when referring to a peer or someone younger who has already been mentioned in the conversation. An example of this is the K-pop song title `제가 개야`, which translates to "That person over there is the one [we talked about]."

---

### Polite Third-Person Pronouns (존댓말)
When referring to someone who deserves respect, such as a teacher, a parent, or an older stranger, the informal forms `얘/걔/쟤` must not be used. Instead, the honorific noun `분 (person)` is used with the modifiers.
- **이분** (this person - polite he/she)
- **그분** (that person - polite he/she)
- **저분** (that person over there - polite he/she)

Example: `이분은 우리 엄마야. (This person is my mom.)` or `그분은 친절하세요. (That person [he/she] is kind.)`

---

### Plural Pronouns (They)
To make these pronouns plural, the particle `들` is added to the end of the singular form.
- **Informal:** `얘들`, `걔들`, `쟤들`
- **Polite:** `이분들`, `그분들`, `저분들`

Additionally, words like `모두 (all)` or `다 (all)` can be used to refer to "they" or "all of them" collectively. For example, `그분들은 모두 친절해요 (They are all kind)`.

---

### Flexibility and Cultural Nuance
The instructor explains that Korean is very flexible with third-person references. Beyond standard pronouns, speakers often use specific titles or descriptions as pronouns.
- **Using Titles:** `이 친구 (this friend)`, `그 선생님 (that teacher)`, `저 학생 (that student)`.
- **Using Family Terms:** When talking about older siblings, even in casual speech, using `걔` can be rude. It is better to repeat the title: `언니들은 친절해요 (My older sisters are kind)` or use `모두 (all)`.
- **Possessives:** To say "his" or "her," the possessive particle `의` is added to the chosen pronoun, such as `그분의 수업 (that person's class / her class)`.
