## Teaching Points

### Overview
The plain form is a versatile speech level in Korean that serves specific functions distinct from the polite or formal honorifics. While it is not typically used in standard back-and-forth conversation with others, it is the standard for written media and specific spoken contexts. The instructor explains that mastering this form is essential for reading, writing, and understanding advanced grammar structures like quotations.

The lesson covers three primary areas: the specific social and literary situations where the plain form is required, the conjugation rules for both action and descriptive verbs across different tenses, and how to form negative statements. Visual aids demonstrate the transformation of verb stems and provide example sentences to illustrate the "announcement-like" tone the plain form carries in speech.

---

### When to Use the Plain Form
The instructor categorizes the use of the plain form into three main areas: writing, speech, and grammar combinations. In writing, it is the default form for newspaper articles, academic essays, and example sentences in textbooks. In speech, its use is more restricted; it is used when a person is talking to themselves (monologue) or when making a casual statement to a friend that feels like an announcement rather than a direct question or response. Finally, it is a foundational component for many complex grammar forms, most notably when quoting what someone else has said.

---

### Conjugation Rules: Present Tense
Conjugating the plain form in the present tense depends on whether the verb is an action verb or a descriptive verb. For action verbs, you take the verb stem and add the suffix ㄴ if the stem ends in a vowel, or 는 if the stem ends in a consonant, followed by 다. For example, the instructor shows how `가다 (to go)` becomes `간다` and `먹다 (to eat)` becomes `먹는다`. A special rule applies to action verbs ending in ㄹ: the ㄹ is dropped before adding ㄴ and 다, such as `놀다 (to play)` becoming `는다`. Descriptive verbs are much simpler in the plain form as they do not change at all from their dictionary form; `춥다 (to be cold)` remains `춥다`.

---

### Conjugation Rules: Past and Future Tense
The plain form for past and future tenses follows patterns established in previous lessons but ends with the specific 다 suffix. For the past tense, the formula is the verb stem plus the appropriate past tense marker `어/아/etc.` followed by `ㅆ다`. For example, `실수하다 (to make a mistake)` becomes `실수했다`. For the future tense, there are two common versions: the verb stem plus `겠다`, or the verb stem plus `(ㄹ/을) 것이다`. The instructor provides the example `공부할 거다` as a plain form version of the future "will study."

---

### Negative Sentences in Plain Form
To create negative sentences, the verb is first made negative and then the plain form rules are applied. For action verbs in the present tense, this results in the ending `지 않는다`. For descriptive verbs, the ending is `지 않다`. The instructor provides examples across tenses: `피곤하지 않았다 (wasn't tired)` for the past, `마시지 않는다` or `안 마신다 (don't drink)` for the present, and `도와주지 않을 거다 (will not help)` for the future.

---

### Examples and Nuance
The instructor provides several sentences to demonstrate the "announcing" feel of the plain form in speech. Because the plain form is not formal, the casual first-person pronoun `나` is used instead of the polite `저`.
- **철수는 병원에 간다.** / Cheolsoo goes to the hospital.
- **나는 과자를 먹는다.** / I eat snacks.
- **엄마는 우리와 함께 논다.** / Mom plays together with us.
- **내가 실수했다.** / I made a mistake.
- **내일 친구와 한국말을 공부할 거다.** / Tomorrow I'll study Korean with a friend.
- **오늘은 진짜 춥다.** / Today is really cold.
- **한국어 시험이 아주 어려웠다.** / The Korean test was very difficult.
- **밥을 안 먹으면 배가 고플 거다.** / If you do not eat, you will be hungry.
- **안 오면 내가 이걸 다 먹는다.** / If you don't come, I'm eating all of this.

The final example, "Elvis left the building" (**엘비스는 건물을 나갔다**), illustrates how the plain form functions as a factual statement or announcement.
