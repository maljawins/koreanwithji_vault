### Overview
This lesson focuses on the linguistic nuances of talking to oneself in Korean. The instructor explains that because there is no listener to show respect to, the standard rules of politeness are suspended. Using polite endings like the 요 form or formal speech when alone sounds unnatural. Instead, Koreans typically use casual speech, specifically the plain form, to express thoughts, exclamations, or questions directed at themselves.

The instructor covers the social etiquette of "self-talk" when others are present, emphasizing that body language and eye contact are the primary signals that distinguish a private thought from a casual announcement. The lesson also introduces specific grammar endings—such as 지, 나/가, and 더라—that carry different pragmatic weights when used in a self-reflective context.

---

### Basic Rules for Talking to Yourself
When you are talking to yourself, there is no need to be polite. Using the 요 form or formal speech would sound awkward because those forms are designed to show respect to a listener. Furthermore, you should never use honorific speech (to exalt yourself) or humble speech (to lower yourself) when you are the subject of your own self-talk. These are strictly for social interactions involving others.

In terms of pronouns, you should always use the casual **나** (I/me) instead of the polite **저**. For simple statements or exclamations about your current state, you can use either the standard casual ending or the plain form. For example, if you are cold, you might say:
- **추워!** / It's so cold!
- **춥다!** / It's so cold!

Both are acceptable, but the plain form (**춥다**) is very common for these types of spontaneous outbursts.

---

### Social Context: Self-Talk vs. Announcements
The instructor explains that the plain form can sometimes function as a "casual announcement" to people around you. To ensure that people realize you are only talking to yourself and not being rude by using casual speech toward them, you must manage your body language. Specifically, you should avoid eye contact.

If you say **춥다** while looking at someone, it sounds like an announcement: "Hey everyone, I'm cold!" If that person is a stranger or a superior, this could be seen as impolite. However, saying **춥다** while looking away or down at the floor clearly signals that you are just muttering to yourself, which is socially acceptable even in the presence of others.

---

### Exclamations and Honorifics
Exclamations follow the same logic of avoiding politeness toward oneself. In a restaurant, you might say to yourself **오, 맛있다!** (Oh, it tastes good!). It would sound strange to say **맛있어요** to yourself. However, once you turn to address the host or waiter, you must switch back to polite speech: **정말 맛있어요** (It tastes so good).

While you don't use honorifics for yourself, you still use them when talking to yourself *about* someone else who deserves respect. For example:
> **선생님이 도와주시겠지?** / The teacher will help me, right?
> **내가 이미 선물 드렸나?** / Did I already give them the present?

In these cases, the honorific suffix **-시-** and the humble verb **드리다** are used because the teacher or the recipient of the gift is the object of respect, even though the sentence is spoken in a casual self-talk ending.

---

### Question Endings for Self-Talk
When asking yourself a question or trying to confirm a thought, several specific endings are used:

The **-지** ending is used to confirm something you already suspect or to ask a direct question to yourself. It is very common with the past tense.
- **아, 뭐였지?** / Ah, what was that?
- **그 사람도 있었지?** / That person was there too (I'm certain).
- **그렇지.** / That's right (and I knew it).

The **-나** or **-가** endings create "soft questions." These express a sense of genuine curiosity or a less direct inquiry into one's own thoughts.
- **엄마였나?** / Was that Mom?
- **그런가?** / Really?
- **집에 없나?** / Are they not at home?
Note that these "soft" endings are generally not used with specific question words like **뭐** (what), **언제** (when), or **어디** (where). For those, you would use **-지** or the **-더라** ending.

The **-더라** ending is specifically for when you have forgotten something you once knew and are trying to recall it.
- **뭐였더라?** / What was that? (I can't remember).
- **어디 있었더라?** / Where was it? (I can't recall).
- **파티에 누가 왔더라?** / Who came to the party? (I can't remember).

---

### The Plain Form as a Question in Speech
While the plain form is usually for statements, it can be used with a rising intonation in casual speech to get someone's attention or to signal that more information is coming.
> **어제 철수를 만났다?** / Yesterday, I met Chul-soo (you know?)

This isn't a true question asking for information; it's a way of saying "Hey, listen to this," before continuing the story. This "attention-grabbing" quality explains why the plain form feels like an announcement when spoken aloud.
