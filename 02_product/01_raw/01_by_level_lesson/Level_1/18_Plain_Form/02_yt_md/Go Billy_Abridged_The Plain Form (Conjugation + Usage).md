## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces the "plain form" as a specific way to conjugate Korean verbs. He clarifies a common misconception: the plain form is not a speech level in itself, but rather a base conjugation that can function as different speech levels depending on the context. It is called "plain" because it lacks inherent politeness or formality markers. The lesson is structured to first teach the mechanical conjugation rules for present, past, and future tenses, and then move into the nuanced social and grammatical applications in both written and spoken Korean.

---

### Present Tense Conjugation

The conjugation rules for the present tense plain form differ based on whether the verb is an action verb or a descriptive verb. For action verbs, the instructor explains that you must look at the verb stem. If the stem ends in a vowel, you attach the consonant `ㄴ` followed by `다`. For example, `보다 (to see)` becomes `본다`. If the stem ends in a consonant, you attach `는다`. For example, `믿다 (to believe)` becomes `믿는다`. There is a specific exception for action verbs ending in `ㄹ`: you must remove the `ㄹ` before applying the vowel rule. Thus, `살다 (to live)` becomes `산다`.

Descriptive verbs are significantly simpler. The instructor demonstrates that the plain form of a descriptive verb is identical to its dictionary form. To conjugate a descriptive verb like `좋다 (to be good)` into the plain form, you simply keep it as `좋다`. He also notes that the progressive tense `~고 있다` follows the descriptive verb rule because it ends in `있다`, resulting in a plain form of `~고 있다`.

---

### Past and Future Tense Conjugation

To form the past tense plain form, the instructor directs students to first conjugate the verb into its standard past tense form and then identify the past tense stem (ending in `ㅆ`). You then simply attach `다` to that stem. For example, `좋아하다 (to like)` conjugates to the past tense `좋아했다`. For the verb `이다 (to be)`, the past tense plain form depends on the preceding sound: `이었다` after a consonant and `였다` after a vowel.

For the future tense, the instructor covers two methods. The first uses the `~겠다` suffix. You take the verb stem and attach `겠다` to form a statement, such as `보겠다 (will see)`. The second, more common method involves the `~(으)ㄹ 것이다` structure. The instructor explains that `것이다` is frequently shortened to `거다` in speech. Therefore, `하다` becomes `할 것이다` or the more natural `할 거다`.

---

### Usage Rules and Grammatical Integration

A fundamental rule of the plain form is that it is never paired with the humble first-person pronoun `저`. Instead, the plain pronoun `나` must be used. The instructor emphasizes that saying `저는 간다` is grammatically incorrect; it must be `나는 간다`. 

The instructor also points out that the plain form is often embedded within larger grammar structures rather than being the end of the sentence. A primary example is the citation form `~다고 생각하다 (to think that...)`. In the sentence `좋다고 생각해요 (I think it is good)`, the word `좋다` is in the plain form, but the sentence itself is polite because it ends in `~해요`. He clarifies that in this lesson, "plain form" specifically refers to making statements, not questions, commands, or suggestions.

---

### Plain Form in Writing

In written Korean, the plain form is used when the specific audience is unknown or when the writing is intended to be objective. The instructor lists several common contexts: advertisements (such as those seen on subways), formal essays or test answers (like the TOPIK exam), newspaper articles, and example sentences in textbooks. He explains that these contexts require a "plain" delivery because they aren't directed at a specific person in a dialogue. Additionally, the plain form is the standard choice for personal journals or diaries, as you are writing for yourself rather than addressing another person.

---

### Plain Form in Speech: Announcements and Self-Talk

When used in spoken Korean, the plain form is categorized as `반말` (casual speech) if directed at someone else. The instructor describes the "feeling" of using the plain form out loud as making an announcement. It sounds like you are using a megaphone to broadcast your actions or thoughts to get attention, such as saying `나 간다! (I'm leaving!)` to a group of friends.

Another major use in speech is "talking to yourself" (self-talk). When you express a thought out loud that isn't directed at anyone—like exclaiming `아, 춥다! (Oh, it's cold!)` while walking alone—the plain form is the most natural choice. Using polite forms like `추워요` when no one is around would be socially unusual. This type of self-talk is acceptable even if people you would normally speak politely to are nearby, provided you aren't making eye contact or directing the comment at them.

---

### The "Attention-Grabbing" Question Intonation

The instructor introduces a specific spoken nuance: using the plain form with a rising question intonation. While the plain form is technically for statements, ending it with a rising tone functions as a way to grab a listener's attention before starting a story. It acts like a "Hey, listen to this" signal. For example, saying `어제 파티에 찰수도 왔다?` with a rising tone translates to "So, Charsu came to the party yesterday (right/you know)?" This sets the stage for the speaker to continue their narrative.

---

### Example Sentences

The instructor provides a variety of examples to demonstrate the plain form in context:

> **나는 김치를 좋아하지 않는다.** / I do not like Kimchi.
