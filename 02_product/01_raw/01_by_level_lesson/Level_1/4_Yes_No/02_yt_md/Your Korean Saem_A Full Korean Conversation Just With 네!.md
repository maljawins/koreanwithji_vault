## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor begins with an observation from her morning subway commute: a man conducted an entire phone call using almost nothing but the word 네. While this might seem bizarre to a learner, she explains that it is perfectly normal in Korean because 네 carries multiple pragmatic functions beyond a simple "yes." The video aims to break down five distinct meanings of 네 to help students understand how a one-word conversation can make complete sense.

---

### Speech Level Context: 네 vs. 응

Before diving into the specific meanings, the instructor establishes the social context of the word. 네 is categorized as 존댓말, the honorific or polite form of speech. It is used when speaking to people who are older, higher in rank, or strangers. In casual situations (반말) with friends or younger people, 네 is replaced by 응. While the video focuses on 네, the instructor notes that for most of the meanings discussed, 응 performs the same functions in casual settings.

---

### Meaning 1: Responding When Addressed

The first function of 네 is as a response when someone calls your name or addresses you. This is equivalent to saying "Yes?" or "Here!" in English. The instructor provides the example of a teacher taking attendance in a classroom; whereas an American student might say "here," a Korean student responds with 네. 

This also applies to answering the phone. Because of caller ID, if you know who is calling, you can skip the standard 여보세요 (hello) and answer directly with 네 or 네 followed by the person's title. A visual example shows the instructor answering a call from her mother by saying:
> **네, 엄마** / Yes, Mom.

Similarly, she notes that her aunt might answer a call from her mother (the aunt's older sister-in-law) with **네, 언니**.

---

### Meaning 2: Answering a Yes/No Question

This is the most common meaning: using 네 as a direct affirmative answer. The instructor demonstrates this with a roleplay where one person asks:
> **주말에 저랑 같이 영화 보러 가실래요?** / Do you want to see a movie with me on the weekend?
> — **네.** / Yes.

A crucial grammatical distinction is made here: this is the only situation where 네 can be replaced by 아니요 (no) to change the meaning. For the other four pragmatic functions, 아니요 cannot be used as a substitute because those functions are about communication flow rather than factual affirmation.

---

### Meaning 3: Backchanneling (Showing You Are Listening)

In conversation, especially during long explanations or rants, staying completely silent can be perceived as not paying attention. In English, people use "uh-huh" or "mm-hmm." In Korean, speakers use 네 (or 응 in casual speech) as a "listening sound." It carries no specific meaning other than signaling to the speaker that you are still present and following along.

---

### Meaning 4: "What?" (Clarification and Disbelief)

The fourth meaning is divided into two sub-categories based on intent, both characterized by a rising intonation (**네?**).

Meaning 4-1 is for clarification. When you miss what someone said and need them to repeat it, you use a short, clipped 네? with a rising tone. This is equivalent to "Sorry?" or "Pardon?" 

Meaning 4-2 is for disbelief or shock. In this case, you heard the person perfectly, but the information is so surprising that you respond with an emphatic 네? to express astonishment. The instructor provides an example sentence:
> **저 이번 달까지만 일하고 그만두기로 했어요.** / I decided to work only until this month and then quit.
> — **네?!** / What?! (Disbelief)

---

### Meaning 5: Confirmation and Acknowledgment

The final meaning functions like "Okay," "Got it," or "I understand." It signals that you have received instructions or information and are giving your consent or confirmation. The instructor provides a workplace example:
> **그 보고서 오늘까지 좀 부탁드려요.** / Please have that report done by today.
> — **네.** / Got it. / Okay.

Another example shows the word used to acknowledge plans:
> **그럼 내일 4시에 봐요.** / Then I'll see you tomorrow at 4.
> — **네.** / Okay.

---

### Bonus: Ending a Phone Call

The instructor explains that Koreans rarely use standard "goodbye" phrases like 안녕히 가세요 or 안녕히 계세요 on the phone because those imply physical movement or staying in a location. Instead, phone calls are often ended using a "finalizing 네."

To signal the end of a call, speakers will often use a transition sentence followed by a lengthened 네 with a slightly rising pitch (**네~~**). Common transition sentences include:
- **그럼 내일 봐요.** / Then, see you tomorrow.
- **들어가세요.** / (Literally "Go in") Take care / Goodbye.
- **그럼 끊을게요.** / Then, I'll hang up.

The instructor notes that **들어가세요** is a very common way to end a call, acting as a signal that the parties are returning to their separate lives. Once one person says a lengthened 네~~, the other responds in kind, and they disconnect.
