### Overview

The instructor explores the versatility of the Korean word 네, which is commonly translated as "yes" but functions in numerous pragmatic ways depending on context, intonation, and repetition. While the video focuses on the polite form 네, the instructor notes that these same functions apply to the casual equivalents 응 or the common spoken variant 어. The lesson covers eleven distinct uses, ranging from simple agreement and acknowledgment to more complex functions like answering negative questions, backchanneling on the phone, and signaling the end of a conversation.

---

### Speech Levels: 네 vs. 응 vs. 어

The word **네** is the standard polite (존댓말) way to say "yes." It is used in formal situations or when speaking to someone who requires a level of respect. For casual situations with friends, speakers use **응**. In actual spoken conversation, **응** is frequently pronounced as a shorter **어**. The instructor emphasizes that all the functional meanings discussed in the video apply to these casual forms as well as the polite **네**.

---

### Basic Agreement and Acknowledgment

The most fundamental use of **네** is to express "yes" or to agree with someone. Beyond a simple affirmative, it often functions as "okay" or "all right." In this capacity, it frequently precedes other polite expressions to soften the transition or show immediate acknowledgment. For example, when receiving an instruction, one might say:
> **네, 알겠습니다.** / Yes, I understand.

Similarly, when someone greets you with **안녕하세요**, it is very common to respond by starting with **네** before returning the greeting:
> **네, 안녕하세요.** / Yes, (all right), hello.

---

### Using 네 to Mean "No"

A common point of confusion for English speakers is how to answer negative questions in Korean. In English, "yes" and "no" usually refer to the facts of the situation. In Korean, **네** and **아니요** refer to whether you agree with the speaker's statement. If someone asks a negative question like "Don't you like this?" and you do not like it, you answer with **네** because you are agreeing with their assessment that you don't like it.

The instructor demonstrates this with the sentence:
> **이거 좋아하지 않아요?** / Don't you like this?
> — **네.** / Yes (I don't like it).

In this specific grammatical context, **네** effectively translates to "No" in English because it confirms the negative state mentioned by the questioner.

---

### Backchanneling and Phone Etiquette

In Korean conversation, especially on the phone, it is culturally important to provide constant verbal feedback to show you are still listening. This is known as backchanneling. Without periodic interjections of **네, 네**, the person speaking may think the connection has been lost or that you are not paying attention.

There are also specific rules for starting and ending phone calls. While **여보세요** is used when you don't know who is calling, if you recognize the caller (via caller ID), you should start the call with **네** followed by their title or name. For example, if your boss calls, you would answer:
> **네, 사장님.** / Yes, Boss. (Hello, Boss.)

To end a call politely, Koreans use a lengthened **네** with a rising intonation: **네~~**. This serves as a signal that you are about to hang up. Usually, both parties will exchange this lengthened **네** before disconnecting.

---

### Intonation and Repetition

The meaning of **네** changes significantly based on how it is pronounced and how many times it is repeated.

A double **네, 네** with a longer first syllable and a shorter second syllable means "All right, I got it, don't worry." It is used when someone is giving you a reminder or a task and you want to reassure them that you've understood.

A quick, double **네, 네** said without a pause means "Yes, of course" or "Go ahead and speak." This is used to give someone permission to continue or to show enthusiastic agreement.

A short, clipped **네?** with a rising question intonation means "Sorry?" or "Pardon?" and is used when you didn't hear what was said. If this rising **네?** is drawn out longer, it shifts meaning to "What?" or "Huh?" expressing surprise or disbelief at what was just heard.

---

### Roll Call and Presence

When a teacher is taking attendance or someone is checking for your presence in a group, the standard response is **네**. While English speakers might say "Here," Koreans do not typically say **여기 있어요** (which literally means "It's over here") in a roll call setting. Instead, they simply say **네** to acknowledge their name being called.
