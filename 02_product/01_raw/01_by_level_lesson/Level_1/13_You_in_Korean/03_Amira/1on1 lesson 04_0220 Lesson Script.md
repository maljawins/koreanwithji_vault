**Honorifics (H)**

(H) in the chart stands for **honorifics**, used when referring to someone of higher status. For example, you can speak to a friend in Informal Low but still use an honorific verb form when talking about your grandmother.

**How to Identify Speech Type Easily**

1. If a sentence ends in **"요"**, it's **Informal High**. So if you just add “요” at the end even if you make a mistake, people will notice that you’re trying to polite.
2. If it ends in **"ㅂ니다"**, it's **Formal High**.
3. If it **doesn’t** end in "요" or "ㅂ니다," it’s **반말 (Low speech level)**.
4. If you see a **syllable starting with "ㅅ" right after the verb stem** (시, 셔, 세), it’s **honorific**.

**Yes & No in Korean**

네 and 예 in 존댓말, they’re inherently polite, so there’s no distinction between formal and informal and you don’t need to add any suffix. They’re interchangeable, but **네** is more common.

**And 응** and **어** (반말) are also interchangeable.

But for "No," **아니** technically means "not" rather than a direct "no," so you need to add a suffix to indicate politeness:

* **아니오** – Informal High
* **아니** – Informal Low
* **아닙니다** – Formal High

**When No means Yes in Korean**

In English, answering **"Yes"** means agreement regardless of whether the question is positive or negative.
(Aren’t you hungry? / Are you hungry? → "Yes" means you are hungry.)

But in Korean, **you respond to the negative in the question. So if you said,**

* **네** → "I am not hungry" (agreeing with the negative statement)
* **아니오** → "I am hungry" (disagreeing with the negative statement)

Think of it like multiplying negative numbers: **(-1) × (-1) = (+1)**.

**“How are you?” in Korean is awkward.**

In Korea, we don’t actually ask “How are you?” except for two cases

1) When it’s been a long time

2) When we want to know “how you are” literally. — like a doctor checking on a patient.

Koreans don’t think of **“how they are” on a daily basis**. So, unless it’s been a few months at least, asking “How are you?” can be really unnatural or it could even imply that something might be wrong with you.

**We ask “Did you eat?”**

Korea used to be extremely poor for centuries, and people would literally starve to death. So, although we’re doing pretty well now, food still means a lot to us.

so much that we even have this idiom “먹고 살다” which literally means “to eat and live” and it means “to make ends meet” That’s how important food is in our culture

**Thank you**

When it comes to saying "thank you" in Korean, there are two words: ‘고맙다’ and ‘감사하다’. And they both mean "to be grateful" or "to be thankful."

‘감사하다’ feels a little stronger, like a deeper expression of gratitude, while ‘고맙다’ is a bit more casual. So you’ll usually use 감사하다 in 존댓말. And for 반말, you only use 고맙다.

And since respect is important in Korean culture, we use formal high speech more often than informal high.

And it goes like, Repeat after me.

And also, note that **고맙다**, like **반갑다** (to be glad), is an irregular verb with the **ㅂ** 받침.

**I’m Sorry**

Just like we had two words for "thank you," there’s two words for saying sorry. **죄송하다** and **미안하다**.

**죄송하다** is more formal and expresses a deeper sense of apology, while **미안하다** is more casual.

So, just like with "thank you," the same logic applies here.

In **존댓말**, **죄송하다** is more commonly used than **미안하다**.

But in **반말 (casual speech)**, we only use **미안하다**.

And it goes repeat after me

죄송합니다

미안합니다

미안해

미안

미안해요

죄송해요

To keep things simple, I’d recommend using **죄송합니다** in 존댓말 and **미안해** in 반말.

**Never say sorry to say “I’m sorry to hear that”**

This is one of the most common mistakes by English speakers.

**These phrases we just learned are** only for apologies.

So, if you say **죄송합니다** to comfort someone, they’ll be like **“Why are YOU sorry?”**— or worse, they might think you’re the one to blame for what happened.

So, don’t say sorry unless it’s your fault. It’s as simple as that.

And there’s no direct translation for phrases like “I’m sorry to hear that” in Korean.

**하다 – The most important verb in Korean**

Before we move onto A does B, you need to know this:
As you probably know now, Korean **verb endings** are one of the hardest parts of the language.

But if you know how to use **하다**, you already know at least **half of Korean verbs**

Many Korean verbs are formed by combining a **word + 하다**.
For example, we just saw **안녕하다** (to be well).

This is why **하다 is the most important verb** in Korean. Dictionary says that it means “to do” but it’s so much more than that.

✅ **Examples:**
**친절하다** → **친절 (kindness) + 하다** → **To be kind**
**좋아하다** → **좋다 (to be good) + 하다** → **To like**
**말하다** → **말 (speech) + 하다** → **To talk / to say / to tell**

So **하다** turns a word into a verb — kind of like how **"-ment"** in "movement" or **"-ness"** in "kindness" turns words into nouns in English

And in the book 500 basic Korean verbs, there’s a concept called model verb and 하다 is one of them. There are 7 model verbs that follow similar conjugation rules. You’ll learn about them later but for now, so you can use 하다 verbs from that book.
