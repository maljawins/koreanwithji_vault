## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor addresses one of the most common points of confusion for Korean learners: how to translate the English word "you." While many languages have a direct equivalent, Korean culture and grammar favor omitting the subject entirely when context makes it clear. The lesson begins by reinforcing that "you" is usually assumed through the topic of conversation or specific polite verb endings that would never be used to describe oneself.

However, there are specific situations where a speaker must explicitly refer to the listener, such as when changing the topic or specifying a particular person among many. The instructor explains that instead of a single pronoun, Koreans use a variety of strategies based on social hierarchy and familiarity. These strategies primarily involve using the person's name with a polite suffix or using their social title. The lesson also covers specific words like 그쪽 and 당신, providing critical warnings about their usage to avoid sounding rude or disrespectful.

---

### Context and Omission
In Korean, it is not usually required to say "you" because the listener can assume the subject from the context. The instructor demonstrates this using the verb 배고프다 (to be hungry). A statement like 배고파요 simply means "I am hungry," while the same phrase with a rising intonation, 배고파요?, means "Are you hungry?" 

The use of honorifics further clarifies the subject. The instructor explains that the polite ending 세요 is used when speaking about someone else to show respect. Therefore, a question like 배고프세요? (Are you hungry?) inherently refers to "you" because a speaker would not use that specific polite form to talk about themselves.

---

### Using Names with 씨
When you are somewhat close to the listener and they are of a similar age or older, the most common way to say "you" is to use their name followed by the suffix 씨. This suffix functions similarly to "Mr." or "Mrs." and is a polite way to address someone. The instructor provides three examples of this in practice:
- **빌리 씨도 배고프세요?** / Are you hungry too, Billy?
- **현우 씨는 언어를 좋아하세요?** / Do you like languages, Hyunwoo?
- **올리버 씨는 한국어를 잘할 수 있으세요?** / Can you speak Korean well, Oliver?

In these examples, the instructor notes that the polite verb ending 세요 is used in conjunction with the name + 씨 format to maintain a consistent level of respect.

---

### Using Social Titles
If you are not close with the listener, or if they are significantly older or higher in rank, you should use their social title instead of their name. A title can refer to a professional role (like "teacher" or "boss") or a family relationship (like "older brother" or "grandmother"). The instructor lists several examples:
- **선생님은 지금 바쁘세요?** / Are you busy now, teacher?
- **아버지도 요리를 잘 하세요?** / Do you also cook well, father?
- **할머니는 오늘 은행에 가세요?** / Are you going to the bank today, grandmother?

The instructor emphasizes that using a title is the safest and most respectful way to address someone you do not know well or who is your senior.

---

### Other Words for "You": 그쪽 and 당신
The instructor introduces two specific words that function as pronouns but come with heavy usage restrictions. 

The first is **그쪽**, which literally means "that side." It can be used to address a stranger when you are unsure of their name or title. However, the instructor warns that this should only be used momentarily. Once you establish a better way to refer to them (like their title), you should stop using 그쪽, as continued use can sound rude.

The second is **당신**. While often found in dictionaries as the translation for "you," it is rarely used in spoken conversation. The instructor warns that saying 당신 directly to someone can sound very disrespectful or even aggressive. Its acceptable uses are limited to:
1. **Print and Speeches:** Used when the specific audience is unknown (e.g., advertisements or formal addresses).
2. **Married Couples:** Occasionally used between spouses to mean "darling" or "honey," though this is not extremely common.
