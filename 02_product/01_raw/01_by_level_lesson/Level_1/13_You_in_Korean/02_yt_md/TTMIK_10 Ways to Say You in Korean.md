## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor, 현우, addresses a common point of confusion for Korean learners: the lack of a single, universal word for "you." While many textbooks introduce a specific word for "you," using it incorrectly in real life can range from sounding overly poetic to being downright offensive. This lesson introduces ten distinct ways to refer to the person you are speaking to, categorized by social context, level of intimacy, and grammatical function. The instructor emphasizes that in many cases, the most natural way to say "you" is to avoid using a pronoun altogether and instead rely on titles or honorific verb endings.

---

### Literary and Romantic Terms: 당신 and 그대

The word **당신** is often the first word for "you" that learners encounter in textbooks, but the instructor warns that it is rarely used in spoken conversation. It has three primary contexts. First, it is a literary or poetic word used in song lyrics and movies. Second, it can be used between a husband and wife as a romantic term of address. Third, and most importantly for learners to avoid, it is used when two people are fighting and want to be confrontational. Because it is so literary, it feels unnatural in daily speech.

Similarly, **그대** is an extremely romantic and poetic word. It is frequently found in literature and love songs to mean "you," but it is never used in actual conversation. Using **그대** in person would sound like you are reciting a poem or singing a song, making it inappropriate for standard social interactions.

---

### Casual and Specific Pronouns: 너 and 자네

When speaking in **반말** (casual language), the standard word for "you" is **너**. The instructor notes that while this is the most direct translation of "you," it must be used with extreme caution. It is only appropriate for people you are very close to, people the same age as you, or people younger than you.

**자네** is a specific way to say "you" used by older people—typically those in their 50s, 60s, or 70s—when addressing someone significantly younger than them. While it is a recognized way to say "you," it is relatively rare in modern daily life compared to other titles and is restricted to this specific age hierarchy.

---

### Neutral and Situational Terms: 그쪽, 아저씨, and 아줌마

The word **그쪽** literally translates to "that side." It is used as a neutral way to refer to someone when you do not know their name or title well. It is common in text messages, online chats, and sometimes in person. However, the instructor cautions that it is not the most polite term; if used with the wrong tone, it can be perceived as slightly cold or offensive.

For middle-aged people, Koreans often use **아저씨** (for men) and **아줌마** (for women). While these are titles used to get someone's attention, they also function as the pronoun "you" within a sentence. For example, the instructor demonstrates the sentence:
> **아저씨는 어디 가세요?** / Where are you (mister) going?

The instructor notes that while men generally don't mind being called **아저씨**, one should be more careful with **아줌마**, as some women may find the term sensitive depending on their age and the context.

---

### Names, Titles, and the Suffixes -씨 and -님

One of the most common and polite ways to say "you" is to use the person's name followed by a suffix. The suffix **-씨** is standard for social acquaintances, while **-님** is more formal. The instructor uses a colleague, 예지, as an example, explaining that he uses **예지씨** as the subject of almost every sentence when speaking to her to avoid using a direct pronoun.

In professional settings, social titles are used instead of "you." Common examples shown on screen include:
- **사장님** (President of a business)
- **선생님** (Teacher)
- **의사선생님** (Doctor)

---

### The "Zero Pronoun" Strategy: Omission and Honorifics

The most advanced and natural way to say "you" in Korean is to omit the word entirely. Because Korean is a context-heavy language, if you are looking at someone and asking a question, they know you are referring to them. To make this clear and polite, speakers use honorific suffixes like **-시-** or honorific verb endings like **-세요**.

The instructor provides several examples where "you" is implied purely through the verb conjugation:
> **뭐 드실래요?** / What do you want to eat? (Using the honorific **드시다** instead of **먹다**)
> **어디에 사세요?** / Where do you live? (Using the **-세요** ending)
> **어떤 일 하세요?** / What kind of work do you do?

By using these honorific forms, the speaker signals that the subject of the sentence is the listener, making a literal word for "you" unnecessary. This is compared to the English imperative "Sit down, please," where the word "you" is understood but not spoken. In Korean, this omission happens in almost all types of sentences, not just commands.
