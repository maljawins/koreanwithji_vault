## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor addresses one of the most common points of confusion for Korean learners: how to say "you" in polite speech (존댓말). While the informal "you" is simply **너**, there is no single, direct equivalent in polite Korean that functions like the English word "you." Using a translator often yields the word **당신**, but the instructor warns that this word is rarely used in natural spoken conversation and can even be offensive if used incorrectly.

The lesson is structured to first clarify the specific, limited contexts where **당신** is actually used, and then to provide the four primary strategies Koreans use to address someone as "you" without being rude. The core takeaway is that Korean relies heavily on third-person titles, social roles, or complete omission of the subject rather than a second-person pronoun.

---

### The Problem with 당신 (Dangsin)
Although **당신** is technically a second-person pronoun meaning "you," it is not used in standard polite conversation. The instructor identifies four specific niches where it appears. First, it is used between married couples as a way to address each other, though it differs from **여보** (honey/dear) in that **당신** functions as a pronoun while **여보** is an affectionate call. Second, it is used in highly negative, hostile, or aggressive tones, often seen in K-dramas during confrontations. Examples shown include:
> **당신 누구야?** / Who are you? (Aggressive)
> **당신 뭐야? 당신 의사 맞아?** / What are you? Are you even a doctor?

Third, it appears in written materials like books, advertisements, or translated texts where the "you" refers to an unspecific general audience. Finally, it is used in poetic or romantic contexts, such as song lyrics (notably in songs by IU), where it sounds artistic rather than awkward. Outside of these four areas, using **당신** in person is generally discouraged.

---

### Strategy 1: Name + 씨 (Ssi) or 님 (Nim)
The most common way to say "you" is to use the person's name followed by a polite suffix. Using a first name alone is considered rude in 존댓말. The instructor demonstrates:
> **내일 제프 씨한테 전화드릴게요.** / I will give you (Jeff) a call tomorrow.
> **내일 Vicky 님한테 전화드릴게요.** / I will give you (Vicky) a call tomorrow.

If you do not know the person's name, the instructor suggests asking politely using phrases like **이름이 어떻게 되세요?** or the more formal **성함이 어떻게 되세요?** (What is your name?).

---

### Strategy 2: Using 그쪽 (Geujjok)
If you are too shy to ask for a name or are in a situation where you don't know it, you can use **그쪽**, which literally translates to "that side." This functions as a placeholder for "you" among people of similar age or status who are not yet close. However, it is inappropriate for addressing people significantly older, such as grandparents. A K-drama clip illustrates its use:
> **그쪽 기다렸어요.** / I was waiting for you.

---

### Strategy 3: Social Titles and Family Terms
Korean culture places high importance on social roles, and these roles are used as the primary way to say "you." The instructor categorizes these into family, school, and work. In family or close social circles, terms like **엄마** (Mom), **아빠** (Dad), **언니/누나** (Older Sister), and **오빠/형** (Older Brother) are used even when speaking directly to the person. For example, asking a mother "When are you coming?" becomes:
> **엄마 언제 와?** / Mom, when are (you) coming?

In school or work, titles like **선배** (Senior), **사장님** (Boss/CEO), or **선생님** (Teacher) are used. The instructor notes that for younger siblings or people of lower status, the informal **너** is acceptable. For middle-aged strangers, terms like **아저씨** (middle-aged man) or **아주머니** (middle-aged woman) are used.

---

### Strategy 4: Job Titles + 님 (Nim) or 선생님 (Seonsaengnim)
When addressing someone by their profession, you add **님** or **선생님** to the job title to mean "you." Calling someone just by their job title (e.g., "Hey, Doctor") is rude.
> **저는 의사 선생님을 믿어요.** / I trust you (Doctor).
> **저는 작가님의 팬이에요.** / I am your (Writer's) fan.

The word **선생님** is particularly versatile; it can be used to address teachers, doctors, or even total strangers to whom you wish to show high respect.

---

### Practice: Subject Omission and Translation
The instructor emphasizes that the easiest way to say "you" is often to delete the subject entirely if the context is clear. In the question "What are you doing tomorrow?", the "you" is implied:
> **내일 뭐 해?** (Informal) / What are (you) doing tomorrow?
> **내일 뭐 해요?** (Polite) / What are (you) doing tomorrow?

For the sentence "I like your personality," the instructor shows how the possessive "your" is replaced by the third-person title + **의** (possessive marker):
> **나는 너의 성격이 좋아.** (Informal) / I like your personality.
> **저는 선생님의 성격이 좋아요.** (Polite) / I like your (Teacher's) personality.
