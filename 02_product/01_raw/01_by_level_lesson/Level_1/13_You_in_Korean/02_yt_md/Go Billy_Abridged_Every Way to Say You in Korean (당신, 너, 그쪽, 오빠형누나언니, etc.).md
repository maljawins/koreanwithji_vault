## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor addresses one of the most common pitfalls for Korean learners: the search for a direct equivalent to the English word "you." In Korean, the concept of "you" is highly contextual and socially stratified. The most important rule established at the outset is that in natural Korean conversation, you usually do not need to say a word for "you" at all. When speaking directly to someone, the subject is assumed to be the listener unless specified otherwise.

The lesson transitions from the "omission rule" to a detailed breakdown of specific terms that are often mistaken for general-purpose pronouns. The instructor warns against the overreliance on dictionary translations like 당신 and explains the strict social conditions required to use the casual pronoun 너. The core of the lesson focuses on the culturally appropriate alternatives: using job titles, social relationships, or names with polite suffixes to refer to the person you are addressing.

---

### The Omission Rule: Why You Don't Need "You"
In English, sentences almost always require an explicit subject, but Korean is a pro-drop language where the subject is frequently omitted if it is clear from the context. When asking a question directly to someone, it is automatically assumed that the question is directed at them. The instructor provides the example:

> **어떻게 생각하세요?** / What do you think? (Literally: How think?)

There is no word for "you" in this sentence, yet it is perfectly clear. Similarly, asking **뭐 했어요?** (What did you do?) is the standard way to speak. If the speaker wanted to ask "What did *I* do?", they would specify the subject **제가** (I), but without it, the default target is the listener. This omission is further reinforced by grammar; for instance, the form **-고 싶다** (to want to) can only be used for the first person (I) or second person (you) in questions. Therefore, **어디 가고 싶어요?** can only mean "Where do *you* want to go?"

---

### The Role of Honorifics in Identifying the Subject
Honorifics provide another layer of clarity that makes explicit pronouns unnecessary. Honorific markers like the infix **-시-** or endings like **-세요** are only used when speaking about someone else, never about oneself. The instructor demonstrates this with:

> **한국말 잘하시네요.** / You speak Korean well.

Because of the honorific **-시-**, it is grammatically impossible for this sentence to mean "I speak Korean well." If you are looking at someone and using honorifics, the "you" is built into the verb itself.

---

### The Problem with 당신 (Dangsin)
Many beginners use **당신** because it is listed as "you" in dictionaries, but the instructor labels this as "wrong" for general use. It is a direct translation used only in specific, limited contexts. First, it is used when the "you" is completely unknown, such as in advertisements or textbooks addressing a generic reader. An example shown is a subway ad:

> **당신은 행복하세요?** / Are you happy?

Second, it is used by some married couples (typically those over 40) as a term of endearment similar to "dear" or "honey." Third, it can be used in a confrontational way during an argument to address a stranger rudely. Because of these specific nuances, learners are advised to avoid it in daily conversation.

---

### The Casual Pronoun 너 (Neo)
The word **너** is the closest equivalent to a casual "you," but it is restricted by strict social rules. It can only be used with close friends of the same age or people younger than the speaker. Using it with someone older or a stranger is considered extremely rude and "talking down" to them.

The instructor notes a common variation: **니** (Ni). While **니** is technically a dialect (Gyeongsang province), it is widely used in speech to avoid confusion between **내가** (I) and **네가** (you). Although spelled **네가**, it is almost always pronounced **니가** in standard conversation to distinguish it from the first-person subject.

---

### Appropriate Alternatives: Titles, Relationships, and Names
The most common and polite way to say "you" is to use the person's social standing or name.

**Job Titles:** If the person has a clear role, use that title + **님**.
- **선생님** (Teacher/Sir)
- **사장님** (Boss/Business owner)
- **손님** (Customer/Guest)

**Social Relationships:** Based on gender and relative age.
- **오빠 / 형** (Older brother/male friend)
- **언니 / 누나** (Older sister/female friend)
- **아저씨 / 아줌마** (Middle-aged man/woman)
- **할아버지 / 할머니** (Grandfather/Grandmother)

**Names:** When using a name, the rules change based on closeness.
- **Close friends:** Name + **이** (if it ends in a consonant). Example: **석진이**.
- **Acquaintances:** Name + **씨**. Example: **진영 씨**. This functions like "Mr." or "Ms." but is less formal.

---

### Specialized Terms: 그쪽, 그대, and 님
The instructor covers three final niche terms:
1. **그쪽** (Geujjok): Literally "that direction/side." It is used with strangers of similar status when you don't know their name or title. It is a polite way to say "you" without being overly intimate.
2. **그대** (Geudae): A poetic "you" found in K-pop lyrics and poetry. The instructor warns that using this in real speech is "cringe overload" and should be avoided.
3. **님** (Nim): Used primarily in online spaces. It can be attached to a username or used alone to address someone on the internet.
