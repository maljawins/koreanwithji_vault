## Teaching Points

### Overview
This lesson addresses one of the most complex aspects of the Korean language for English speakers: how to say "you." While English relies on a single pronoun, Korean offers a vast array of titles, honorifics, and social markers that change based on the speaker's relationship to the listener, their relative age, and their professional status. The instructor emphasizes that in many cases, the most natural way to say "you" is to omit the word entirely, as the subject is often implied through context and honorific verb endings.

The learning arc begins with a warning against the literal translation of "you" (당신) and moves toward the most common and polite strategy: using titles. The lesson covers professional titles, general respect markers like 씨 and 님, family terms used for both relatives and strangers, and specific terms for service workers. By the end of the video, students understand that "you" in Korean is not a single word but a social navigation tool used to show appropriate levels of respect and familiarity.

---

### The Problem with 당신 and Omission
The instructor explains that while 당신 is often the first word taught as "you" in textbooks, it is rarely appropriate in real-life conversation. It can sound impolite, confrontational, or overly impersonal. Its acceptable uses are limited to advertising (addressing a general, unknown audience), literal translations in textbooks, or occasionally between older married couples as a term of endearment similar to "darling." Because of these pitfalls, the primary rule in Korean is to omit the subject whenever possible. If you are looking at someone and using honorific speech, they will naturally understand that they are the subject of your sentence.

---

### Using 씨 for General Respect
The title 씨 is a standard way to show respect to someone of similar or lower status whom you are not on casual terms with. It translates roughly to Mr., Ms., or Mrs. and is used after a person's name. The instructor notes that 씨 can follow a first name, a last name, or a full name (e.g., 현우 씨). However, it should never be used for someone in a significantly higher position, such as a boss or a teacher, as it does not carry enough respect for those roles. Conversely, using it with very close friends can feel overly formal and awkward.

---

### Professional Titles and the Honorific 님
The most respectful way to address someone is by their job title followed by the honorific suffix 님. This is the standard in workplace environments. Common examples include:
- **사장님** (Boss/CEO)
- **과장님** (Section Chief)
- **대리님** (Deputy Section Chief)
- **회장님** (President/Chairman)
- **팀장님** (Team Leader)
- **고객님** (Customer)

These titles can stand alone or follow a person's last name (e.g., 박 사장님). The instructor highlights that 님 provides more respect than 씨 and should always be used with professional roles. He also notes that titles like **교수님** (Professor), **목사님** (Pastor), and **박사님** (Doctor/PhD) follow this same pattern.

---

### The Versatility of 선생님
While **선생님** literally means "teacher," its usage is much broader. It is the standard way to address any teacher (never use just 선생), but it also functions as a general term of respect for middle-aged or older adults, similar to "Sir" or "Madam." It is a safe, polite way to address a stranger or a customer when you don't know their name or specific title. Additionally, it is the common way to address a medical doctor in a clinical setting.

---

### Seniority and Juniority: 선배 and 후배
In schools, companies, or other institutions, Koreans use **선배** (senior) and **후배** (junior) to define relationships based on when someone joined the organization, regardless of their actual age. To show respect, these are often used with 님: **선배님** and **후배님**. While you can call a close senior simply 선배, it is considered inappropriate to call a junior 후배 to their face; instead, you should use their name + 씨 or just their name if speaking casually.

---

### Family Titles for Relatives and Strangers
Korean family titles are frequently used to address people who are not actually related to the speaker. This creates a sense of social "closeness" or acknowledges age-based hierarchy.
- **Grandparents:** **할머니** (Grandmother) and **할아버지** (Grandfather) can be used for any elderly person. For extra respect, use **할모님** or **할아버님**.
- **Parents:** **어머니/어머님** (Mother) and **아버지/아버님** (Father) are used for middle-aged adults.
- **Aunts and Uncles:** **이모** (Maternal Aunt) and **삼촌** (Uncle) are common for middle-aged strangers, particularly in casual settings like restaurants.
- **Siblings (by gender of speaker):** **형/누나** (used by males) and **오빠/언니** (used by females) are used for older friends.

The instructor warns that some people may find titles like **아줌마** (middle-aged woman) or **아저씨** (middle-aged man) slightly offensive or overly familiar depending on the context. When in doubt, using **여기요** or **저기요** (Excuse me) to get attention is the safest approach.

---

### Speaking in the Third Person
A unique feature of Korean is using titles to refer to oneself in the third person to emphasize a relationship. A father might say **아빠는...** (Dad is...) instead of "I am..." when talking to his child. Similarly, a teacher might refer to themselves as **선생님** when speaking to students. This style reinforces the social roles between the speaker and the listener.
