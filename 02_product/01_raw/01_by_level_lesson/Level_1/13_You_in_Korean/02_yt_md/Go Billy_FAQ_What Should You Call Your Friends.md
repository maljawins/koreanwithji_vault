## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor addresses a common misconception among Korean learners regarding the word 친구. While often translated simply as "friend," the term has a much narrower cultural definition in Korea, primarily referring to someone of the exact same age. The video explores how age hierarchy dictates social terminology and provides a variety of alternative expressions to use when referring to people you are close with who fall outside your specific age bracket.

---

### The Strict Definition of 친구
The word 친구 specifically refers to a friend who is the same age as you, calculated by birth year. To determine if someone is your 친구, you use your Korean age: take the current year, subtract your birth year, and add one. If two people were born in the same year, they are considered the same age and can call each other 친구. Consequently, a friend who is even one year older or younger is technically not your 친구. While native speakers may "give a pass" to non-native learners and use the word more loosely, or younger generations may use it for friends with a very small age gap, it is never used for someone significantly older, such as a teacher or an elderly neighbor.

---

### Describing Closeness with 친하다
Since 친구 is age-restricted, the instructor suggests using the descriptive verb 친하다 (to be close/friendly) to describe relationships regardless of age. By saying 우리는 친해요 (We are close), you convey friendship without triggering age-based titles. This verb can also be used as an adjective by conjugating it to 친한. This allows you to describe anyone you are close with, such as a 친한 선생님 (a teacher I am close with).

---

### Terms for Younger and Older Friends
When referring to a friend who is younger than you, the standard term is 동생. To specify the nature of the relationship, you can add adjectives. Using 아는 (from 알다, to know) creates 아는 동생, which implies a younger person you know or a casual friend. Using 친한 동생 implies a much closer younger friend. For older friends, you must use the standard hierarchical titles: 오빠 (older brother to a female), 형 (older brother to a male), 누나 (older sister to a male), or 언니 (older sister to a female). Just like with younger friends, you can add 친한 or 아는 to these titles to specify if they are a "close older brother figure" or just an "older male acquaintance."

---

### Workplace and Best Friend Terminology
In a professional setting, you should not use 친구 for coworkers. Instead, Koreans use 선배 (senior) and 후배 (junior). These terms are based on how long someone has been at an institution, not their biological age. A person who joined the company before you is your 선배 even if they are younger than you. For extra politeness, you can attach the honorific suffix 님 to get 선배님 or 후배님. For a "best friend," the instructor introduces the word 절친, which is a shortened version of the descriptive verb 절친하다 (to be very close).

---

### Acquaintances and "Squid Game" Slang
The word 지인 (acquaintance) is a useful formal term for someone you know but are not necessarily close with; it literally combines the characters for "to know" (지) and "person" (인). Finally, the instructor mentions the word 깐부, popularized by the drama *Squid Game*. While it refers to a very close friend or teammate who shares everything, it is not a common everyday word. Using it seriously might make you sound like a fan of the show, but it can be used jokingly with friends or teammates during a game to signify a "ride or die" bond.
