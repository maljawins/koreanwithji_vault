## Lesson Overview

This lesson focuses on expanding the student's ability to express nuances of exclusivity, addition, and desire in Korean. The primary objectives are to master the particles 도 (also/even), 만 (only), and 밖에 (nothing but), and to understand how they combine with other particles like 에, 에서, 부터, 까지, 에게, and 한테 to create specific meanings. The lesson also revisits the topic marker 은/는 to demonstrate how it functions as a contrast marker when attached to other particles.

In addition to particles, the lesson covers two forms of the progressive tense (~ing): 고 있다 for a broader timeframe and 는 중이다 for actions happening in the exact moment. Students will also learn how to express desire using 고 싶다 (I want to) and its more natural negative counterpart 기 싫다 (I don't want to/I dislike). Finally, the lesson introduces negative commands using the helping verb 말다 in the form 지 마세요 (Don't do it).

The lesson begins with a detailed review of a previous assignment, correcting common errors in irregular verb conjugation (ㄹ and ㅂ irregulars), particle usage (은/는 vs 이/가), and natural phrasing. This review serves as a foundation for the new grammar points, emphasizing that while some forms may be grammatically correct, native speakers often prefer specific structures for naturalness and emotional expression.

Here, you're saying "The clothes dried fast today," not "Clothes in general dried fast today," right? 은/는 is generally used to talk about general facts or to contrast. In this case, “Clothes in general dried fast today” doesn’t really make sense. So when you use 옷은, it automatically sounds like you're setting up a contrast.

- **On screen:** 도 – TOO, ALSO, EVEN; 만 – ONLY, JUST. Rules: Can be placed after ANY noun. Examples: 나도 네가 좋아 (I like you, too); 너는 왜 일만 해? (Why do you do only work?). - **Ji's teaching:** Let’s learn 도 and 만. 도 means "also" or "even," and 만 means "only." Both of these particles can be placed after any noun, and they can also be combined with other particles like 에, 에서, 부터, 까지, 에게, and 한테. They can be attached to verbs as well, but we’ll revisit those when we learn about sentence connectors like “even if” or “only when.”

### Slide 13 — 도 – Too, Also, Even: Placement
- **On screen:** "I also study math" has 3 meanings in English (highlighted: I also, math, study math). Rule: "In Korean, 도 (and 만) must be placed after the specific word you want to emphasize." Examples with yellow highlights: 저도 수학을 공부해요 (I, too); 저는 수학도 공부해요 (math, too); 저는 수학 공부도 해요 (also study math).
- **Ji's teaching:** In English, the sentence “I also study math” can have three different meanings depending on the emphasis: "I, too," "math as well," or "studying math is one of the things I do." In English, intonation and context dictate the meaning. But in Korean, the meaning changes based on where you place the particle. 저도 수학을 공부해요 means "I, too, study math." 저는 수학도 공부해요 means "I study math too (along with other subjects)." 저는 수학 공부도 해요 means "I also do math study (along with other activities)." This rule also applies to 만.

### Slide 14 — 도 – Too, Also, Even: Restrictions
- **On screen:** 도 can be placed after ANY NOUN (Subject, Object). 도 can be placed after other particles (Time, Location, Person). Yellow highlights on examples: 저도, 강아지도, 월요일에도, 집에서도, 친구한테도. Right side: "도 cannot be placed after Topic marker 은는, Subject marker 이가, Object marker 을를." Red "X" marks on incorrect forms like 저는도. "Instead, 도 replaces them."
- **Ji's teaching:** 저도 강아지를 좋아해요 means "I, too, like dogs." 저는 강아지도 좋아해요 means "I like dogs too (among other things)." In Korean, 도 covers both “also” and “even”—the difference comes from context. "Even" is just like a dramatic version of “also.” 도 can attach to other particles: 월요일에도 (even on Monday), 집에서도 (even at home), 친구한테도 (even to my friend). Note that 도 cannot be added after topic, subject, or object particles. Instead, 도 replaces them. You cannot say 저는도; you must say 저도.

### Slide 15 — 만 – Only, Just
- **On screen:** 만 can be placed after ANY NOUN or other particles. Yellow highlights on examples: 저만, 고기만, 일요일에만, 체육관에서만. Right side: "을 & 이 can be attached to 만 but it’s not necessary." Examples: 만을 / 만이. Bottom right: "만 vs 만은." Examples: 이 순간만 (this moment only); 이 순간만은 (this moment at least). Blue box: "I could forget other moments but at least not this moment."
- **Ji's teaching:** Just like 도, 만 can be placed after any noun, and where you place it changes the meaning. It can also be attached to other particles. The only difference is that you can use 만 with subject, topic, and object markers, but 만 should come before them, resulting in 만이, 만은, or 만을. Usually, you drop the 을 and 이 and just use 만. However, there is a slight difference between 만 and 만은. 난 이 순간만 기억해 is plain and factual: "This is the only moment I remember." But 이 순간만은 기억해 shows contrast: "I might forget other moments, but at least not this one."

### Slide 16 — 밖에 + Negative Verbs
- **On screen:** 밖에 literally means “outside of.” Rule: "밖에 is ONLY used with [Negative verbs]." Translation: “Anything but.” Examples with blue highlights on negative verbs and yellow on the noun+밖에: 학교에서 밖에 공부를 안 해요; 한 시간 밖에 없어요. Figurative use: 남자친구 밖에 몰라요 (She's deeply in love). Combination: 사무실에 밖에 없어요.
- **Ji's teaching:** There’s another way to say "only" in Korean: 밖에. It literally means “outside of” and can be translated as “anything but.” Importantly, 밖에 is always used with negative verbs. It works similarly to the word “any” in English when used in a negative sentence. 저는 학교에서밖에 공부를 안 해요 means "I don’t study anywhere but at school." 오늘 한 시간밖에 없어요 means "I don’t have anything but one hour today." It can also be used figuratively. 제 친구는 남자친구밖에 몰라요 means she’s so focused on him that she’s blind to anything else. Make sure to put a space between other particles and 밖에, like 사무실에 밖에, because 밖 is a separate word.

### Slide 17 — 밖에 vs 만 (note: script marker said Slide 8)
- **On screen:** Restrictions for 밖에: Cannot be used with ‘아니다’ (나만 남자야 O, 나밖에 남자가 아니야 X). Cannot be used in propositive/imperative sentences (이것만 해 O, 이것밖에 하지 마 X). Nuance comparison: "When 밖에 is more natural." Blue box explains that 밖에 implies something is lacking or not enough. Comparison table for positive/negative sentences.
- **Ji's teaching:** Let’s compare 만 and 밖에. First, 밖에 cannot be used with the descriptive verb 아니다. You must say 여기서 나만 남자야. Also, 밖에 can’t be used in propositive or imperative sentences. You say 이것만 해, not 이것밖에 하지 마. Sometimes 밖에 sounds more natural than 만. Both 저는 채소만 먹어요 and 저는 채소밖에 안 먹어요 mean “I only eat vegetables,” but the second one adds the nuance that something is lacking—perhaps you wish you could eat other things too. Finally, 만 can be used in both positive and negative sentences, but 밖에 is strictly for negative sentences.

### Slide 18 — To and from [People] (note: script marker said Slide 9)
- **On screen:** 에게, 한테, 께. Rule: Used only for people and animals. Examples: 아미라에게, 아미라한테. Note: "한테 is much more common in spoken Korean. 에게 is almost always only used in written Korean." Honorific: "께 is honorific version of 에게." Example: 선생님께 사탕을 드렸어요. Note: [주다 → 드리다].
- **Ji's teaching:** To say “to someone” for a person or animal, you must use a particle. You can say 아미라에게 or 아미라한테. 한테 is much more common in everyday spoken Korean, while 에게 is more formal and used in writing. There’s also an honorific version: 께. You use this for someone higher in status, like a teacher. When using 께, you should also switch the verb from 주다 to the humble version 드리다 to keep the tone consistent.

### Slide 19 — To and from [People] (note: script marker said Slide 10)
- **On screen:** 에게서, 한테서, 께. Rule: "서 is optional." Examples: 아미라에게(서), 아미라한테(서). Honorific: 부모님께. Note: "께서 does not mean from. 께서 is honorific subject marker (께서 = 이/가)." Examples comparing 께서 (subject) and 께 (from).
- **Ji's teaching:** To say “from someone,” you use 에게서, 한테서, or 께. The 서 is optional. 한테서 is spoken, 에게서 is formal. For someone you respect, use 께. Note that 께서 and 께 are not the same. 께서 is the honorific subject marker that replaces 이/가. For example, 선생님께서 저에게 사탕을 주셨어요 means "The teacher (subject) gave me a candy." But 선생님께 사탕을 받았어요 means "I received a candy from the teacher."

- **Ji's teaching:** When you attach 는 after other particles, you are highlighting contrast. 12시에는 수업이 있어요 implies you might be free at other times. 이 카페에서는 커피를 안 마셔요 could mean you drink coffee elsewhere, or you drink something else here. 여기부터는 제 자리입니다 sets a boundary that wasn't there before. 지금까지는 많이 참았습니다 implies that from now on, you won't be patient anymore. 왜 나한테는 과자밖에 안 줬어? compares what you got to what others received, often expressing a feeling of unfairness.

### Slide 28 — V.S 고 있다 – Progressive Tense (note: script marker said Slide 19)
- **On screen:** Action Verb Stem + 고 있다. Note: "Why Action verbs only? because you can’t be beautiful-ing." Red "X" on 예쁘고 있다. Rule: "All you have to do is conjugate 있다." Examples: 살고 있어요 (Present); 운전하고 있었어요 (Past); 알고 있을게 (Future/Promise).
- **Ji's teaching:** The progressive tense is formed by adding 고 있다 to an action verb stem. You can't use descriptive verbs like "pretty" because you can't be "beautiful-ing." To say "I am becoming pretty," we use a different structure. You only need to conjugate 있다. 살고 있어요 means the action is ongoing now. 운전하고 있었어요 describes what you were doing at a past moment. 나만 알고 있을게 is a common way to promise to keep a secret; it literally means "Only I will be knowing."

### Slide 29 — Important: 고 없다 is wrong
- **On screen:** "고 없다 is wrong." Rule: "To say, 'not doing', you have to negate 있다 with 안 or 지 않다." Examples: 음악을 안 듣고 있어요 (O); 음악을 듣고 있지 않아요 (O). Red "X" on 음악을 듣고 없어요.
- **Ji's teaching:** A very important point: 고 없다 does not exist. To say you are "not doing" something, you must negate the verb 있다 using 안 or 지 않다. So you say 음악을 안 듣고 있어요 or 음악을 듣고 있지 않아요.

### Slide 30 — V.S 는 중이다 – Progressive Tense (note: script marker said Slide 20)
- **On screen:** 중(中) is a Sino-Korean word meaning middle or center. ~는 중이다 = “to be in the middle of.” Works with Action verbs only. Examples: 가는 중이에요 (Present); 먹는 중이었어요 (Past); 샤워하는 중일거야 (Conjecture). Note: "This form doesn’t have a 'future' tense." Note: "You can remove '하는' with 하다 verbs" (운전 중이에요). Image of a sign: 영업중 (OPEN).
- **Ji's teaching:** 중 means "middle." ~는 중이다 literally means to be in the middle of an action. You conjugate 이다. 가는 중이에요 means "I'm on my way." 먹는 중이었어요 means you were eating right up until this moment. This form does not have a future tense; 중일 거야 is just a guess ("I guess she's in the middle of..."). With 하다 verbs, you can drop 하는 and just say 운전 중이에요. You'll see signs like 영업 중 (OPEN) all over Korea.

### Slide 31 — What’s the difference? (note: script marker said Slide 21)
- **On screen:** A comparison table between 고 있다 and 는 중이다. 고 있다: Broader time range (these days, recently), general routine. 는 중이다: Focuses on what is happening at the moment (right now). Timeline checkmarks show 고 있다 works for Present/Past/Future, while 는 중이다 works for Present/Past but NOT Future.
- **Ji's teaching:** Let’s compare them by time. 고 있다 covers a broader timeframe. 요즘 운동하고 있어 means you are working out these days, even if you aren't doing it this exact second. 는 중이다 focuses on the exact moment. 친구랑 얘기 중이야 means you are in the middle of the conversation right now. Again, 는 중이다 has no future tense; it's only for the present or past.

### Slide 32 — I want to~ ~고 싶다 (note: script marker said Slide 22)
- **On screen:** Action Verb Stem + 고 싶다. Rule: "This form can only be used with 1st person (I, we) and 2nd person (You)." Blue box: "because Korean doesn’t allow you to assume his, her, or their wants." Note: For 3rd person, use ~고 싶어 하다. Examples: 피자가 먹고 싶어요; 호주에 여행가고 싶어요; 왜 한국어를 배우고 싶어요?; 뭐 하고 싶어?
- **Ji's teaching:** ~고 싶다 means "want to." Crucially, you can only use this for the first person ("I want") or second person ("Do you want?"). Korean doesn't allow you to assume what someone else wants. For a third person (he/she/they), you must use ~고 싶어 하다, which indicates they are behaving as if they want something. Even when the subject is omitted in these examples, it's clear who we are talking about because of this rule.

### Slide 33 — Why 피자가 먹고 싶어요? (note: script marker said Slide 23)
- **On screen:** Both 피자가 먹고 싶어요 and 피자를 먹고 싶어요 are correct. Yellow highlight: "싶다 is a descriptive verb and works like 'to be desirable'." Explanation: In sentence 1, 피자 is the subject of 싶다. In sentence 2, 피자 is the object of 먹다. Note: "This form [Sentence 1] is more natural and common."
- **Ji's teaching:** Why do we sometimes use 가 instead of 를 with ~고 싶다? 싶다 is actually a descriptive verb meaning "to be desirable." In 피자가 먹고 싶어요, the pizza is the subject of "desirable." It's like saying "Pizza is the thing that is wanted." This is the most natural way to say it. In 피자를 먹고 싶어요, pizza is the object of "eat," and 싶다 acts like an auxiliary verb. This is also correct but sounds a bit more mechanical. To sound like a native, use 가/이.

### Slide 34 — Don’t want to~ ~기 싫다 (note: script marker said Slide 24)
- **On screen:** Negating ~고 싶다: 안 하고 싶다 or 하고 싶지 않다. Yellow highlight: "Action Verb Stem + -기 싫다." Note: "This directly expresses the idea of disliking or not wanting to do something." Examples: 가기 싫어요; 먹기 싫어요; 일하기 싫어요. Note: "This form is more natural and emotionally expressive."
- **Ji's teaching:** You can negate ~고 싶다 by saying 안 하고 싶어요 or 하고 싶지 않아요. These are correct but can sound a bit weak, like "it's not that I want to." If you really don't feel like doing something, use ~기 싫다. This uses the verb 싫다 (to be disliked/undesirable). It expresses that doing the action is unpleasant to you. It's very common when you're tired or not in the mood.

### Slide 35 — Example: ~기 싫다 (note: script marker said Slide 25)
- **On screen:** Dialogue example: 오늘 헬스장 갈 거예요? (Are you going to the gym today?) / 아니요, 오늘은 운동하기 싫어요 (No, I don’t feel like working out today). Yellow highlight: "기 싫어요 feels more natural."
- **Ji's teaching:** If someone asks if you're going to the gym, saying 운동하기 싫어요 sounds very natural and slightly more emotional than just saying you don't want to go.

### Slide 36 — What is 기? (note: script marker said Slide 26)
- **On screen:** Rule: "The suffix 기 turns the verb into a noun (called nominalization)." Green highlight on "nominalization." Example: 공부하다 → 공부하기 + 싫어요 = Act of studying is undesirable.
- **Ji's teaching:** The suffix 기 turns a verb into a noun. So 가기 싫어요 literally means "Going is unpleasant." You are nominalizing the action to describe your feeling toward it.

### Slide 37 — Try saying (note: script marker said Slide 27)
- **On screen:** Practice sentences: "I don’t want to wake up early" (일찍 일어나기 싫어요); "I don’t want to clean my room" (저는 방을 청소하기 싫어요).
- **Ji's teaching:** (Ji prompts the student to practice these sentences aloud.)

### Slide 38 — What’s the difference? (note: script marker said Slide 28)
- **On screen:** Comparison table between 안 하고 싶다 and 가기 싫다. 안 하고 싶다: Negated desire, focus on absence of want, slightly less natural. 가기 싫다: Dislike of action itself, focus on unwillingness, sounds more natural and commonly used. Summary: "In most cases, go with 기 싫다."
- **Ji's teaching:** Both are grammatically fine, but native speakers almost always default to ~기 싫다. 안 하고 싶어요 focuses on the absence of desire and sounds a bit formal. 가기 싫어요 expresses a natural, emotional reaction—like "Ugh, I really don't feel like it." In a professional setting, you might use 하고 싶지 않습니다, but otherwise, stick to 기 싫다.

### Slide 39 — Example sentences (note: script marker said Slide 29)
- **On screen:** Examples combining particles and desire: 카페에서만 공부하고 싶어요 (Only at a café); 저도 한국어를 배우고 싶어요 (I also); 물밖에 마시고 싶지 않아요 (Nothing but water); 집에서는 쉬고 싶어요 (At home, not elsewhere).
- **Ji's teaching:** Let’s combine everything. 카페에서만 공부하고 싶어요 means you only want to study at a café, perhaps not at home. 저도 한국어를 배우고 싶어요 is a polite way to express a shared desire. 물밖에 마시고 싶지 않아요 uses the 밖에 + negative structure to mean "I only want to drink water and nothing else." 집에서는 쉬고 싶어요 uses the contrast marker 는 to imply that while you might work hard elsewhere, at home you just want to rest.

### Slide 40 — Example sentences (note: script marker said Slide 30)
- **On screen:** Examples with ~기 싫다: 월요일에는 정말 일하기 싫어요 (Really don't want to work on Monday); 채소만 먹기 싫어요 (Don't want to eat only vegetables); 주말에는 일만 하기 싫어요 (Don't want to only work on weekends).
- **Ji's teaching:** 월요일에는 정말 일하기 싫어요 is a very relatable sentence! 채소만 먹기 싫어요 means you don't want ONLY vegetables—maybe you want meat too. 주말에는 일만 하기 싫어요 implies that weekends should be for more than just work.

### Slide 41 — I want something (note: script marker said Slide 31)
- **On screen:** Common mistake: 원하다 = "to want." Note: "Sounds unnatural in everyday speech... Often used in song lyrics or formal writing." Example: 김치를 원해요 sounds like "I desire kimchi" (too strong). Correct: 김치가 먹고 싶어요. Note: 원하다 is useful as a modifier (내가 원하는 것 = The thing that I want).
- **Ji's teaching:** Many learners try to use 원하다 because it translates to "to want." But in casual speech, 김치를 원해요 sounds way too poetic or dramatic, like "I desire kimchi." Native speakers say 김치가 먹고 싶어요. 원하다 is mostly for song lyrics, books, or formal writing. However, it is very useful as a modifier, like in 내가 원하는 것 (the thing that I want), which we will learn later.

### Slide 42 — More natural to say “I want to have something” (note: script marker said Slide 32)
- **On screen:** Rule: Use 가지다 (to have, possess) + 고 싶다. Note: "가지다 is shortened to 갖다." Example: 저는 새 휴대폰을 가지고 / 갖고 싶어요 (I want to have a new phone).
- **Ji's teaching:** If you want an object, you say "I want to have it." We use 가지다 (shortened to 갖다). So, 저는 새 휴대폰을 갖고 싶어요 is the natural way to say "I want a new phone." This is why we learned the "want to do" form first—it allows you to be flexible: "want to eat," "want to learn," or "want to have."

### Slide 43 — Don’t use 가지다 for everything (note: script marker said Slide 33)
- **On screen:** Note: In English, "have" is used for everything (dinner, a dog). Rule: "Using 가지다 for food or pets sound awkward." Red "X" on 햄버거를 가지고 싶어요 and 강아지를 가지고 싶어요. Correct: 햄버거를 먹고 싶어요; 강아지를 키우고 싶어요 (I want to raise a dog).
- **Ji's teaching:** In English, you "have" dinner or "have" a dog. But in Korean, 가지다 implies possession of an object. Saying 햄버거를 가지고 싶어요 sounds like you want to possess the burger, not eat it. For a dog, saying 강아지를 가지고 싶어요 sounds like you want to own it like a toy. Instead, say 햄버거를 먹고 싶어요 or 강아지를 키우고 싶어요 (I want to raise a dog).

### Slide 44 — Don’t do it: 지 말다
- **On screen:** Verb stem + 지 말다. Conjugation table for 말다: 하지 마 (I.L), 하지 마요 (I.H), 하지 마세요 (I.H Honorific - Most common), 하지 마십시오 (F.H). Note: "말다 is a helping verb (To stop, cease, not do)." Note: Can also mean "not this but that" (이거 말고 저거).
- **Ji's teaching:** To tell someone not to do something, attach 지 말다 to the verb stem. 말다 is an irregular verb ending in ㄹ. The most common polite form is 하지 마세요. 말다 is a helping verb and isn't used by itself. It can also mean "not A but B," as in 이거 말고 저거 (not this one, that one), which we'll cover in a future lesson.

## Teaching Points

- **Exclusivity and Addition (도, 만, 밖에):**
    - 도 (also/even) and 만 (only) replace subject (이/가), topic (은/는), and object (을/를) markers but follow other particles (에, 에서, 한테, etc.).
    - 밖에 (nothing but) must always be paired with a negative verb (안, 못, 없어요, 몰라요). It implies a sense of "lacking" or "not enough" compared to the more neutral 만.
    - The placement of these particles determines the focus of the sentence (e.g., 저도 vs. 수학도).

- **Recipient and Source (에게, 한테, 께):**
    - 에게 (formal/written) and 한테 (spoken) mark the recipient ("to").
    - Adding 서 (에게서, 한테서) marks the source ("from"), though 서 is often omitted in speech.
    - 께 is the honorific version for "to/from" a person of higher status.
    - 께서 is strictly an honorific subject marker (replacing 이/가) and does not mean "from."

- **Progressive Tenses:**
    - 고 있다: Used for actions ongoing over a broader period (e.g., "these days"). It is the only progressive form with a future tense.
    - 는 중이다: Used for actions happening in the immediate "middle" of the moment. It cannot be used for future plans (중일 거야 is a guess, not a plan).
    - Both forms apply only to action verbs, not descriptive verbs.

- **Expressing Desire:**
    - 고 싶다: Used for 1st person statements and 2nd person questions. It treats the desired object as the subject (using 이/가) because 싶다 is a descriptive verb ("to be desirable").
    - 기 싫다: The preferred way to express "don't want to" in casual speech, focusing on the unpleasantness of the action.
    - 원하다: Avoid in casual speech for objects; use 먹고 싶어요 or 갖고 싶어요 instead.

- **Negative Commands:**
    - Formed by Verb Stem + 지 말다.
    - The most common polite form is 지 마세요.
    - 말다 is a helping verb that requires a preceding verb stem.
