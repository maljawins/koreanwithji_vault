## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor provides a comprehensive guide on how to give commands and make suggestions in Korean, ranging from polite honorifics to casual speech among friends. The lesson emphasizes that the concept of "please" is often embedded within specific grammatical structures rather than being a direct translation of the English word. The arc of the lesson moves from basic polite commands using (으)세요, through the nuances of asking for favors with 주다, to various ways of suggesting activities like "shall we" or "let's."

---

### Polite Commands with (으)세요

The most common way to give a polite command is by attaching (으)세요 to a verb stem. If the verb stem ends in a consonant, you attach 으세요; if it ends in a vowel, you attach 세요. For example, the verb 가다 (to go) becomes 가세요. This form is inherently polite and functions similarly to adding "please" in English. The instructor notes that while the word 제발 technically means "please," it is rarely used in daily life and is reserved for situations involving desperate begging. Therefore, learners should rely on the (으)세요 form to convey politeness.

This structure is actually a regular 요-form conjugation that has been made honorific. Because it is honorific, it is used when addressing someone you should show respect to, such as a teacher, a boss, or a stranger. For example, 하세요 means "please do it," and 여기 보세요 means "please look here." It is important to remember that honorifics are only used when talking to or about someone else; you would never use this form to describe your own actions.

---

### Irregularities and Honorific Verbs

When using the (으)세요 ending, certain verbs follow specific rules. For verbs whose stems end in the letter ㄹ, the ㄹ is removed before attaching 세요. For example, the verb 닫다 (to close) becomes 닫으세요. Additionally, some verbs have entirely different honorific versions that must be used instead of just adding the standard ending. A prime example is 먹다 (to eat), which has the honorific form 드시다. To tell someone to eat politely, you must say 드세요 rather than 먹으세요.

---

### Casual Commands and the 라 Ending

In casual speech (반말) with close friends or people younger than you, commands can be formed by simply using the plain 요-form without the 요. For example, 가 (go) or 먹어 (eat). To make a command sound more distinct—though still casual—you can attach 라 to the end of the conjugated verb. The instructor demonstrates this with examples like 끝나면 집에 가라 (Go home when it's over) and 양치하고 자라 (Brush your teeth and go to sleep). He warns against using the verb stem plus 으라 (e.g., 하라), as this sounds archaic and biblical.

---

### Formal and Written Commands

There are more formal versions of commands that are less common in personal conversation but appear in announcements or signs. The (으)십시오 ending is a formal version of (으)세요. Another form often seen on doors or signs is the verb stem plus 시오, such as 미시오 (Push) or 당기시오 (Pull). These are impersonal, formal ways of giving directions in writing.

---

### Asking for Favors with 주다

The verb 주다 (to give) is central to asking for favors. The common phrase 주세요 literally means "give it to me honorifically." To ask for an object, you use the pattern Noun + 을/를 주세요, such as 사과를 주세요 (Please give me an apple). In casual settings, this becomes 줘요 or 줘.

To ask someone to perform an action as a favor, you conjugate a verb into the 요-form and then add 주세요. This creates the meaning "please do [verb] for me." For example, 사진을 찍어주세요 means "please take a photo (as a favor for me)." This is softer and more common than a direct command. Other examples include 제 숙제를 해주세요 (Please do my homework for me) and 알려주세요 (Please let me know).

---

### Softening Commands with the Adverb 좀

The adverb 좀 is a shortened form of 조금 (a little). While it can mean "a little," its primary function in commands is to soften the tone, making the request sound less harsh or demanding. Adding 좀 makes a sentence sound more like "could you please" rather than a direct order. For instance, 물 좀 주세요 (Could you give me some water, please?) sounds more natural and polite than just asking for water directly. Similarly, 공부 좀 하세요 (Please study) sounds like a gentle nudge rather than a strict command.

---

### Making Suggestions: "Wanna?" and "Shall we?"

To ask if someone wants to do something or to suggest an activity, you can use the (으)ㄹ래요? form. This is generally informal and asks about the other person's intention. For example, 이거 먹을래요? (Do you want to eat this?) or 같이 영화 보러 갈래요? (Do you want to go see a movie together?).

A more direct way to make a suggestion is using (으)ㄹ까요?, which translates well as "shall we?" or "should we?" For example, 영화 보러 갈까요? (Shall we go see a movie?). For an even softer, more indirect suggestion, you can use the pattern [Verb] + (으)면 어떨까요?, which means "How about if we [verb]?" An example provided is 같이 공부하면 어떨까요? (How about we study together?).

---

### Saying "Let's"

The most basic way to say "let's" in casual speech is to attach 자 to the verb stem, such as 가자 (let's go) or 밥 먹자 (let's eat). This form is strictly for close friends of the same age or younger. The instructor strongly advises against using the (으)읍시다 form (e.g., 합시다), as it can sound awkward or like you are talking down to someone. Instead, to say "let's" politely, you should use the word 같이 (together) followed by a polite 요-form verb. For example, 같이 가요 (let's go together) or 같이 놀아요 (let's hang out together).
