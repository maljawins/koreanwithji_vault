## Teaching Points

### Overview

This lesson focuses on unique honorific verbs that do not follow the standard rule of adding the `-(으)시다` suffix. While many Korean verbs can be made honorific by attaching this suffix to the verb stem, a specific set of high-frequency verbs has entirely different honorific counterparts. The instructor emphasizes that using the standard suffix on these specific verbs is grammatically incorrect. The lesson also explores the nuances of using honorifics with the `요` form, the application of honorifics to inanimate objects associated with respected persons, and how honorifics function within the progressive tense.

---

### Unique Honorific Verbs

The instructor introduces several essential verbs that have unique honorific forms. For the actions of eating or drinking, the standard verbs `먹다` and `마시다` both transform into the single honorific verb `드시다`. An example provided is `물을 더 드실래요? (Would you like to drink some more water?)`. The verb for sleeping, `자다`, becomes `주무시다`, as seen in the sentence `아버지는 거실에서 주무셨어요 (Father slept in the living room)`. When referring to someone passing away, the verb `죽다` is replaced by `돌아가시다`, exemplified by `증조 할아버지가 작년에 돌아가셨어요 (My great-grandfather passed away last year)`.

The verbs for existence and non-existence also have unique forms. `있다` becomes `계시다` when referring to a person's presence, such as in `선생님은 지금 어디 계세요? (Where is the teacher now?)`. Conversely, `없다` becomes `안 계시다`, as in `사장님은 사무실에 안 계세요 (The boss is not in the office)`. The instructor explicitly warns that creating honorifics by adding the suffix to the original stems—resulting in forms like `먹으시다`, `마시시다`, `자시다`, `죽으시다`, `있으시다`, or `없으시다`—is incorrect when talking about someone else.

---

### Combining Honorifics with the 요 Form

Honorific speech can be combined with the `요` form to increase the level of respect. While a standard sentence like `혹시 알아요? (By chance do you know?)` is not necessarily disrespectful, it is not particularly polite when addressing a complete stranger. To make this more respectful, one should use the honorific verb for `알다 (to know)`, which is `아시다`. This results in the more appropriate question `혹시 아세요?`.

The instructor clarifies a crucial point: honorific verbs are used when talking *about* someone you respect, not just when talking *to* them. For instance, while you use `아세요?` to ask a stranger if they know something, you would not use honorifics if you were asking that same stranger about a close friend. A sentence like `혹시 파란 모자를 쓴 남자애가 이쪽으로 안 지나갔나요? (By chance didn't a boy wearing a blue hat pass by this way?)` uses standard speech for the boy because he is not a subject requiring honorific respect, even though the speaker is talking to a stranger.

---

### Honorifics for Things and Indirect Respect

A common phenomenon in Korean involves using honorifics for inanimate objects or body parts belonging to a respected person. While `계시다` is for people, the forms `있으시다` and `없으시다` are used when referring to things. This is acceptable when the object is directly part of the person, such as `할머니 눈이 크셔 (Grandmother's eyes are big)`, or owned by them, like `새로 사신 가방은 정말 예쁘세요 (The new bag you bought is really pretty)`.

This "indirect honorific" style is frequently used by service workers to sound as polite as possible. A coffee shop worker might say `주문하신 커피가 나오셨습니다 (The coffee you have ordered has come out)`, effectively "honoring" the coffee because it belongs to the customer. Although some grammarians consider using honorifics for objects incorrect, the instructor notes that it is extremely common in daily life and service industry contexts.

---

### Honorifics with Friends and Sarcasm

Using honorific speech with friends is generally unnecessary and can sound awkward, overly fancy, or even annoying. However, it can be used for humorous effect or sarcasm. If a close friend makes a funny mistake, one might sarcastically say `와, 정말 잘하셨네요 (Wow, you did that really well!)`. The instructor cautions that sarcasm should only be used with very close friends and in situations where the joke is obvious to everyone involved.

---

### Honorifics in the Progressive Tense

In honorific speech, the progressive tense form `-고 있다` changes to `-고 계시다`. For example, the verb `하다 (to do)` becomes `하고 계시다`. The instructor explains that it is unnecessary to add an honorific suffix to the main verb if it doesn't have a unique honorific form; thus, `하고 계시다` is preferred over `하시고 계시다`. However, if a verb has a unique honorific form, that form should be used. For `먹다`, the correct progressive honorific is `드시고 계시다`, while `먹고 계시다` would be incorrect.
