## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the concept of honorific speech, known in Korean as 높임말. He clarifies that honorific speech is a separate linguistic layer from politeness levels like the 요 form or formal speech. While politeness levels are determined by who you are talking to, honorific speech is determined by who you are talking about. This means honorifics can be used within any politeness level, including casual speech, to show extra respect toward the subject of a sentence.

The lesson covers the fundamental rules of when to use honorifics, how to form honorific verbs using the suffix (으)시다, and how these verbs conjugate across different speech levels. The instructor emphasizes that honorifics are a way to acknowledge the social status or seniority of the person being discussed, regardless of whether they are present in the conversation.

---

### What Is Honorific Speech?
Honorific speech, or 높임말, is used to show "extra respect" or "honor" toward the person you are talking about. The instructor highlights a crucial distinction: this is not necessarily the person you are talking to. You use 높임말 when saying anything about someone else or asking questions about someone else. It does not matter if the person being discussed is in the room or listening to the conversation; the respect is inherent to the subject.

There are strict boundaries for honorific usage. First, you must never use honorific speech when talking about yourself, as it would sound awkward and arrogant. Second, it is generally not used for close friends or people to whom you wouldn't normally show extra formal respect. Third, honorifics are never used for inanimate objects because there is no social need to show respect to a thing. Finally, honorifics are not used for animals. The instructor explains that using the same level of respect for a pet as for its owner effectively puts the owner and the animal on the same social level, which is inappropriate in Korean culture.

---

### The (으)시다 Suffix and Verb Formation
The core of honorific speech involves transforming regular verbs into honorific verbs by attaching the suffix (으)시다 to the verb stem. The instructor provides specific rules for this transformation. If a verb stem ends in a vowel, you attach 시다. If it ends in a consonant, you attach 으시다. There are two major irregular rules: if the stem ends in ㄹ, you remove the ㄹ before attaching 시다; if the stem ends in ㅂ, you remove the ㅂ and attach 우시다.

The instructor demonstrates these transformations with several examples:
- **가다 (to go)** becomes **가시다**
- **읽다 (to read)** becomes **읽으시다**
- **살다 (to live)** becomes **사시다** (ㄹ dropped)
- **춥다 (to be cold)** becomes **추우시다** (ㅂ changed to 우)

Once a verb is made honorific, it can be conjugated into any politeness level. In the 요 form, the (으)시다 suffix specifically conjugates to (으)세요. For other forms, it follows standard conjugation patterns, often resulting in (으)셔 in casual speech or (으)십니다 in formal speech.

---

### Conjugating Honorific Verbs
To illustrate how honorific verbs function across speech levels, the instructor conjugates the honorific verb 하시다 (from 하다, to do):

- **Formal Speech:** 하십니다 (present), 하셨습니다 (past), 하시겠습니다 (future)
- **요 Form:** 하세요 (present), 하셨어요 (past), 하실 거예요 (future)
- **Casual Speech:** 하셔 (present), 하셨어 (past), 하실 거야 (future)

The instructor reiterates that because these forms show extra respect to the subject, you would never use them to say "I do" or "I am."

---

### Honorific Speech with Friends
A common point of confusion for learners is whether honorifics can be used in casual settings. The instructor explains that because honorifics are about the subject, not the listener, you can use casual endings while still using honorific verbs. For example, if a character named 철수 is speaking casually to his friend 영희 about her grandmother, he would use a casual ending but an honorific verb to respect the grandmother:

> **할머니는 어디 사셔?** / Where does your grandmother live?

Here, 사셔 is the casual conjugation of the honorific verb 사시다. However, if 철수 were speaking directly to the grandmother, he would use both the honorific verb and the polite 요 form:

> **어디 사세요?** / Where do you live?

Using honorific speech is essential because failing to use it when discussing someone like a teacher or a grandparent is considered impolite, regardless of who you are talking to or if the subject is present.

---

### When to Use Honorific Speech
The instructor provides a list of common situations and people that require honorific speech:
- Anyone older than you, including strangers you are not well-acquainted with.
- Teachers, bosses, leaders, or anyone of a higher rank.
- Grandparents and other people's parents or grandparents.
- Co-workers (unless a mutual decision is made to speak casually).
- Acquaintances you are not close with (excluding children).
- Your own parents (optional, depending on family dynamics).

A helpful tip offered is to focus on the "overall feel" of the conversation. If you want to express that you respect or "look up to" the person you are talking about, use honorifics. Conversely, older people or those of higher rank may choose not to use honorifics when talking about those younger or lower in rank.

### Asking Someone's Age
Since age determines the use of honorifics, it is common in Korea to ask someone's age or birth year upon meeting. The instructor provides the following variations:

**Asking Age:**
- **Casual:** 몇 살이야?
- **Informal (요 form):** 나이가 어떻게 돼요?
- **Honorific:** 나이가 어떻게 되세요?

**Asking Birth Year:**
- **Casual:** 몇 년생이야?
- **Informal:** 몇 년생이에요?
- **Honorific:** 몇 년생이세요?

### Listening Practice: Boss and Employee
The video includes a dialogue between a boss (사장님) and an employee (직원) to show honorifics in action. The employee uses honorifics to address the boss and discuss a customer, while the boss uses casual speech toward the employee but still uses honorifics to refer to the customer.

> **직원: 사장님, 지금 바쁘세요? 2시에 예약한 손님이 오셨습니다.**
> (Boss, are you busy now? The customer who made an appointment at 2:00 has arrived.)
> **사장님: 벌써 시간이 그렇게 됐어? 그럼 영수 씨가 손님께 가게 안내를 해 줄래?**
> (Is it already that time? Then, Young-su, would you show the customer around the store?)
> **직원: 네. 가게 안내만 하면 되나요?**
> (Yes. Is it okay if I just show them the store?)
> **사장님: 응. 통화 금방 끝나니까 그것만 부탁할게.**
> (Yes. My call will end soon, so please just do that.)

In this exchange, the employee uses 바쁘세요 (honorific of "to be busy") and 오셨습니다 (honorific of "to come") because he is speaking to/about superiors or customers. The boss uses casual endings (됐어, 줄래) with the employee but would use honorifics if referring to the customer's actions.

---

### Unique Honorific Verbs and Final Notes
The instructor concludes by noting that while (으)시다 is the standard way to create honorifics, some verbs have unique honorific forms that must be memorized. For example, 먹다 (to eat) does not become 먹으시다; instead, it becomes 드시다. 

He also provides a quick quiz and practice for transforming verbs:
- **좋아하다** → **좋아하시다**
- **이다** → **이시다**
- **힘들다** → **힘드시다**
- **귀엽다** → **귀여우시다**
- **보내다** → **보내시다**

Finally, he clarifies that while all honorific verbs contain the (으)시다 suffix, not every verb ending in those sounds is necessarily an honorific verb; it is simply the most common identifier.
