## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the concept of honorific nouns, which are specialized versions of common nouns used to show respect toward the person being discussed. Just as certain verbs have honorific equivalents (like using 드시다 instead of 먹다), specific nouns must be swapped out when speaking about someone of higher status, such as a teacher, boss, or grandparent.

The lesson emphasizes a critical rule: honorific nouns are only used when referring to someone else. You never use them to refer to yourself, your own belongings, or your own family members when speaking to others. The instructor covers five essential pairs: house, person, name, age, and birthday, providing example sentences to contrast the standard and honorific forms.

---

### What Are Honorific Nouns?
Honorific nouns are used in conjunction with honorific speech levels (존댓말). While not every noun in the Korean language has an honorific version, the most common ones related to a person's life and identity do. The instructor presents a summary list on screen:
- **집 (house)** becomes **댁**
- **명 (person counter)** or **사람 (person)** becomes **분**
- **이름 (name)** becomes **성함**
- **나이 (age)** becomes **연세**
- **생일 (birthday)** becomes **생신**

---

### Using 댁 (House)
The noun **댁** is the honorific version of **집**. You must continue to refer to your own home as **집**, but when discussing the residence of a teacher, grandparent, or boss, you use **댁**. The instructor demonstrates this with a contrast:
> **우리 집이 엉망이야.** / Our house is a mess.
> **할아버지 댁에 놀러 갔어요.** / I went to play at my grandfather's house.

When asking where someone lives, the choice of noun depends on the level of politeness required. **집이 어디예요?** is polite, but **댁이 어디입니까?** is much more formal and respectful.

---

### Using 분 (Person)
The word **분** serves as the honorific replacement for both the noun **사람 (person)** and the counter **명 (people)**. You use **명** for your own group or peers, but **분** for those you wish to respect.
> **파티에 몇 명이 왔어요?** / How many people came to the party?
> **몇 분이세요?** / How many people (in your group)?

The instructor notes that **분** is also the root for the honorific demonstratives **이분 (this person)**, **그분 (that person)**, and **저분 (that person over there)**. An example of using it as a noun is:
> **현우 씨는 아주 좋은 분이세요.** / Hyunwoo is a very good person.

---

### Using 성함 (Name)
**성함** is the honorific version of **이름**. While you always refer to your own name as **이름**, you should use **성함** when asking for the name of a stranger or a superior.
> **제 이름은 빌리입니다.** / My name is Billy.
> **성함이 어떻게 되세요?** / What is your name? (Honorific)

The instructor highlights that even when using the polite **어떻게 돼요?** ending, using **성함** elevates the respect level.

---

### Using 연세 (Age)
The honorific version of **나이** is **연세**. This is typically used for people significantly older than the speaker.
> **나이가 어떻게 돼요?** / How old are you? (Standard polite)
> **연세가 어떻게 되세요?** / How old are you? (Honorific)

The instructor includes a cultural tip: asking for someone's age can be sensitive or rude in certain contexts, similar to Western cultures. If you must ask, using the honorific **연세** is the safest and most respectful approach.

---

### Using 생신 (Birthday)
**생신** is used instead of **생일** for the birthdays of elders or superiors.
> **어제 제 생일이었어요.** / Yesterday was my birthday.
> **어제 우리 할머니 생신이었어요.** / Yesterday was my grandmother's birthday.

When asking a superior about their birthday, the formal question would be **생신이 언제입니까?** rather than the standard **생일이 언제예요?**.
