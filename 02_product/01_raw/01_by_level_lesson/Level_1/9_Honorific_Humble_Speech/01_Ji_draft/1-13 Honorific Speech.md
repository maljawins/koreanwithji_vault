🔹**Reminder – What is an honorific?**

I have seen many creators and teachers misuse the term “honorific” without properly defining what it even is. And this is not only confusing but downright misleading for so many learners. So, I’m going to say this once again as a reminder.

**Honorific and Speech Level are NOT the same thing.**

You use different speech levels depending on *who* you're talking *to* whereas you use honorifics to show respect to people you’re talking ABOUT like elders, teachers, your boss or clients.

When you’re talking to your best friend about your grandmother, you’d be speaking in 반말 (speech level) but still use an honorific to address your grandmother.

So, I repeat. Honorific and Speech Level are NOT the same thing.

Now that we got that out of the way, let’s get right into the lesson

**How to make honorific verbs**

Here’s how to make a honorific verbs. You add **시** or **으시** to the verb stem.

* If the stem ends in a vowel, just add **시**.
  + Example: **주다 (to give)** → **주시다** (to give [honorific])
    Now you have your new stem, 주시
* If the stem ends in a 받침 (final consonant), you add **으시**.
  + Example: **읽다 (to read)** → **읽으시다** (to read [honorific])
    And now, 읽으시 is your new stem.

**받침-sensitive - Reminder**

As you just saw, 시 and 으시 are “받침-sensitive”. I talked about this very common suffix pattern in level 1 if you remember.

You’ve already seen this with topic and subject marker: 은/는 and 이/가.

* After a **받침**, the following suffix **starts with ㅇ**, the empty consonant.
  (Like 은, 이)
* After a vowel though, the following suffix d**oesn’t start with ㅇ**.
  (Like 는, 가)

Most Korean suffixes, whether it’s a particle, sentence connector, they usually follow this rule with very few exceptions.

So from now on, I’m just going to refer to this whole idea as 받침-sensitive.

**Honorific verbs conjugation – Present Tense (Informal)**

So how do you conjugate honorific verbs? It’s the same rule. 시 doesn’t end in ㅗ or ㅏ, so it’s should be 어.

Let’s use 하다 - to do as an example.

In honorific form, 하다 becomes 하시다. Then when you conjugate it, 하시다 becomes

* Casual: 하셔
* Polite: 하셔요 or 하세요

First of all, in 반말, it’s always 셔**, not 세.** So, 하세 is just wrong.

Example: [엄마가 요리를 하셔 / 하세(X)]

**And as you can see, there are two versions for the polite speech.**

**I’ll talk about which one to use in a minute but let’s first look at** why we have two versions.

**Phonetically**, 시 + 어 becomes **셔**.
When you say 시어 quickly, it naturally sounds like 셔.

하시+어 → 하셔요

**Alphabetically**, 시 + 어 becomes **세**.
The vowels of 시 and 어, ㅣ and ㅓ, combines into ㅔ. [Show the movement of the vowels]

하시+어 → 하세요

Now, 세요 is much more common in modern Korean though you might hear 셔요 from time to time. But from my experience it was mostly from an older generation. So, I’d say just stick with 세요.

Well, that was the simple answer. And here’s the technical answer.

When you’re referring to a second person (Title+님, Name+씨), you’d almost always use 세요, not 셔요.

For example:

왜 거짓말을 하세요? – Why are you lying?

**하셔요** here sounds a bit like old Korean because you’re referring to the 2nd person.

**And when talking about a third person** (이 분, 저 분, 그 분)**, both 셔요 and 세요 sound natural.**

아버지가 영화를 보셔요 – My father watches (is watching) a movie.

아버지가 영화를 보세요.

So, both of these sentences sound perfectly fine.

But again, when in doubt, just stick with 세요 in polite speech and 셔 in casual speech.

**Honorific verbs conjugation – Present Tense (Formal)**

Formal speech is the easiest to make. Since the stem of honorific verbs always ends in 시, all you need to do is add ㅂ니다.

[Show 보시다 (Hon.) – Look, See, Watch]

할머니는 아기를 보십니다

So 보시다 becomes 보십니다.

And 적으시다, which comes from [적다 - to write down], becomes 적으십니다. The only difference is 시 and 으시 and the rest is the same.

Formal high 교수님꼐서 점수를 적으십니다.

**Irregular honorific verbs**

Just like every other language, Korean also has exceptions so not all verbs follow the 시 & 으시 pattern. Instead, they have completely different words in their honorific form.

But don’t worry there’s only a handful of verbs like this.

먹다 to eat and 마시다 to drink become 드시다. And why is 드시다 used for both 먹다 and 마시다?

In Korean, you can “eat” liquid, too. 먹다 technically refers to anything you put in your mouth. There’s also 잡수다 for 먹다 but this is not very common these days although you might hear it occasionally.

We also learned that 있다 turns into 계시다 in level 1, greetings are farewell.

자다 to sleep becomes 주무시다

And 죽다 to die becomes 돌아가시다. Now, 돌아가시다 is actually a compound verb – a combination of 돌다 to turn and 가시다 to go. So, to turn and go – To go back. So it’s a euphemism like “to pass away” in English

**These are just about all the irregular honorific verbs you need to know.** But here are some outdated ones just in case you might encounter them.

시장하시다 is an honorific version of 배고프다 To be hungry. Again, outdated. You could just apply the regular suffix rule and say 배고프시다 instead

편찮으시다 is an honorific version of 아프다 to be sick or ill. But nobody says this.

Let’s talk about humble verbs, now.

**Humble Speech**

What is humble speech?

Whereas we use honorifics to elevate someone, humble speech is for lowering ourselves to show respect.

Just like 나 - I, in humble speech is 저 and 우리 - we becomes 저희.

When it comes to humble VERBS though, this usually happens in interactive actions like giving, seeing, asking or telling. And there’s only a handful of humble verbs.

**만나다 or 보다 to meet or see becomes 뵙다.**

**묻다 to ask becomes 여쭈다.**

**주다 to give becomes 드리다**

**말하다 to tell, say, or speak becomes 말씀드리다.**

So for example, I gave a candy to grandma would be 할머니에게 사탕을 드렸어요, not 줬어요.
