## Lesson Overview

In this lesson, Ji guides Amira through an expansion of her foundational Korean knowledge, moving beyond simple "A is B" sentences to more complex structures and nuanced grammar. The primary focus is on broadening the range of subjects available to the learner by introducing a comprehensive set of pronouns, including plural forms, third-person references, and honorific variations. Ji emphasizes the cultural importance of distinguishing between people and objects when using pronouns, a key aspect of polite Korean speech.

A significant portion of the lesson is dedicated to the distinction between the topic marker 은/는 and the subject marker 이/가. Ji acknowledges that this is one of the most challenging concepts for learners to master, as it relies heavily on context and nuance rather than direct English equivalents. Through the example of "A is not B" (아니다), the lesson explores how these markers shift emphasis within a sentence and respond to different types of questions.

Finally, the lesson introduces the STLOHV framework, specifically focusing on the S O V (Subject-Object-Verb) sentence structure. Amira learns how to use the object particle 을/를 and explores the versatility of the verb 하다, which serves as a "verb-maker" for a vast number of Korean words. Ji uses a "plant analogy" to explain the irregular conjugation of 하다, helping Amira visualize how stems, branches, and fruits combine to form different speech levels.

### Slide 4 — 3rd Person Pronouns in Korean
- **On screen:** The format 이, 저, 그 + Person. Notes on gender-neutrality and the importance of distinguishing people from things (것).
- **Visual info:** A red "X" marks "This is my boss" (addressing a person as a thing) and a blue "O" marks "This person is my boss."
- **Ji's teaching:** Before we dive into 3rd-person pronouns, let’s cover a few key things. Pronouns for people in Korean are typically gender-neutral. Most follow the "this/that + person" format. Remember 이, 저, 그 (This and that)? The same logic applies to people. In Korean, it’s crucial to distinguish people from objects. It is considered rude to address people as things (것). So, we should say “This person is my boss” instead of “This is my boss.”

### Slide 5 — She, He, They
- **On screen:** A table for 3rd person pronouns. Low forms: 얘, 쟤, 걔. Neutral: 이/저/그 + Noun. Honorific: 이 분, 저 분, 그 분. Plural forms: 얘네(들), 이 분들, etc.
- **Visual info:** 그녀 (She) and 그 (He) are marked as "Only in Books." 들 is noted as required for 분들 to indicate plurality.
- **Ji's teaching:** If the person is your friend, you use 얘 (this person), 쟤 (that person), or 걔 (that person). Repeat after me: 얘는 제 친구예요, 쟤는 제 남동생이에요, 걔는 제 여동생이에요. For honorifics, we add 분 after 이, 저, and 그: 이 분, 저 분, 그 분. For casual "they," 얘네들 or 얘네 is more proper than 얘들, 쟤들, 걔들. Repeat after me: 얘네는 제 자식들입니다. 쟤들은 내 아들들이야. 걔네들은 제 딸이에요. Since the subject already shows plurality, you can drop 들 or keep it. However, for honorific "they," 들 is required: 이 분들은 제 동료들입니다. 저 분들은 여러분의 고객들이에요. 그 분들은 내 손님들이야. You can drop 들 in the predicate (like 동료 instead of 동료들) because 이 분들 makes plurality clear. We do have gender-specific pronouns 그녀 and 그, but these are almost never used in real life. They sound poetic or old-fashioned. 이들, 저들, 그들 are neutral but mostly heard on the news, not in everyday speech.

### Slide 6 — Possessive Adjectives (My, Your, his, etc)
- **On screen:** The possessive particle 의. Equivalent to [Of] or ['S].
- **Visual info:** Yellow highlighting notes that 의 is often shortened or omitted when combined with pronouns, causing the pronoun to act like an adjective.
- **Ji's teaching:** To say things like "my," "your," or "his," you add 의, the possessive particle. It’s the equivalent of "of" or "‘s" in English. 의 is pronounced as 에 when used as a possessive marker. When combined with certain pronouns, 의 is often shortened or completely omitted. When 의 is omitted, the pronoun ends up acting like an adjective. For example, 우리의 책상 becomes 우리 책상 (Our desk).

### Slide 7 — My, Your, Our

Korean usually follows spelling for pronunciation, but this is a rare exception! For plural "your," you can keep or drop 의. Repeat: 이건 너희 장비야. 저 분은 너네 교수님이셔. However, for 여러분 (formal "you all"), 의 is rarely dropped: 이 분은 여러분의 리더입니다. For "our," you can keep or drop 의 for 우리 or 저희: 여기는 우리 집이에요, 얘는 우리의 남동생이에요, 저기는 저희 사무실이에요.

### Slide 10 — A is NOT B
- **On screen:** Formula: 아니(not) + 이다(be) = 아니다. Sentence structure: ___은/는 ___이/가 아니다.
- **Visual info:** Example: 이 사람은 제 친구가 아니에요 (This person is not my friend). Color coding: 은/는 (yellow), 이/가 (blue), 아니다 (green).
- **Ji's teaching:** Now that we know how to say "A is B" and various pronouns, let’s learn "A is NOT B." Repeat: 이 사람은 제 친구가 아니에요. 아니다 follows the same conjugation rules as 이다 because it’s a combination of 아니 (not) and 이다. You see a new particle here: 이/가. This is also a subject particle, but it works differently from 은/는. The difference between them is notorious for being one of the most difficult concepts because they don't exist in English and context changes everything. It’s completely normal if it takes a long time to fully understand.

### Slide 11 — 은/는 & 이/가 (Usage #1)
- **On screen:** Table showing 받침 rules.
    - 사람 (ends in ㅁ 받침) + 은 / 이
    - 친구 (no 받침) + 는 / 가
- **Visual info:** Yellow highlighting: "Any Suffix with silent letter ㅇ [은 & 이] is for words with 받침." "Any Suffix with non-silent letter [는 & 가] is for words with no 받침."
- **Ji's teaching:** Before we get into the differences, remember this general rule of thumb: 은 and 는 are the same thing; 이 and 가 are the same thing. If any suffix, including particles, starts with the silent letter ㅇ (like 은 and 이), it’s for words with 받침. If it starts with a non-silent letter (like 는 and 가), it’s for words with no 받침. This applies not only to particles but also to verbs, which we'll see in our plant analogy later.

### Slide 12 — Terminology
- **On screen:** Establishing terms: 은/는 = Topic Marker; 이/가 = Subject Marker.
- **Visual info:** A large orange "X" crosses out the terms "Topic Particle" and "Subject Particle" to avoid confusion, as both can mark subjects.
- **Ji's teaching:** To avoid confusion, let’s establish clear terminology. Some call 은/는 the "topic particle" and 이/가 the "subject particle." The problem is that both can be placed after the subject. Calling 이/가 the "subject particle" is misleading. So, we’ll use the word "marker." From now on, 은/는 is the "Topic Marker" and 이/가 is the "Subject Marker."

### Slide 13 — Venn Diagram
- **On screen:** A Venn diagram. Left circle (Yellow): 은/는 Topic Marker (Any Noun, S T L O). Right circle (Blue): 이/가 Subject Marker (Only S). Overlap: Subject Particle.
- **Ji's teaching:** The first difference is that 은/는 can be used for any noun in a sentence—subject, time, location, or object. But 이/가 can only be used for the subject. Therefore, both can function as "subject particles," but they mark different ideas.

### Slide 14 — 은/는 VS 이/가 Example
- **On screen:** Two sentences:
    - 아미라는 호주 사람이에요.
    - 아미라가 호주 사람이에요.
- **Ji's teaching:** Both of these sentences mean "Amira is Australian," but the nuance and implied meaning are very different. We’ll explore why in a moment.

### Slide 15~16 — Why Topic Marker?
- **On screen:** 이 사람은 제 친구가 아니에요.
- **Visual info:** A large arrow points to the right. "ABOUT THIS PERSON" (Topic) leads to "NOT MY FRIEND" (Information). Yellow highlighting: "Similar to [As for] & [When it comes to]." Green highlighting: "places emphasis on [THE FACT THAT HE IS NOT MY FRIEND]."
- **Ji's teaching:** 은/는 marks what or who the sentence is about. In this sentence, we are talking about 이 사람 (this person). 은 marks this as the topic. This is why we use 은/는 when introducing ourselves (저는/나는). The closest English equivalent is "[As for]" or "[When it comes to]." Naturally, 은/는 places emphasis on what comes after it—the information about the topic. Here, the emphasis is on the fact that he is not my friend.

### Slide 17~18 — Why Subject Marker?
- **On screen:** 이 사람은 제 친구가 아니에요.
- **Visual info:** A large arrow points to the left. "WHO IS HE NOT?" points back to "제 친구가." Yellow highlighting: "Similar to [Be the one that]." Blue/Green highlighting: "places emphasis on [WHO, WHAT, WHICH] it is that is not my friend."
- **Ji's teaching:** Unlike 은/는, 이/가 marks the subject of the verb. Pay attention—it marks the subject of the verb, not necessarily the whole sentence. 이/가 responds to the subject of the verb 아니에요 (is not). Simply put: "Who is he not?" The answer is 제 친구 (my friend). It places emphasis on what comes before the marker—emphasizing who it is that is not your friend. The closest English equivalent is "[be the one that]."

### Slide 19 — 은/는 VS 이/가 (Questions)
- **On screen:** Comparison of questions and responses.
    - "Where is Amira from?" → 아미라는... (As for Amira...)
    - "Who is Australian?" → 아미라가... (Amira is the one who...)
- **Visual info:** Notes: 은/는 can be dropped when the topic is clear; it is required when introducing a new topic.
- **Ji's teaching:** Because they mark different things, they answer different questions. If someone asks, "Where is Amira from?", Amira is the topic. You use 는: 아미라는 호주 사람이에요. Once introduced, you can drop 아미라는 and just say 호주 사람이에요. But if someone asks "Who is Australian?", we need to emphasize who it is. We use 가: 아미라가 호주 사람이에요.

### Slide 20 — 3 main uses of 은/는
- **On screen:** 1. Stating General Facts (사과는 맛있어요). 2. Introduces a new topic. 3. Showing contrast (사과는 맛있어요 + [하지만 포도가 맛없어요]).
- **Ji's teaching:** 은/는 has three main uses. First, stating general facts like "Apples are tasty." Second, introducing a new topic. If the topic is obvious, no need to repeat it, but when it’s a new topic like "As for Tom," it is required. Third, showing contrast. Depending on context, saying 사과는 맛있어요 could imply other fruits are not as good.

### Slide 21 — 은/는 abbreviation
- **On screen:** 는 turns into ㄴ 받침 and merges with the word.
    - 나는 → 난
    - 너는 → 넌
    - 이것은 → 이거는 → 이건
- **Ji's teaching:** We often abbreviate the topic marker. 는 becomes a ㄴ 받침: 나는 becomes 난, 너는 becomes 넌, and 이것은 becomes 이건.

### Slide 22 — 이/가 abbreviation
- **On screen:** 것이 → 게.
    - 이것이 → 이게
    - 저것이 → 저게
    - 그것이 → 그게
- **Ji's teaching:** We also abbreviate 이/가. Specifically, 것이 becomes 게. Native speakers almost always use 이게, 저게, and 그게 instead of the full forms.

### Slide 23 — Remember when using 이 / 가
- **On screen:** Special forms:
    - 나 → 내 = 내가 (not 나가)
    - 저 → 제 = 제가 (not 저가)
    - 너 → 네 = 네가 (pronounced 니가)
- **Ji's teaching:** Certain pronouns change their forms when combined with 가. 나 changes to 내 (내가), 저 changes to 제 (제가), and 너 changes to 네 (pronounced 니가).

### Slide 24 — Honorifics of Subject Particles
- **On screen:** Table: 이/가 → 께서; 은/는 → 께서는 (께선).
- **Visual info:** Note: 받침 is irrelevant for these honorifics. They are not required if you use an honorific verb.
- **Ji's teaching:** Just like pronouns and verbs, some particles have honorific versions. 이/가 becomes 께서 and 은/는 becomes 께서는. 받침 doesn't matter for these. They aren't always necessary if you are already using an honorific verb, but it's good to keep them in mind.

### Slide 25~26 — I like cats
- **On screen:** Two ways to say "I like cats":
    - 나는 고양이가 좋아 (Descriptive: "As for me, cats are good")
    - 저는 고양이를 좋아해요 (Action: "I like cats")
- **Visual info:** 좋다 is a descriptive verb (adjective) and cannot have an object. 좋아하다 is an action verb and uses the object marker 을/를.
- **Ji's teaching:** Looking at your notes, you wrote 나는 고양이를 좋습니다. There are two ways to say this. First: 나는 고양이가 좋아. 좋다 is a descriptive verb meaning "to be good." 가 marks 고양이 as the subject of "being good," and 는 marks "me" as the topic. It literally means "As for me, cats are good." Descriptive verbs cannot have objects. Second: 저는 고양이를 좋아해요. 좋아하다 is an action verb, so it uses the object marker 를. This literally translates to "I like cats."

### Slide 27 — I like Somali food
- **On screen:** Comparison of 좋다 vs 좋아하다 for food.
- **Visual info:** A note explains that [서 먹어요] sounds awkward because liking food implies eating it.
- **Ji's teaching:** You wrote "I like Somali food, so I eat it" as 저는 소말리 음식을 좋아서 먹습니다. Great job on 좋아서, but if you like a food, it's obvious you like eating it. We can just say 저는 소말리 음식이 좋아요 or 저는 소말리 음식을 좋아해요.

### Slide 28 — My favorite season is Summer
- **On screen:** 내가 좋아하는 계절은 여름이야.
- **Visual info:** Note: 좋아하는 means "that/which I like." The 는 here is NOT a topic marker; it's a modifier.
- **Ji's teaching:** You wrote 제 제일 좋아하는 계절은 여름입니다. 좋아하는 actually means "that which I like," which is an advanced modifier. The 는 here is part of the verb, not a topic marker. Since it's an action verb, you need a subject for it, so you should use 제가 (the one who likes) instead of 제 (my).

### Slide 29 — S O V Object Particle
- **On screen:** 을 / 를 marks the object.
    - 을: for nouns with 받침 (제 가족을)
    - 를: for nouns without 받침 (김치를)
- **Visual info:** Note: Only Action Verbs can have objects. "Be" verbs like 이다 cannot.
- **Ji's teaching:** Next, S O V sentences. Only action verbs can have objects. The 을/를 particle marks the object. Its use is straightforward: 을 is for nouns with 받침, and 를 is for nouns without. Repeat: 저는 제 가족을 사랑해요, 저는 김치를 좋아해요.

### Slide 30~31 — 하다 – The most important verb
- **On screen:** 하다 turns words into verbs. Examples: 안녕하다, 감사하다, 말하다, 좋아하다.
- **Visual info:** Yellow highlighting: "At least 50% of Korean verbs have word + 하다 format." It works like the English suffixes -ize or -ify.
- **Ji's teaching:** About 50% of all Korean verbs follow the "Word + 하다" format. It’s arguably the most important verb. It turns words into verbs: 친절 (kindness) + 하다 = 친절하다 (to be kind). 말 (speech) + 하다 = 말하다 (to talk). 좋아하다 is a compound verb combining 좋다 (good) and 하다.

### Slide 32 — 하다 Conjugation
- **On screen:** A table of 하다 conjugations across speech levels (Informal/Formal, High/Low).
- **Visual info:** Note: "하다 is an irregular verb... the only verb with 여 suffix (Branch)."
- **Ji's teaching:** 하다 is an irregular verb. It doesn't follow conventional rules. For now, I recommend just memorizing when to use 해 and 하.

### Slide 33~38 — 하다 Conjugation (Plant Analogy)
- **On screen:** A series of diagrams using a tree/plant metaphor:
    - **Stem:** 하 (The base)
    - **Branch:** 여 (Tense) or 시 (Honorific)
    - **Leaf:** Context (Plain)
    - **Fruit:** 요 (Respect) or ㅂ니다 (Formal High)
- **Visual info:**
    - Slide 33: 하 + 여 + 요 = 해요 (Informal high). Note: Normally branches are 아 or 어, but 하다 uses 여.
    - Slide 34: 하 + 시 + 여 + 요 = 하셔요 (Informal high honorific).
    - Slide 35: 하 + 여 = 해 (Informal low).
    - Slide 37: 하 + ㅂ니다 = 합니다 (Formal high). Note: Formal High has no "Leaf" (context marker).
- **Ji's teaching:** Let's use the plant analogy. Normally, branches are 아 or 어, but for 하다, it’s 여. 하 + 여 doesn't logically produce the 해 sound, but that's just how it is. In Formal High (합니다), the branch connects directly to the fruit (ㅂ니다) without a leaf.

### Slide 39~41 — S O V sentences
- **On screen:** Example sentences with S, O, and V color-coded:
    - 나는 한국어를 공부해 (I study Korean)
    - 선생님이 문법을 설명하셔요 (The teacher explains grammar)
    - 아빠가 차를 운전하십니다 (Dad drives the car)
    - 제 여동생이 화장실을 청소해요 (My younger sister cleans the bathroom)
- **Ji's teaching:** Let's practice these S O V sentences. Notice the markers and the 하다 conjugations we just learned. Repeat them after me to get used to the flow.

### Slide 42 — Noun + 하다 verb
- **On screen:** 공부하다 = 공부를 하다.
- **Visual info:** Yellow highlighting: "You can separate Noun + 하다 verbs to O을/를 하다." This only works when the verb is an action verb and the first part is a noun.

### Slide 43 — Intro to Time and Location

## Teaching Points

- **Pronoun Etiquette:** In Korean, it is vital to distinguish between people and things. Using 것 (thing) to refer to a person is rude. Instead, use person-specific pronouns like 이 분 (this person - honorific) or 얘 (this person - casual).
- **The Plural Marker 들:** While 들 functions like the English 's', it is often omitted in Korean if the plurality is already clear from the context or the pronoun used (e.g., 너희).
- **Topic Marker (은/는) vs. Subject Marker (이/가):**
    - **은/는 (Topic):** Marks what the sentence is *about*. It sets the stage, introduces new topics, or shows contrast. It places emphasis on the information following it (the right side of the sentence).
    - **이/가 (Subject):** Marks the subject of the *verb*. It identifies "who" or "what" is performing the action or being described. It places emphasis on the subject itself (the left side of the sentence).
- **Descriptive vs. Action Verbs:**
    - **Descriptive Verbs (Adjectives):** Like 좋다 (to be good), these cannot take an object. The thing being described is marked with the subject marker 이/가.
    - **Action Verbs:** Like 좋아하다 (to like), these take objects marked with 을/를.
- **The Verb 하다:** This is a "verb-maker" that attaches to nouns to create verbs. It is irregular, using the branch 여 instead of the standard 아/어, resulting in the conjugated form 해 in informal speech.
- **Suffix Rules (받침):** Suffixes starting with a silent ㅇ (은, 이, 을) follow syllables with a final consonant (받침). Suffixes starting with a non-silent letter (는, 가, 를) follow syllables without a final consonant.
