**이/가** is also a **subject particle**, but it **works differently** from **은/는**. And the different between 은/는 and 이/가 is one of the most difficult concepts to understand because they simply don’t exist in English and context changes everything.

Before we get into the difference between 은/는 and 이/가, I first want you to know about this general rule of thumb.

은 and 는 are the same thing

이 and 가 are the same thing.

If any suffix including particles, starts with the silent letter ㅇ like 은 and 이, it’s for words with 받침 and if the suffix doesn’t start with the silent letter like 가 and 이, it’s for words with no 받침.

And what I meant by “including particles” is that we also have suffixes for verb conjugation. Remember the plant analogy? Stem, Branch, Leaf, and Fruit?

Yes, suffixes in verb endings work pretty much the same way, which we’ll learn about later. But just know that remembering this rule could go a long way.

First of all, to avoid confusion in the future, I want to establish a clear terminology for **은/는** and **이/가**.

Some people call **은/는** the **"topic particle"** and **이/가** the **"subject particle."**
The problem with calling **이/가** the **"subject particle"** is that both **은/는** and **이/가** can be placed after the subject of a sentence.
This means that calling **이/가** the "subject particle" can actually be misleading, since **both** can be subject particles.

So, instead of calling them **particles,** we’re going to use the word **"marker."**
From now on, we’ll refer to **은/는** as the **"topic marker"** and **이/가** as the **"subject marker."**

Now, the **first** difference between **은/는** and **이/가** is that **은/는** can be used for any noun in a sentence, including the subject, time, location, and object.
But **이/가** can only be used for the **subject.**.

Therefore, both 은/는 and 이/가 can be “subject particles”.

I’m going to send you some videos on **은/는 and 이/가** as an assignment.
You’ll notice that some people in those videos call them **"topic particles"** and **"subject particles,"** but again, don’t get too caught up in those names because they can be both be “subject particles” “marking” different ideas.

Now, both of these sentences 아미라는 호주 사람이에요 / 아미라가 호주 사람이에요 mean Amira is Australian.

In this sentence, we’re clearly talking about **이 사람 (this person).**

Now, let’s look at **이/가,** the **subject marker.**

Unlike **은/는,** which marks the **topic,** **이/가** marks the **subject of the verb.**
Pay attention to this—it marks the **subject of the verb, not the subject of the entire sentence.**
The word **"subject"** can be misleading, so don’t let it confuse you.

Again, who is he not? 제 친구 my friend, so 가 marking the subject of the verb 아니다

Now, let’s go back to our example sentence: **Amira is Australian.**

You would have to say: **톰은 미국 사람이에요.** — **As for Tom, he is American.**
This changes the **topic** of the sentence from Amira to Tom.

This is how **은/는** introduces a **new topic** into the conversation.
