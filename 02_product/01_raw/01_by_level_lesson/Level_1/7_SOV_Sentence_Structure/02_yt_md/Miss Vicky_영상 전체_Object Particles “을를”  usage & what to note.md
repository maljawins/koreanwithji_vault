## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor provides a comprehensive guide to the Korean object marking particles 을 and 를. The lesson begins by defining what a grammatical "object" is—the target directly affected by a verb's action—and contrasts the Korean Subject-Object-Verb (SOV) sentence structure with the English Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) structure. The core of the lesson focuses on the mechanical application of these particles based on the presence of a final consonant (받침) in the preceding noun.

Beyond basic mechanics, the instructor delves into more advanced nuances, categorizing verbs that require special attention. This includes "bestowal verbs" (수요동사) where the receiver is marked differently than the direct object, verbs that surprisingly take subject markers instead of object markers, and the behavior of passive verbs. Finally, the lesson explains how the topic marker (은/는) can replace object markers to add specific nuances like emphasis or contrast.

---

### Defining the Object and Sentence Structure
In Korean, the sentence structure follows a Subject + Object + Verb pattern. This differs from English, where the object follows the verb. An object is defined grammatically as the target on which an action is performed. For example, in the sentence "I like you," "I" is the subject, "like" is the verb, and "you" is the object. The instructor clarifies that even if the object is a person, it is still grammatically considered the "object" of the verb.

---

### Using 을 and 를
The choice between the two object marking particles depends entirely on whether the noun ends in a final consonant (받침).
- **을** is used after a noun ending in a final consonant.
- **를** is used after a noun ending in a vowel (no final consonant).

The instructor demonstrates this with several examples:
- **숙제 (homework)** ends in a vowel, so it becomes **숙제를**.
- **저녁 (dinner/evening)** ends in a consonant, so it becomes **저녁을**.
- **너 (you - casual)** ends in a vowel, so it becomes **너를**.
- **나 (me - casual)** ends in a vowel, so it becomes **나를**.
- **단어 (word)** ends in a vowel, so it becomes **단어를**.
- **영화 (movie)** ends in a vowel, so it becomes **영화를**.
- **한글 (Hangeul)** ends in a consonant, so it becomes **한글을**.
- **머리 (hair)** ends in a vowel, so it becomes **머리를**.

---

### When Object Particles Are Not Needed
Not every sentence contains an object. The instructor identifies three main scenarios where object particles are not used:
1. **The "To Be" Verb (이다):** Sentences that state "Subject equals Noun" (e.g., "I am a student") do not have an action being done to a target. These use the 이다 structure.
2. **Adjectives/Descriptive Verbs:** Adjectives describe the state of a subject (e.g., "I am smart") and do not take objects.
3. **Intransitive Contexts:** A verb might be capable of having an object, but if the object is not specified in the sentence (e.g., "I can read"), no object particle is used.

---

### Bestowal Verbs (수요동사)
Bestowal verbs imply giving or bestowing something to someone. In English, these often have a direct and indirect object. In Korean, only the item being given (the direct object) is marked with 을/를. The person receiving the item is marked with 에게 or 한테 (meaning "to").

The instructor lists seven key bestowal verbs:
- **주다 (to give)**
- **보내다 (to send)**
- **가르치다 (to teach)**
- **말하다 / 말해주다 (to tell)**
- **보여주다 (to show)**
- **사주다 (to buy someone something)**
- **가져다 주다 (to bring someone something)**

Example:
> **저는 선물을 제 친구한테 줬어요.** / I gave a gift to my friend.
(선물 is the object marked with 을; 친구 is the receiver marked with 한테).

---

### Special Cases: Calling and Summoning
The verb **전화하다 (to make a phone call)** treats the person being called as a receiver, not a direct object. Therefore, you use **한테/에게**, not **을/를**. Saying "엄마를 전화하다" would imply you are treating your mother as the physical telephone.

However, the verb **부르다** has two meanings that *do* take object markers:
1. **To call someone by a name:** The person being called is the object.
   > **저희 가족은 저를 '귀염둥이'라고 불러요.** / My family calls me a "cutie pie."
2. **To summon someone:** The person being told to come is the object.
   > **당장 지수를 불러.** / Call (summon) Jisu right now.

---

### Verbs Using Subject Markers (이/가) Instead of 을/를
Some verbs that feel like they should take an object in English actually take the subject marker 이/가 in Korean:
- **되다 (to become):** This indicates a change in the subject's status, not an action on an object.
  > **저는 최고가 될 거예요.** / I will become the best.
- **있다 / 없다 (to have / to not have):** These literally mean "to exist" or "to not exist." The thing possessed is the subject of that existence.
  > **저는 동생이 있어요.** / I have a younger sibling (Literally: As for me, a younger sibling exists).
- **좋다 / 싫다 (to be good/liked / to be bad/disliked):** These are descriptive verbs. To use object markers, one must use the active verbs **좋아하다** or **싫어하다**.
- **필요하다 (to be needed):** This is a descriptive verb meaning "to be necessary."
  > **휴식이 필요해요.** / I need a rest (Literally: Rest is needed).

---

### Passive Verbs and "To Be Good At"
Passive verbs (e.g., **취소되다** - to be canceled) are descriptive and focus on the state of the subject, so they use **이/가**. This contrasts with active verbs (e.g., **취소하다** - to cancel), which take **을/를**.

A unique case is **잘하다 (to be good at)**. While "to be good at" is descriptive in English, the Korean version literally means "to do [something] well." Since it involves "doing," the skill or activity is treated as an object.
- **한국어를 잘해요** (Good at Korean)
- **요리를 잘해요** (Good at cooking)
- **못하다** (to be bad at) also takes **을/를**.

---

### Nuance: Replacing 을/를 with 은/는
The topic marker **은/는** can replace object markers to provide specific emphasis or contrast.
1. **Emphasis/Topic:** In the sentence "As for onions, she doesn't like them," onions (the object) would be marked with **는** to highlight them as the topic.
2. **Contrast:** When comparing two things, **은/는** is used on both objects.
   > **저는 노래는 못하는데 춤은 잘 춰요.** / I am not good at singing, but I am good at dancing.
   (노래 and 춤 are objects, but marked with 는/은 to show the contrast between the two abilities).
