**Introduction to Speech level**

When you learn a new language, the very first thing you learn after the alphabet tends to be basic phrases like "hello," "bye," or "thank you".

But the way you say these phrases in Korean **change** depending on the relationship you have with the person you’re talking to. This is called "speech level". For example, the way you greet your best friend and your boss would be completely different. And this means these “basic phrases” inherently contain grammar because we use different “endings” to show different levels of respect. So, this is the very first thing we’re going to learn unlike most other languages.

**Terminology Simplified**

Before we get started, you might have noticed some inconsistency in the terms being used. Like they throw around words like "formal," "polite," and "honorifics" everywhere without really defining them clearly.

This can be confusing, so we’re going to standardize our terminology and clear things out once and for all.

Now, there are 7 speech levels in Korean. I know. It’s a lot. But the good news is, you can completely forget about 3 of them because they are “old” Korean which you can only hear in historical TV-shows called “사극”.

**High** and **Low** indicate the level of respect toward the listener. **Note that low respect level does not mean disrespectful;** it's just a classification.

In Korean, High is 존댓말, meaning respectful speech and Low is 반말, meaning half speech literally.

And I’m going to be using the Korean term “존댓말” and “반말” more often than high or low. So I advise you to get familiar with them.

**The Plain Form (Formal Low)**

And at this point, you're probably wondering about the fourth one.

Like, "How can you be formal with low respect level?"

This speech level is mainly used **when you’re speaking to someone you don’t know personally. It’s commonly used in** books**,** advertisements, essays and news articles.

And it’s also the form you’d use when you’re talking to yourself. So as far as the respect level, it sounds really “plain” hence the name “plain form”.

So, it’s not as commonly used as the other three in conversations. But that’s not to say it isn’t EVER used in speech. And when used in speech, it’s considered 반말.

We’re not going to pay too much attention about the specific usage of the plain form, but just know that this will become more relevant in \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_.

Let’s talk about the two types of 존댓말 first. The famous ~요 form and the ~니다 form.

**Polite Speech (~요 Form)**

The 요 form is called informal high because it’s not too formal but still polite like in “안녕하세요”, meaning “Hello”. THIS is the one you’ll be using most of the time. It’s how you would speak to someone you meet for the first time or to a stranger like a restaurant server or a staff in a movie theater. We’re going to call this speech level the “polite” speech.

**Formal Speech (~니다 Form)**

Next, we have the formal high speech. If you’re a K-drama fan, you’ve probably noticed a lot of sentences ending in “니다” like in 감사합니다, which means “thank you” or “니까?” for questions like in “안녕하십니까?” which is “Hello” for formal situations.

This speech level is not as commonly used as the polite speech in our day-to-day life because it’s more suited for formal situations like a business presentation or the news.

Fun fact, we call this speech level 다나까 in slang, which literally means 다 or 까 because your sentence would ALWAYS end in 다 or 까 in this form.

And this is the only speech level you’re allowed to use to your superiors in the military and God forbid you ever say 요 by accident. They WILL make your life harder than it needs to be. But this probably doesn’t concern you so, let’s call this speech level the “formal” speech and move on.

**반말 (Casual Speech)**

Lastly, we have the casual speech. Grammatically, it’s exactly the same as the 요 form but just without the 요 at the end. This is why it’s called 반말 – Half speech, like half-completed speech. And this is how you would speak to people who are close to you, like your best friend.

The keyword here is “CLOSE”. This is very important to remember. Even if someone is younger than you, that doesn’t mean you get to talk to them in 반말 straight away; especially if you’ve met them for the first time. That would be very rude.

For example, I talk to my mother, who’s obviously a lot older than me in 반말 but I would speak in 존댓말 to say, a high school student I meet for the first time, who is almost 20 years younger than me.

What we do in most cases is that we mutually agree to speak in 반말 with clear verbal consent – we have a phrase for that. “말을 놓다” which literally means “to drop speech”. Again, the key is that you have to be “close” enough to that person to use 반말.

So, to keep things nice and simple, we’re going to call this the “casual” speech.

So, there we have it. We have 2 kinds of 존댓말, the polite speech and the formal speech and 반말 the casual speech.

**Honorific & Humble Speech**

There are two more ways to show respect in Korean.

First, we have honorific speech. I have seen so many people and resources misusing the term “honorific”. And this is not only confusing but also misleading for so many learners. So, I’m going to make this very clear.

**Honorific and Speech Level are NOT the same thing.**

Whereas you use different speech levels depending on who you’re talking TO, honorific speech is the person you’re talking ABOUT.

Let’s say you’re talking to your closest friend about your grandmother. The speech level you’d be using would of course be 반말, casual speech because it’s your best friend you’re **talking TO** but you’d need to use the **honorific speech** to address your grandmother because that’s who you’re **talking ABOUT**.

In Korean, this is called 높임말, which comes from the verb 높이다 “to elevate”. So, it elevates someone you’re talking about. You learned the counter words for people 분 and 명 during the lessons on Korean Numbers. So, 분 would be the honorific of 명.

We’re going to cover in depth how to change a regular verb into an honorific verb in lesson #. But you might have heard many Korean sentence ending in 세요 like in 안녕하세요.

If you see a syllable that has a ‘ㅅ’ after the verb stem, it’s most likely an honorific verb.

We also have what’s called humble speech. While honorific speech is used to elevate someone, humble speech is about lowering yourself. It’s called 낮춤말 in Korean, coming from 낮추다 meaning to lower.

For example, there’s two ways to say “I” in Korean, 나 and the humble version 저.

And there are only a handful of words you need to know for humble speech, as they're mainly used in interactive actions like giving, asking, telling, or meeting. We’re going to learn more honorifics and humble speech in lesson 13 and 14.

**Summary**

To sum up: There are three ways to show respect in Korean—Speech level, honorific speech, and humble speech.

And as far as the speech level, there are four speech levels used in modern Korean: Informal High & Low, and Formal High & Low.

But of those four, three are most commonly used in actual conversations:

We have two kinds of 존댓말: The polite speech – or the 요 form and the formal speech – the 니다 form.

And we have the casual speech, or 반말, which is just the 요 form without the 요 at the end.
