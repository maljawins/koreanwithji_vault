## Teaching Points

### Overview
This lesson introduces formal speech in Korean, a level of politeness distinct from the honorific and humble speech previously covered. While honorific speech is used to show respect toward others and humble speech is used when referring to oneself in polite contexts, formal speech functions as a way to speak as an official. It carries a tone of high respect and professionalism, making it common in public, military, and specific social settings.

The instructor explains that formal speech is not intended for use with friends or casual acquaintances. Instead, learners will encounter it in announcements, speeches, formal letters, and high-stakes social introductions, such as meeting a new boss or future parents-in-law. The lesson provides a comprehensive guide to conjugating verbs into formal present, past, and future tenses, including the specific rules for questions and irregular stems.

---

### When to Use Formal Speech
Formal speech is reserved for specific "official" contexts. The instructor lists several key scenarios on screen: making official announcements, giving public speeches, writing formal letters, and meeting high-status individuals like a teacher or a new boss for the first time. It is also the exclusive form of speech used within the Korean army.

Beyond these situational uses, certain set phrases in Korean are more commonly expressed in formal speech than in other levels. Two prominent examples shown are:
- **잘 먹겠습니다.** / Thank you for the food (said before a meal in non-casual settings).
- **생일 축하합니다.** / Happy birthday.

---

### Conjugating Formal Sentences (Present Tense)
To turn a verb into a formal statement (not a question), the instructor introduces the **니다** form. The conjugation depends on whether the verb stem ends in a consonant or a vowel. If the verb stem ends in a consonant, you attach **씁니다**. If the verb stem ends in a vowel, you attach a **ㅂ** (as a batchim) followed by **니다**.

The instructor provides the following examples:
- **하다 (to do)** → **합니다**
- **먹다 (to eat)** → **먹습니다**

A special rule applies to verb stems ending in the consonant **ㄹ**. In these cases, the **ㄹ** is removed before attaching the **ㅂ니다** ending. For example, the verb **살다 (to live)** becomes **삽니다**.

---

### Formal Questions
When asking a question in formal speech, the ending changes from **니다** to **니까**. The conjugation rules remain the same: attach **씁니까** to stems ending in a consonant and **ㅂ니까** to stems ending in a vowel.

Examples shown on screen:
- **하다** → **합니까?**
- **먹다** → **먹습니까?**
- **살다** → **삽니까?** (following the **ㄹ** drop rule)

---

### Past and Future Tense in Formal Speech
To form the past tense, the instructor explains that you first take the regular past tense verb stem and then attach **씁니다** for statements or **씁니까** for questions.
- **했습니다** / I did it.
- **하셨습니까?** / Did you do it? (using the honorific infix **시**)

For the future tense, one method is to conjugate the **것이다** form. This results in **것입니다** (or the contracted **겁니다**) for statements, and **것입니까** (or **겁니까**) for questions.
- **저는 갈 겁니다.** / I will go.
- **하실 겁니까?** / Will you go?

Another common way to express the future tense in formal speech is using **겠다**. This conjugates to **겠습니다** or **겠습니까**. While **겠다** sounds much more polite and official, the instructor notes that in regular informal conversations, the **것이다** form is more frequent.

---

### Practical Examples and Nuance
The instructor concludes with a series of example sentences to demonstrate formal speech in context. A notable distinction is made between **알겠습니다** and **알다**. While **알다** simply means "to know," **알겠습니다** is used to signal that you have understood a piece of information and will act upon it.

Example sentences provided:
- **네, 사장님. 알겠습니다.** / Yes, boss. I understand.
- **저는 미국에서 왔습니다.** / I am from America.
- **만나서 반갑습니다.** / Nice to meet you.
- **아주 좋은 생각입니다. 감사합니다.** / It is a very good idea. Thank you.
- **선생님, 피곤하십니까?** / Teacher, are you tired?
- **어디 사십니까?** / Where do you live?
