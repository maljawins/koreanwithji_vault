## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces the concept of honorific speech, known in Korean as 높임말. This term is derived from the word 높다 (to be high), specifically the causative form 높이다 (to make something high), signifying words that have been elevated to show respect. The core function of honorific speech is to figuratively elevate the person being discussed. A crucial distinction is made: honorifics are about the person you are talking *about*, whereas speech levels like casual or formal speech are about the person you are talking *to*.

The learning arc begins with the conceptual framework of honorifics, moves into the grammatical mechanics of verb endings, addresses unique irregular verbs, and concludes with honorific nouns and particles. The instructor emphasizes that honorifics can be mixed with any speech level—casual, formal, or plain—depending on the relationship with the listener versus the respect owed to the subject of the sentence.

---

### The Subject of Respect

Honorific speech is used to show extra respect toward the person someone is talking about. This person could be the listener, but it does not have to be; it can be anyone, whether they are present or not. For example, if speaking to a friend (using casual speech) about a grandmother, it would be considered rude not to use honorifics for the grandmother. Therefore, one might use casual endings for the friend but honorific verb forms for the grandmother's actions.

There are specific limits to who receives honorifics. They are reserved strictly for people, never animals or inanimate objects. Using honorifics toward a boss's dog, for instance, would be socially awkward as it places the dog on the same level as the boss. Similarly, honorifics are generally not used for children or regular objects. The instructor notes that while there are rare exceptions for objects in specific commercial contexts, the general rule is "people only." Typical recipients of honorific speech include teachers, mentors, bosses, higher-ranking individuals, strangers, grandparents, and often one's own parents depending on the family dynamic.

---

### Basic Honorific Verb Endings: (으)시다

The most fundamental way to create an honorific verb is by attaching the suffix (으)시다 to the verb stem. If the verb stem ends in a consonant, 으시다 is added; if it ends in a vowel, 시다 is added. The instructor demonstrates this with 읽다 (to read) becoming 읽으시다 and 하다 (to do) becoming 하시다.

There are specific rules for stems ending in certain consonants. For stems ending in ㄹ, such as 살다 (to live), the ㄹ is removed before adding 시다, resulting in 사시다. For stems ending in ㅂ that undergo the ㅂ-irregular change, such as 덥다 (to be hot), the ㅂ is replaced with 우 before adding 시다, resulting in 더우시다. Once these honorific stems are formed, they can be conjugated into any tense or speech level.

---

### Conjugating Honorific Verbs

While honorific verbs conjugate like normal verbs in most tenses, the present tense 요 form (polite level) has a unique but simple pattern. Instead of the expected 시어요, it almost always becomes 세요. For example, 하시다 becomes 하세요 and 읽으시다 becomes 읽으세요. This 세요 ending is the standard way to use honorifics in the present polite style.

In other tenses, the conjugation follows standard rules. For the past tense, 하시다 becomes 하셨어요 (시 + 었 + 어요). For the future tense, it becomes 하실 거예요 (시 + ㄹ 거예요). The instructor provides example sentences to illustrate these:
> **피자를 좋아하세요?** / Do you like pizza?
> **할머니도 피자를 좋아하세요?** / Does grandmother also like pizza?
> **준비하셨어요?** / Did you get ready? / Are you prepared?

---

### Unique Honorific Verbs

Certain common verbs have unique honorific versions that must be used instead of the (으)시다 suffix. Using the regular suffix with these verbs sounds awkward or incorrect to native speakers. The instructor lists the most essential pairs:
- **먹다 (to eat)** becomes **드시다**
- **자다 (to sleep)** becomes **주무시다**
- **죽다 (to die)** becomes **돌아가시다 (to pass away)**
- **있다 (to exist/be at a place)** becomes **계시다** (when referring to a person's presence)

A special note is made regarding 있다. While 계시다 is used for a person's location (e.g., "Grandmother is at home"), the form 있으시다 is used when talking about things a respected person possesses, such as their time, a bag, or body parts.
> **할머니는 지금 집에 계세요.** / Grandmother is at home right now.
> **지금 혹시 시간이 있으세요?** / Do you happen to have time right now?

---

### Honorific Nouns and Counter Words

Respect is also expressed through specific nouns and counters when referring to a respected person's attributes or presence. These words should never be used to refer to oneself. The instructor highlights the following transitions:
- **집 (house)** becomes **댁**
- **사람 (person)** becomes **분** (also used as a counter: 한 분, 두 분)
- **이름 (name)** becomes **성함**
- **나이 (age)** becomes **연세**
- **생일 (birthday)** becomes **생신**

In a restaurant setting, staff will often ask **몇 분이세요? (How many people?)**, to which the customer should respond using the regular counter 명 (e.g., **두 명이요**) because one does not use honorifics for oneself. Another example provided is **오늘 할머니 생신이에요 (Today is grandmother's birthday)**.

---

### Honorific Particles

Finally, the instructor introduces honorific versions of common particles. While these are not as strictly required as the verb forms, they add an extra layer of respect.
- **은/는 (topic marker)** can become **께서** or **께서는**
- **이/가 (subject marker)** becomes **께서**
- **에게/한테 (to a person)** becomes **께**

The instructor demonstrates these in complete sentences:
> **사장님께서는 일찍 나가셨어요.** / The boss left early.
> **선생님께서 똑똑하세요.** / The teacher is smart.

While one can "get away" with using regular particles like 할머니는, using 께서 and 께서는 is recommended when the speaker wishes to be particularly respectful. Like all honorifics, these are only used for people, never for objects like pizza.
