## Teaching Points

### Overview
This lesson focuses on the importance and usage of formal speech in Korean, specifically the `입니다` and `~습니다` endings. The instructor addresses a common misconception among learners that formal speech is only for giving speeches or official presentations. Instead, he demonstrates that formal speech is a vital part of daily life and social etiquette in Korea, used to show respect and maintain appropriate social distance in various professional and personal settings.

The video covers the grammatical construction of formal endings, the distinction between the humble first-person pronoun `저` and the plain pronoun `나`, and the specific social contexts where formal speech is required. By the end of the lesson, learners should understand not just how to conjugate verbs into formal forms, but also why matching the correct pronoun with the appropriate verb ending is essential for natural-sounding Korean.

---

### What is 입니다?
The word `입니다` is the formal conjugation of the verb `이다 (to be)`. While many learners are first introduced to the polite (해요체) forms `이에요` (used after a consonant) and `예요` (used after a vowel), `입니다` serves as the formal equivalent regardless of whether the preceding noun ends in a consonant or a vowel. It is used in official capacities or when the speaker wants to sound extra polite.

---

### When to Use Formal Speech
Formal speech is not limited to public speaking. The instructor outlines several key situations where it is the expected standard. First, it is used for any official statement or announcement to a group, such as a project leader addressing a team. Second, many common set phrases are inherently formal, including `감사합니다 (thank you)`, `실례합니다 (excuse me)`, `잘 먹겠습니다 (I will eat well)`, and `잘 먹었습니다 (I have eaten well)`.

Socially, formal speech is crucial for making good first impressions. It is highly recommended when meeting a partner's parents for the first time, as being overly formal is safer and more impressive than being too casual. Similarly, it is used when meeting a boss or a teacher for the first time. Finally, formal speech is the mandatory standard in the Korean military, where every sentence must end with these direct, formal endings.

---

### Conjugating Formal Endings
The process for making a verb or adjective formal is straightforward. For statements, you take the verb stem and attach `습니다` if the stem ends in a consonant, or `ㅂ니다` if the stem ends in a vowel. For example, `하다 (to do)` becomes `합니다`, and `먹다 (to eat)` becomes `먹습니다`. To turn these into questions, the ending changes from `~니다` to `~니까?`, resulting in forms like `합니까?` or `먹습니까?`.

There is a specific rule for verb stems ending in the consonant `ㄹ`. In these cases, the `ㄹ` is removed before attaching the formal ending. For instance, the verb `살다 (to live)` drops the `ㄹ` and adds `ㅂ니다` to become `삽니다`. This formal ending can also be applied to other tenses, such as the future tense `~겠습니다`.

---

### Matching Pronouns: 저 vs 나
A critical part of using formal speech correctly is choosing the right word for "I" or "me." Korean has two main options: `저` (humble) and `나` (plain). `저` is used to lower oneself relative to the listener, making it the appropriate choice for polite and formal situations. `나` does not carry this humble nuance and is used when speaking to equals or subordinates in casual settings.

The instructor emphasizes that you must match the pronoun level with the verb ending level. Using `저는` with the formal ending `입니다` is correct because both are polite/humble. However, mixing them creates a mismatch. `나는 빌리입니다` sounds awkward because `나` is not humble while `입니다` is very formal. Similarly, `저는 빌리야` is incorrect because `저` is humble but the ending `~이야` is casual (반말).

---

### Exceptions and Nuances
While the general rule is to avoid mixing `나` with formal endings, there are rare exceptions where a speaker might use `나는` with `~습니다`. This occurs when the speaker is in a formal situation but does not need to lower themselves to the audience. An example would be the President of Korea giving an official speech to citizens; the President wants to sound formal and official but does not necessarily need to use humble speech toward the general public.

Another example is the Korean Pledge of Allegiance, which begins with `나는` but uses formal verb endings throughout. This is because the pledge is a formal declaration rather than a conversation directed at a specific person. Despite these exceptions, the instructor advises learners to stick to the standard pairing of `저` with formal/polite endings and `나` with casual endings to avoid sounding awkward or rude.
