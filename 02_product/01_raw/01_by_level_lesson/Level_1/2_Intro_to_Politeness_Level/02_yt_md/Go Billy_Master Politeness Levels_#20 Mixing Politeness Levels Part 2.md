### Overview
This lesson continues the exploration of mixing politeness levels in Korean, moving beyond the concept of 반존대 (mixing casual and polite speech) covered previously. The instructor emphasizes that mastering politeness is less about memorizing rigid rules and more about understanding the "feel" of the speech. A core principle provided is that it is always better to sound overly polite and slightly awkward than to be too casual and risk being rude.

The instructor covers specific nuances such as using the neutral pronoun 나 (I/me) with formal endings, how to navigate situations where age and social rank conflict, and the pragmatic reasons why speakers might switch between formal (하십시오체) and polite (해요체) styles within a single conversation or email. The goal is to provide learners with the tools to navigate common but complex social interactions in Korea.

---

### Mixing Formal and Casual Elements
While formal speech (하십시오체) generally feels strong and respectful and casual speech (반말) feels friendly and intimate, they are usually not mixed. An example of an incorrect mixture shown on screen is 제가 해 드릴 거야, which sounds awkward and grammatically mismatched. However, there are specific instances where certain words typically associated with casual speech appear in formal contexts.

The pronoun 나 (I/me) is a neutral noun, whereas 저 is a humble noun that lowers the speaker. While 나 is standard for casual speech, it is sometimes used with formal endings, such as in the sentence 나는 빌리입니다 (I am Billy). This combination signals two things: first, the speaker does not feel the need to lower themselves to the listener (perhaps due to higher status); second, the speaker recognizes the occasion as formal. This is common for company leaders or high-ranking officials like the President of Korea when giving public announcements.

---

### The Use of 나 with the 요 Form
Learners may also encounter native speakers using 나 with the polite 요 form, such as 나는 빌리예요 (I am Billy). This is often a marker of age dynamics. An older person might use 나 to indicate they are senior to the listener, but use the 요 form to maintain a baseline of politeness and avoid sounding rude. The instructor notes that older and younger people often choose different vocabulary to reflect this; for example, a younger person says 밥을 먹다 (to eat a meal) while an older person might say 식사를 하다. Similarly, a younger person says 아니에요 (you're welcome), while an older person might use the more traditional 천만해요.

---

### When Age and Rank Conflict
A common dilemma occurs when a speaker's social rank is higher than someone who is chronologically older—for example, a young supervisor managing an older team. In such cases, the supervisor should speak politely (using the 요 form or formal speech) because of the team members' age. Simultaneously, the older team members should speak politely to the supervisor because of their professional rank. The result is a mutual exchange of polite speech. While speech levels might lower as the parties become closer, this should never be assumed; the default remains mutual politeness.

---

### Switching Between Formal and Polite Styles
In media, advertisements, or radio shows, speakers often switch between formal 하십시오체 and the polite 요 form. Using only formal speech can feel overly stiff and "cold." By mixing in the 요 form, the speaker sounds more approachable and friendly while still maintaining high respect. 

The instructor provides a visual example of a professional email to demonstrate this:
> **김 선생님, 안녕하세요.** / Hello, Teacher Kim.
> **지난주에 보내주신 자료 잘 받았습니다.** / I received the materials you sent last week.
> **오늘 확인해 보니 중간에 잘못된 정보가 약간 있더라고요.** / Checking it today, there was a little bit of wrong information in the middle.
> **표시해서 보내드리니 수정 부탁드릴게요.** / I'm marking it and sending it, so please correct it.
> **천천히 보내 주셔도 됩니다.** / You can send it back slowly (take your time).
> **좋은 주말 보내세요.** / Have a nice weekend.

In this email, the speaker uses formal endings (받았습니다, 됩니다) for factual statements but switches to the 요 form (있더라고요, 부탁드릴게요, 보내세요) to soften the tone and sound friendlier. This is appropriate for general professional correspondence but would not be used in extremely rigid environments like the military or official government decrees.

---

### Closeness as a Guide
When deciding whether to use formal speech or the 요 form, the speaker should consider the level of closeness. A former teacher might be addressed with the 요 form because of an existing bond, whereas a new boss or a public audience usually requires the distance and respect of formal speech. This also explains why celebrities who are close friends in private will speak very politely to each other on camera; they do this to avoid appearing disrespectful to viewers who are unaware of their personal friendship.
