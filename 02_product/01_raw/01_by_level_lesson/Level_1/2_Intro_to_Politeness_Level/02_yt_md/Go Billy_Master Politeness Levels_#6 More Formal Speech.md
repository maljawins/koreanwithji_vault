### Formal Future Tense: Two Variations
The instructor introduces two primary ways to express the future tense in formal speech. The first method involves taking a verb stem and attaching ㄹ 겁니다 (if the stem ends in a vowel) or 을 겁니다 (if it ends in a consonant). Special rules apply for specific stems: if a stem ends in ㄹ, that ㄹ is removed before attaching the ending; if it ends in ㅂ, the ㅂ is replaced with 우 before attaching the ending. For example, 가다 (to go) becomes 갈 겁니다, 먹다 (to eat) becomes 먹을 겁니다, and 살다 (to live) becomes 살 겁니다. This form is derived from 것 (thing) and 이다 (to be), so it can also appear as the more literal 할 것입니다. In question form, the ending changes to 겁니까 or 것입니까.

The second future tense form uses the 겠다 suffix. To conjugate this, one simply attaches 겠습니다 to the verb stem regardless of whether it ends in a vowel or a consonant. Examples include 가겠습니다, 먹겠습니다, and 살겠습니다. When comparing the two, the 겠다 form sounds slightly more polite and official than the 것이다 form. While 겠다 can also indicate a descriptive "must be" or "would be" in other contexts, in the future tense it is almost always conjugated as 겠습니다 or the question form 겠습니까.

### Formal Past Tense Conjugation
Conjugating formal speech into the past tense requires taking the already conjugated past tense base of a verb and attaching 습니다. The instructor reminds students that different verbs have different conjugation rules for their bases. For instance, 하다 (to do) conjugates to the base 해, which becomes 했습니다 in formal past tense. Other examples shown include 가다 becoming 갔습니다, 먹다 becoming 먹었습니다, and 살다 becoming 살았습니다. To turn these into questions, the 니다 ending is replaced with 니까, resulting in forms like 했습니까.

### Social Contexts and Pragmatic Usage
The instructor emphasizes that while formal speech is respectful, using it with friends or close acquaintances in regular conversation is usually awkward or overly fancy. It can even be used humorously among friends to sound mock-officially. However, there are several specific scenarios where formal speech is the standard. First, it is used for official announcements and public speeches. Second, it is appropriate when introducing oneself for the first time in professional or academic settings, such as a business meeting or a new classroom.

Formal speech is also the standard for official written communication. This includes company announcements or letters sent from a business to its customers, as businesses treat customers with high respect. However, personal letters to friends should match the writer's spoken style. A helpful tip provided is to consider how one would speak to the recipient in person when deciding which level to use in writing. Additionally, formal speech is recommended when meeting someone you wish to impress, such as a new boss, a teacher, or parents-in-law for the first time. As the relationship becomes closer, the speaker may transition to the more common 요 form.

### Fixed Formal Phrases and Military Speech
Certain expressions in Korean are effectively "set phrases" that almost always utilize formal speech, even if the rest of the conversation is in a different style. These include 감사합니다 (Thank you), 죄송합니다 (I'm sorry), and 생일 축하합니다 (Happy birthday). The instructor notes that even if one is close enough to a boss to use the 요 form, saying 잘 먹었어요 (I ate well) after a meal might sound impolite compared to the formal 잘 먹겠습니다 (before the meal) or 잘 먹었습니다 (after the meal).

A unique application of formal speech occurs in the Korean military, where members are required to use only the 니다 and 니까 endings, avoiding the 요 form entirely. This style can be combined with honorifics, such as 하십니다. While superiors may use casual speech (specifically 해라채) toward lower ranks, subordinates must remain formal. The instructor mentions a famous military-specific ending, 지 말입니다, which is used to "bend" the rules of strict formal endings while maintaining the required 니다 suffix. While well-known due to pop culture, it is often considered inappropriate in actual military practice.
