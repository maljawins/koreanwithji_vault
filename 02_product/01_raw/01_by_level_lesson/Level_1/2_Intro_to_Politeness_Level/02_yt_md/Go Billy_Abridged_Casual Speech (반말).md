## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces the concept of 반말, which literally translates to "half-speech" but is understood as casual speech. Unlike honorific or humble speech, which focuses on the status of the person being discussed, casual speech is defined entirely by the relationship with the person being addressed. It is a level of speech that does not include extra markers of respect or politeness. While it can be perceived as rude if used in the wrong context, it carries a friendly, "no formalities" vibe when used appropriately among peers or close associates.

The lesson covers the social rules of when to use casual speech, the mechanical process of converting polite sentences into casual ones, and specific vocabulary changes required to maintain the correct level. The instructor emphasizes that casual speech is not just about removing endings; it involves a shift in pronouns, particles, and even the way names are addressed.

---

### When to Use Casual Speech (반말)

Casual speech is appropriate for people with whom you have a close relationship and where no formal respect is required. This primarily includes close friends who are the same age or younger, as well as romantic partners like boyfriends, girlfriends, or spouses. However, the instructor notes that age still plays a significant role; even with friends, if one person is older, the younger person might still use polite speech while the older person speaks casually.

Family members are another common group for casual speech, including parents, though this varies by individual family dynamics. Additionally, casual speech is used when talking to animals, small children (though some people choose to remain polite), and most importantly, when talking to yourself. The instructor points out that you never show extra respect to yourself in your own thoughts or when looking in the mirror. Finally, you can use casual speech with anyone who has explicitly given you permission to do so, regardless of age or rank, though it is often best to keep this "secret arrangement" private and revert to polite speech in public to avoid looking disrespectful to others.

---

### How to Form Casual Speech

The most common way to create casual speech is to start with the standard polite 요 form and simply remove the 요. For example, the polite question 어디 가요? (Where are you going?) becomes the casual 어디 가?. Similarly, the past tense 밥 먹었어요? (Did you eat?) becomes 밥 먹었어?.

There are important exceptions to this "remove 요" rule. When using honorifics (으시다), which are used to show respect to the subject of the sentence, you do not use the 으세요 ending in casual speech. Instead, you conjugate the honorific stem directly into the casual form. For instance, if talking to a friend about a grandmother who is not home, you would say 할머니가 지금 집에 안 계셔. This sentence is casual toward the listener (the friend) but remains honorific toward the subject (the grandmother).

For the copula 이다 (to be), the casual ending is 야 after a vowel and 이야 after a consonant. For example, 우리 집이야 (It's my house) or 할 거야 (I will do it). The negative form 아니다 (to not be) always becomes 아니야 regardless of what precedes it, such as 우리 집이 아니야 (It's not my house).

---

### Essential Vocabulary Changes

Switching to casual speech requires changing specific words that carry inherent politeness levels. The humble first-person pronoun 저 (I/me) must be replaced with the casual 나. Using 저 with casual endings is grammatically inconsistent. Similarly, the polite words for "yes," 네 or 예, are replaced by the casual 응 or 어. For "no," the word 아니 is used as a simple exclamation, which is distinct from the conjugated verb 아니야.

The word 너 (you) is the casual equivalent of "you," but the instructor warns that it should be used sparingly. It can sound like you are talking down to someone or putting them below you on the social ladder. It is acceptable with very close friends of the same or younger age, but it should never be used with someone older, even if they have given you permission to speak casually. When used with the subject marker 가, 너 becomes 네가, which is often pronounced as 니가 in speech to distinguish it from 내가 (I).

---

### Addressing Friends and Others

When talking about a friend in a casual context, you attach 이 to their name if it ends in a consonant. For example, 정국 becomes 정국이 and 진영 becomes 진영이. If the name ends in a vowel, like 유라, it is used as is. For older friends, you use kinship terms like 형 (older brother to a male), 오빠 (older brother to a female), 누나 (older sister to a male), or 언니 (older sister to a female). You can also combine their name with the title, such as 찰수 형.

When calling out to someone to get their attention, you attach 아 to names ending in a consonant (정국아!) and 야 to names ending in a vowel (찰수야!).

---

### Casual-Only Grammar Forms

Certain grammar structures exist only within the casual speech level. The instructor highlights three common forms:

1. **Suggestions (~자):** Attach 자 to an action verb stem to mean "let's." For example, 가다 (to go) becomes 가자 (let's go).
2. **Friendly Commands (~라):** Attach 라 to a conjugated action verb for a command that feels strong yet friendly. For example, 이거 먹어봐라 (Try this/Eat this).
3. **Strong Questions (~냐 / ~니):** Attach 냐 to a verb stem for a strong, direct question, like 어디 가냐? (Where are you going?). The ending 니 is used similarly but feels slightly softer and less aggressive than 냐.
