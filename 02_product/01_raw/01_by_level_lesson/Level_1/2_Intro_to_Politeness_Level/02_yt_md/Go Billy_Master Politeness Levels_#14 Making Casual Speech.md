## Teaching Points

### Overview

This lesson focuses on the mechanics of casual speech (반말) in Korean, moving beyond simple verb endings to cover pronouns, affirmative/negative responses, and specific grammar forms used only in informal contexts. The instructor emphasizes that casual speech is not just about dropping the polite suffix 요; it requires a shift in vocabulary and sentence structure to remain natural. The learning arc covers the transition from humble to neutral pronouns, casual ways to say "yes" and "no," the "let's" suggestion form, and various ways to formulate commands and questions. Finally, the lesson addresses the common practice of dropping grammatical markers in spoken Korean to sound more colloquial.

---

### Saying "Me" Casually

The instructor explains that the word **저 (I/me)** is a humble noun used to lower the speaker relative to the listener. Consequently, **저** should never be used in casual speech. Instead, speakers must use the neutral pronoun **나**. This pronoun changes based on the particle attached: it becomes **나는** with the topic marker and **내가** with the subject marker. Unlike **저**, **나** does not lower the speaker but maintains a neutral status appropriate for friends or those of a lower rank.

---

### Casual Yes and No

Affirmative and negative responses change significantly in casual contexts. Instead of the polite **네** or **예 (yes)**, casual speech uses **응**. The instructor notes that you may also frequently hear **어** used as an alternative to **응**. To say "no," the polite **아니요** is shortened to **아니**. It is important to distinguish **아니** (meaning "no") from **아니야**, which is the casual conjugation of the verb **아니다 (to not be)**.

---

### Casual Grammar: "Let's" with 자

Certain grammar forms are exclusive to casual speech. The form **자** is used to mean "let's" and is attached directly to an action verb stem. Examples provided include **먹자 (let's eat)**, **공부하자 (let's study)**, and **가자 (let's go)**. The instructor warns that this ending should never be combined with honorific or humble speech; for instance, one should not say **드시자** or **가시자**. This **자** ending is technically categorized as part of the **해라체** (plain style) speech level.

---

### Casual Commands and Suggestions

Commands can be formed simply by using the casual conjugation of a verb (the **요** form without the **요**). For example, **하다 (to do)** becomes **해**. However, the instructor highlights that direct commands can often sound impolite even in casual speech. It is frequently better to suggest an action rather than command it, especially when there is any ambiguity in rank. A comparison is shown between a direct command and a suggestion:

> **영화를 보러 가세요.** / Go watch a movie.
> **영화를 보러 가시는 게 어떨까요?** / How about going to see a movie?

For a stronger command among very close friends, the **라** ending can be attached to a conjugated verb, such as **해라 (do it!)** or **먹어라 (eat it!)**. This is distinct from the **(으)라** ending used in indirect quotes (e.g., **으라고**).

---

### Casual Questions: 니 and 냐

While questions in casual speech can be made by simply using a rising intonation with the standard casual ending (e.g., **해?**), there are specific question endings: **니** and **냐**. These are attached to the verb stem. **니** has a slightly softer feel, while **냐** feels stronger. Examples include **하니? / 하냐?** and **먹니? / 먹냐?**. Like the **라** command ending, these are part of **해라체** and should only be used with very close friends as they sound much stronger than the regular conjugated verb.

---

### Removing Markers in Spoken Korean

In natural spoken Korean, regardless of the politeness level, it is common to remove grammatical markers like **은/는**, **이/가**, and **을/를**. This makes the speech sound more colloquial. However, removing every single marker can make the speech sound like slang. The instructor provides five variations of the same sentence to show the spectrum of "slanginess":

1. **나는 배가 고픈데 같이 밥을 먹으러 갈래?** (Full markers)
2. **나는 배고픈데 같이 밥을 먹으러 갈래?**
3. **나는 배고픈데 같이 밥 먹으러 갈래?**
4. **난 배고픈데 같이 밥 먹으러 갈래?**
5. **나 배고픈데 같이 밥 먹으러 갈래?** (Most colloquial)

The rule of thumb is that markers should only be removed if the meaning remains clear; if removing a marker makes the sentence vague, it must be kept.
