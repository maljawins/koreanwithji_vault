The instructor provides a comprehensive summary of Korean politeness levels, focusing on the "feel" and social nuance of each style rather than just the grammatical mechanics. The lesson is framed around a humorous social media question: how would one speak to a criminal about to attack? This serves as a springboard to explain that politeness levels are not just about rules, but about the amount of respect and distance one chooses to project toward a listener or a subject.

The lesson categorizes speech into three primary styles based on the listener (Formal, 요-form, and Casual) and two additional categories based on the subject being discussed (Honorific and Humble). Finally, it introduces the "Plain Form" used primarily in writing. The instructor emphasizes that these categories are not mutually exclusive; for instance, one can use honorifics while speaking in a casual style.

---

### Formal Speech (하십시오체)

Formal speech is characterized by endings like `~니다` and `~니까`. For example, the verb `하다 (to do)` becomes `합니다`, and `먹다 (to eat)` becomes `먹습니다`. This style is the standard for official announcements, public speeches, formal letters, and the military. It is also the safest choice when meeting authority figures for the first time, such as a new boss, teacher, or parents-in-law.

The instructor notes a common misconception that this form is only for "giving speeches." In reality, it is used in regular conversation to convey a strong, respectful feel toward the listener. It establishes a clear professional or social distance that signals high levels of respect.

---

### The 요 Form (해요체)

The `요` form is the most common politeness level in daily life. It includes any conjugation ending in `요`, as well as the polite "no," `아니요`. While it is generally "polite," its specific nuance is soft and polite rather than the "strong and respectful" feel of formal speech.

This style can be adjusted for even more softness or politeness. Adding endings like `~나요?` or `~가요?` when asking questions makes the sentence sound extra gentle. Because it is versatile, it is often the default for strangers or acquaintances where one wants to be polite without being overly stiff.

---

### Casual Speech (반말)

Casual speech, or 반말, is used when the `요` ending is dropped. For example, `했어요 (I did)` becomes `했어`, and `아니에요 (It is not)` becomes `아니야`. This style does not show any extra respect toward the listener.

The instructor clarifies that casual speech is not inherently "rude." Instead, when used correctly with close friends, siblings, or those younger than the speaker, it carries a friendly feel. However, using it with someone who requires respect—like a stranger or an elder—would be perceived as disrespectful because the speaker is failing to provide the expected social "extra respect."

---

### Honorific and Humble Speech

Unlike the previous three levels which focus on the listener, Honorific and Humble speech focus on the person being talked about.

Honorific speech typically involves the `~(으)시다` ending, seen in forms like `하세요`. The instructor uses the visual metaphor of putting someone on a pedestal. By using honorifics, you are "raising up" the subject of the sentence because you look up to them or they deserve extra respect. This can be used even when talking to a close friend (in casual speech) if the person you are talking about is someone like a grandparent or a teacher.

Humble speech functions as the opposite side of the same coin. It involves specific verbs like `드리다 (to give - humble)` or `뵙다 (to see/meet - humble)`. Instead of raising the other person up, you are "lowering yourself" or "bowing down" in your speech. This effectively achieves the same result of looking up to the other person. Humble speech is generally used when the person receiving respect is the object of the verb (e.g., "I gave a gift to the teacher").

---

### Plain Form (해라체)

The Plain Form is identified by verbs ending in `~다` in their dictionary-like conjugated state, such as `한다`, `먹는다`, or `춥다`. This form is unique because it is generally not directed at a specific audience. It is the standard for newspapers, journals, books, and general writing.

In spoken Korean, the Plain Form is used for "self-talk" or making general announcements to no one in particular. It can also be used among friends to get someone's attention or announce a fact casually. Unlike casual speech, it feels "plain" rather than "friendly."

---

### Summary of Nuance and the "Criminal" Scenario

The instructor summarizes the "feel" of each level:
- **Formal:** Strong and respectful.
- **요 Form:** Soft and polite.
- **Casual:** Friendly (if appropriate) or disrespectful (if not).
- **Plain:** Neutral, non-directed, or for writing.
- **Honorifics/Humble:** Raising others or lowering oneself to show subject-based respect.

Returning to the opening question about speaking to a criminal: the instructor suggests using the `요` form combined with honorifics. Using casual speech might provoke the attacker by being rude, while formal speech might be too stiff. The `요` form with honorifics provides a "soft polite" tone that acknowledges the situation while maintaining a level of respect that might keep the speaker safe.
