**Intro**

Welcome. Let’s get straight into it and learn how to say **“can” and “can’t”**. There are many ways you can say this in Korean. And we’re going to look at 3 of them in this lesson.

Are there more than 3? Yes! But we’re going to learn the most basic ones first here then learn more in level 4.

The first form is V**erb Stem + ㄹ/을 수 있다 or 없다**.

**First off — what does 수 mean?**

**수** is actually a noun, and it means **“ability,” “way,” “means,”** or **“possibility.”**
So when you say **수 있다**, you’re literally saying: 👉 “There is a way (to do it)” or I have the ability to do it.

And when you say **수 없다**, you’re saying: 👉 “There IS no way”.

So, it could be translated as [be able to] / [not be able to]

**✅ 수 있다 – Can / Be able to**

Let’s take a look at some examples:

📍 **저는 노래를 할 수 있어요.** → I can sing. [I have the ability to sing]

📍 **제 친구는 한국 요리를 할 수 있어요.** → My friend can cook Korean food. *She has the ability to do that.*

**❌ 수 없다 – Can’t / Be unable to**

Now, this one has a **stronger nuance** than a simple casual “I can’t go because I’m busy.”

It’s more like: I don’t have the ability to do that. It implies that **the situation is out of your control** because it literally means “there is no way”,

📍 **그 날 파티에 오래 있을 수 없었어요.** → I couldn’t stay at the party for long that day.
Maybe something came up or maybe you weren’t feeling well — but the point is, **you couldn’t** — it wasn’t an option.

📍 **제 친구는 올 수 없을 거예요.** → My friend won’t be able to come.
Again, it’s not that she just doesn’t *want* to — it’s that she **can’t**, due to some condition or limitation.

So, the keyword to remember about this form is “Ability”

**수 있다** = There’s a way → I *can*

**수 없다** = There isn’t a way → I *can’t* because it’s impossible or out of my hands

**이러다 / 저러다 / 그러다 + ㄹ 수 있다 (없다)**

Now, this is a super common expression. You’ll hear all the time — especially when reacting to situations, either to show **empathy** or **shock**

You already know the concept of **이** **저** and **그.** Now, when you combine these with **-러다**, we get.

* **이러다** → to act like this / to do or say so [like this]
* **그러다** → to act like that / to do or say so [like that]
* **저러다** → to act like that / to do or say so [like that over there]

**By the way, these** are **action**.

**And if you add -(으)ㄹ 수 있다, we get 이럴 수 있어 / 저럴 수 있어 / 그럴 수 있어**

These are used to say:

* “That can happen.” “That’s understandable.” “I can see why.” “Yeah, that makes sense.”

🧠 Especially **그럴 수 있어** is **very common** and used a lot in everyday speech when reacting to someone’s situation.

It’s basically a way to say: “I get it. That could happen. I understand where you’re coming from.”

Let’s look at an example:

**A:** 너무 늦게 일어났어. → I woke up too late.

**B:** 그럴 수 있어. → Yeah, that happens. / Understandable.

Now, as for 없다 part, You’ll often hear: 이럴 수가… / 저럴 수가… / 그럴 수가… (Without saying 없어)

These phrases are extremely common in Korean — especially in emotional or dramatic situations — and they typically end with just “수가…”, trailing off.

Technically, this expression comes from: 이럴 수가 없어 = “There’s no way this could happen”
But in real speech, we drop the “없어” because the emotion is already clear from the tone and context.

🧠 Think of it like: “No way…” “How can this be…” “This can’t be happening…”

So 이럴 수가 on its own already delivers the full emotional punch. There’s no need to say 없어

**V.S + ㄹ/을 수(marker) 있다 (없다)**

Now let’s look at how we can add more meaning to the expression (으)ㄹ 수 있다 / 없다, using particles.

Sometimes, we want to emphasize how we can or can’t do something, or show a specific feeling or nuance. So, we can add particles after 수 – like 가, 는, 도, 밖에.

Let’s go over each one.

할 수가 있다 / 없다 This form just adds more emotion.

Like 할 수가 있어 sounds like “Yes, I *can* do this.”

Like in 할 수가 없어! = “There’s just no way” versus “there’s no way”

할 수는 있다 / 없다 는 here shows contrast

So, 할 수**는** 있어요 = “I *can* do it, but…” (I’m not sure I want to, or it’s not easy, you don’t know why you have do it)

할 수**는** 없어요 = “I can’t do it – *at least not this*.”

할 수도 있다 / 없다. We learned that 도 means “also” and “even”.

So, 할 수도 있어요 means “It’s also possible that I can do it.” Or it can translate to “Might” in English.

할 수도 없어요. “I *can’t even* do this.” This sounds a bit more desperate, like, “I have no way to do even this.”

할 수밖에 없다 This one’s very useful and super natural.

We learned “~밖에 없다” right?

Now, if you think of 수 as “choice” it literally means, “There isn’t any choice *but* to do this.”

So… I have no choice but to do it.

**2 more ways to say “Can’t” using 못**

There’s two more ways to say “can’t” in Korean — and these are *super common* in Korean.

**So, first we have 못 + Verb**

못 is an adverb and it’s placed right before the verb, and it works just like 안, which we’ve already seen for saying “don’t.” **But instead of “don’t,” 못 means “can’t.”**

**And it works with:**

* **Only action verbs — not descriptive verbs**
* **Pure Korean verbs like 가다 (to go), 오다 (to come), 하다 (to do)**
* **3-syllable Sino-Korean 하다 verbs, like 변하다 (to change), 취하다 (to take)**

📝 Example:

나는 못 갈 거야. “I won’t be able to go.”

Super easy to use. Just place 못 before the verb.

Now, 못 can also be used to mean someone is bad at doing something.

📝 Another example:

얘는 요리를 못해. “She can’t cook.”
(It means she doesn’t cook well — she’s not good at it.)

**2. Verb Stem + 지 못하다**

**Now, as for this other one, 지 못하다, this one also means “can’t,” and it’s a little more formal or polite than just using 못. And you CAN use this one with descriptive verb.**

Also super easy to use. Just add 지 못하다 after the verb stem regardless 받침 and that’s it.

**📝 Example:**

나는 가지 못할 거야. “I won’t be able to go.”
Same meaning as 못 갈 거야, but this one has a slightly softer or more formal tone.

**📝 Another example with a descriptive verb:**

완벽하지 못해요. “It’s not perfect.”

This doesn’t just mean “it can’t be perfect,” but really: “I tried to make it perfect — but couldn’t.”
So this form implies that there was an effort.

**할 수 없다 vs 못하다 vs 하지 못하다**

So, there’s three ways to say “can’t” in Korean. They’re used differently depending on context, formality, and nuance.

🔹 First, 할 수 없다.

This word 수, means “way” or “possibility.” So literally, it means: “There is no way to do [something].” So, we use this form when something is **out of your control**.

So, you would say 갈 수 없다 when there’s no time or your car’s nor working, etc

**And this one the only form that’s 받침 sensitive.**

**🔹 못 + Verb**

This is the most common and versatile way to say “can’t” in Korean. 못 works like 안, the negation we already learned.

So, it goes directly in front of the verb and works with action verbs, pure Korean verbs and 3 syllable Sino-Korean verbs.

**This can mean:**

* **You don’t know how / You’re bad at it / Or You can’t do it because of circumstances**

**So, this form is flexible — it covers both skill and situation.**

🔹 3. Verb Stem + 지 못하다

This one is almost the same in meaning as 못 + verb, but it’s a little more formal or polished. But this one works with descriptive verbs, too.

**Verb Stem + 지 (를/는/도) 못하다**

Now, with the 못 + verb, the meaning is straightforward. 못해요 means I can’t do it. And that’s it. You can’t go deeper into exactly how you can’t do it, or how you feel about it.

But when you use **지 못하다**, you can add **particles like 를, 는, or 도**, and this gives you the ability to emphasize or contrast the action in much richer ways.

Like you can emphasize **what** you can’t do, or **how completely** you’re unable to do it.

**🔸 Expression 1: 가지 못해요. This is the standard 지 못하다 form.**

**🔸 Expression 2: 가지를 못해요**

The object particle 를 adds emphasis. You can think of this like “The car just won’t go. It physically can’t move.”

You’re focusing on the **action** of “going” as something that is **physically or emotionally blocked**.

👉 You’re emphasizing the act of going as something that’s just not possible.

**🔸 Expression 3: 가지는 못해요**

This one uses 는 implies contrast. So, this sentence means: “I can’t go… but maybe I can do something else.” Let’s say your family asks if you’re coming home this weekend. You might say:

“이번 주말에 집에 가지는 못해요…” but the sentence isn’t over.

You're hinting you’ll call, or FaceTime, or something. So, 는 softens the rejection and adds contrast.

**🔸 Expression 4: 가지도 못해요**

Now we’re adding 도 — which means “even.”

So this becomes: “I can’t even go.” It’s like saying: I have so much work I can’t even go.

Like I’m helpless. “Forget about it. I can’t even go.”

**🔸 So, 어떻게 하지도 못해요. means “I can’t even do anything.” Like I’m stuck here.**

By the way, 어떻게 means how and in Korean we say 어떻게 하다 like “do how” just like we use the word “thing” like do “something” or “anything”
