## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor addresses a common point of confusion for Korean learners: the three different ways to express "can't" or inability. While **못**, **~지 못하다**, and **~ㄹ/을 수 없다** can all translate to "I can't go" in English, they carry distinct grammatical rules and subtle nuances in meaning. The lesson breaks down each form individually, explains the situational differences between personal inability and objective impossibility, and concludes with a critical note on how spacing changes the meaning of the word **못하다**.

---

### The Adverb 못

The word **못** is classified as an adverb. Because it is an adverb, it must be placed directly before the action verb it is modifying. For example, to change the verb **가다** (to go) into "cannot go," you place **못** before it with a space, resulting in **못 가다**. 

The primary nuance of **못** is personal inability. When you use this form, you are implying that you personally cannot do something due to specific circumstances like being too busy, being sick, or being hurt. It does not mean the action itself is impossible for everyone; rather, it suggests that under different circumstances, you might be able to do it. It is a way of saying "it's on me" that I cannot perform the action.

A key grammatical constraint for **못** is that it cannot be used directly before a noun. This is the same rule that applies to the negative adverb **안**. For verbs that consist of a noun plus **하다**, such as **공부하다** (to study), you must place **못** before the **하다** part. Therefore, **공부 못하다** (cannot study) is correct, while **못 공부하다** is incorrect because **공부** is a noun.

---

### The Grammar Form ~지 못하다

The second form, **~지 못하다**, is a grammatical structure rather than a simple adverb. It is created by taking an action verb stem and attaching **~지 못하다** to the end. For the verb **가다**, the stem is **가**, resulting in **가지 못하다**. Note that when used in this specific grammar pattern, there is no space between **못** and **하다**.

In terms of meaning, **~지 못하다** carries the same nuance as the adverb **못**, indicating an inability to do something for personal reasons. The main difference lies in the level of formality. Using **~지 못하다** sounds slightly more formal or "fancy" than using the adverb **못**. It is often more appropriate for formal writing or professional settings, but in general conversation, both are interchangeable without being incorrect.

---

### The Form ~ㄹ/을 수 없다

The third form is constructed by attaching **~ㄹ 수 없다** or **~을 수 없다** to a verb stem. If a verb stem ends in a vowel, like **가다**, it becomes **갈 수 없다**. If it ends in a consonant, like **먹다** (to eat), it becomes **먹을 수 없다**. If the stem already ends in the letter **ㄹ**, such as **살다** (to live), you simply keep the **ㄹ** and add the rest of the form: **살 수 없다**.

The word **수** literally means "ability" or "way," and **없다** means "to not exist." Therefore, this form literally translates to "there is no way" or "there is no ability" to do something. This creates a significant nuance shift compared to **못**. While **못** implies a personal reason, **~ㄹ/을 수 없다** implies objective impossibility or a lack of possibility. 

Using **~ㄹ/을 수 없다** for simple personal excuses can sound awkward because it emphasizes that the action is impossible regardless of the person's will. For example, comparing **못 봤어요** and **볼 수 없었어요** (both meaning "I couldn't see it"), the first implies a simple failure to see it because of a reason like being busy. The second, **볼 수 없었어요**, sounds more literal and emphasizes that something was completely out of your control—as if no matter what you did, there was no way to see it. It removes personal fault by suggesting the situation itself was impossible.

---

### Spacing and Meaning: 못 하다 vs 못하다

The instructor highlights a crucial distinction in written Korean regarding the word **못하다**. While they sound identical when spoken, the spacing changes the meaning entirely.

When written with a space as **못 하다**, it is the combination of the adverb **못** and the verb **하다** (to do). This means "cannot do" for the reasons previously discussed (busy, sick, etc.). For example, **요리 못 해요** (with a space) means "I cannot cook [right now/today]" perhaps because I am too busy.

When written without a space as **못하다**, it becomes a single verb meaning "to be bad at" or "to be unskilled at" something. Therefore, **요리 못해요** (no space) means "I am bad at cooking." This version refers to a lack of skill or talent rather than a situational inability to perform the task.
