## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces a critical grammatical shift required when speaking about the desires and feelings of others. While beginners learn to express their own wants using the form 고 싶다, this form is restricted to the first and second person. To talk about what a third person wants or feels, Korean requires the addition of the auxiliary verb 하다. This lesson explains the mechanics of the ~아/어 하다 construction, why it is culturally and linguistically necessary in Korean, and how it transforms descriptive verbs into action verbs, thereby unlocking new grammatical possibilities like commands and progressive tenses.

---

### The Limitation of 고 싶다 and the Third Person

The standard form for expressing "want to" is attaching 고 싶다 to an action verb stem. For example, 가다 (to go) becomes 가고 싶어요 (I want to go). However, the instructor emphasizes that this form is strictly for "I" or "you" situations. In Korean, it is linguistically considered impossible to know 100% what another person is feeling or wanting. Therefore, using the standard 고 싶다 for a third person sounds unnatural and technically incorrect.

The instructor defines the persons as follows: the first person is "I" or "me"; the second person is "you" (speaking directly to someone); and the third person is anyone else (he, she, they, or a specific name like Chulsoo). To correctly say "Chulsoo wants to go," one must use the form 고 싶어 하다.

- **Incorrect:** 철수는 가고 싶어요.
- **Correct:** 철수는 가고 싶어 해요. / Chulsoo wants to go.

---

### Transforming Descriptive Verbs with ~아/어 하다

The construction ~아/어 하다 is not limited to "wanting." It is used with descriptive verbs (adjectives) to describe the apparent feelings or states of a third person. To create this form, take a descriptive verb stem, conjugate it using the standard 아/어/여 rules, and then attach 하다.

The instructor provides several examples of this transformation:
- **기쁘다 (to be happy) → 기뻐하다**
- **슬프다 (to be sad) → 슬퍼하다**
- **힘들다 (to be difficult/hard) → 힘들어하다**
- **무섭다 (to be scared) → 무서워하다**

Visually, the instructor notes that while there is usually no space between the conjugated verb and 하다, the specific form 싶어 하다 is an exception and does include a space.

---

### Meaning Shift: From State to Action

Attaching 하다 does more than just change the "person" of the sentence; it changes the word class from a descriptive verb to an action verb. A descriptive verb like 슬프다 describes a state of being sad. Adding 하다 changes the meaning to "to feel sad" or "to behave outwardly as if sad."

When you say 저는 슬퍼요 (I am sad), you are stating your internal state. When you say 철수는 슬퍼해요 (Chulsoo is sad), you are technically saying that Chulsoo is "feeling" or "acting" sad based on your observation. This allows the speaker to make assumptions about a third person's internal state without claiming absolute knowledge of their mind.

Example sentences shown on screen:
> **마크 씨도 김치를 먹어보고 싶어 해요.** / Mark also wants to try (eating) kimchi.
> **잭슨 씨가 많이 기뻐해요.** / Jackson is very happy (feeling/acting happy).
> **아이는 무서워했어요.** / The child was afraid (felt/acted afraid).

---

### Grammatical Benefits of the Action Verb Form

Because ~아/어 하다 turns descriptive verbs into action verbs, it allows the speaker to use grammatical structures that are forbidden for descriptive verbs.

First, you can issue commands. You cannot tell someone "to be" a certain state using a descriptive verb (e.g., 슬프지 마세요 is incorrect). However, you can tell them not to "act" or "feel" that way using the action verb form.
- **Correct Command:** 슬퍼하지 마세요. / Don't be sad (Don't act/feel sad).

Second, you can use the progressive tense (~고 있다), which is generally reserved for action verbs.
- **Progressive Example:** 지민 씨는 힘들어하고 있어요. / Jimin is having a hard time (is currently struggling/feeling the difficulty).

The instructor concludes by noting that while this form is primarily for the third person, it is occasionally seen in the first or second person, though he does not recommend it for learners as a standard practice.
