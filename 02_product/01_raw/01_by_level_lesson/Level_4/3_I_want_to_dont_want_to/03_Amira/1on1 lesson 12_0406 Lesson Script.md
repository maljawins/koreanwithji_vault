Okay now, let’s learn 도 & 만.

* **도** means **"also"** or **"even."**
* **만** means **"only."**

Both of these particles can be placed **after any noun**, and they can also be **combined with other particles** like **에, 에서, 부터, 까지, 에게, and 한테**.

And they can be attached to verbs as well but we’ll revisit those when we learn about sentence connectors. You know things like “Even if” “Only when”

Slide 13

In English, the sentence **“I also study math”** can have three different meanings depending on the emphasis:

1. **“I, too, study math”** – Someone else studies math, and I do, too
2. **“I study physics, and I study math too”** → I study other subjects, and math as well.
3. **“I do many things, and studying math is one of them”** – emphasis on *studying*.

So, in English, the **intonation and context** dictate the meaning
But in Korean, the meaning changes based on **where you place the particle**.

* **저도 수학을 공부해요** → *I, too, study math.*
* **저는 수학도 공부해요** → *I study math too (along with other subjects).*
* **저는 수학 공부도 해요** → *I also do math study (along with other activities).*

This rule also applies to **만** ("only"), but for now, let’s focus on **도**.

Slide 14

So, here’s another example sentence.

* **저도 강아지를 좋아해요**
  → *I, too, like dogs.*
  (Meaning: I’m not the only one who likes dogs, or *Even I* like dogs.)
* **저는 강아지도 좋아해요**
  → *I like dogs too.*
  (Meaning: I like other animals or things, and dogs are one of them. It could also mean *I even like dogs*, depending on context.)

In Korean, **도** covers both “also” and “even” — the difference comes from context.

If you think about it, the word “even” is just like a dramatic version of “also”, used when there’s contrast or surprise.

So, Korean doesn’t have a separate word for “Even”.

Also, **도** can attach to other particles to add more meaning:

* **월요일에도 미팅이 있어요**
  → *I have a meeting on Monday, too* / *Even on Monday.*
* **저는 집에서도 운동을 했어요**
  → *I worked out at home, too* / *Even at home.*
* **친구한테도 선물을 줬어요**
  → *I gave a gift to my friend, too* / *Even to my friend.*

❗ **Note:** 도 cannot be added *after* topic (은/는), subject (이/가), or object (을/를) particles. Instead, **도 replaces them**.

For example:

* ❌ 저**는도** / 저**가도** / 저**를도**
* ✅ 저**도**

Slide 15

Just like **도**, **만** (meaning *only*) can be placed after any noun — and **where** you place it changes the meaning.

It can also be attached to other particles like **에, 에서, 부터, 까지, 에게, 한테**, just like **도**.

The only difference is that if you can use 만 with subject, topic, and object marker.

But 만 should come before subject topic and object marker so it becomes 만이 만은 만을.

But you usually drop the 을 and 이 and just replace them using 만.

However, there is a slight difference between just 만 and 만은

**So what’s the difference?**

* **난 이 순간만 기억해.**
  → *This is the only moment I remember.* (Plain and factual)
* **이 순간만은 기억해.**
  → *I might forget other moments, but not this one.*
  (Shows contrast — thanks to 은)

If you said 이 순간만 it just simply means this is the only moment I remember but if you said 만은 기억해, it would mean I could maybe forget other moments but at least not this moment.

So, the feeling and the nuance is completely different.

Slide 16

Now, there’s **another way to say "only"** in Korean, and that’s **밖에**.

밖에 **literally means “outside of,”** and it can be translated as **“anything but.”**
But here’s the important part —
**밖에 is always used with negative verbs. And i**t cannot be used in a positive sentence.

Think of it like this —
It works similarly to the word **“any”** in English when used in a **negative sentence**.
That’s why it always appears with **안** or **~지 않다** forms

For example:

**저는 학교에서밖에 공부를 안 해요**
⤷ I don’t study anywhere but at school.
⤷ In other words, **I only study at school.**

Another one:

**오늘 한 시간밖에 없어요**
⤷ I don’t have anything but one hour today.
⤷ Meaning, **I only have one hour.**

**It can also be used figuratively.**
For example:

**제 친구는 남자친구밖에 몰라요**
⤷ It doesn’t literally mean she only knows her boyfriend.
⤷ It means she’s so focused on him that she’s blind to anything else — like, **she’s all about her boyfriend.**

And 밖에 can also be attached to other particles.

For example: 에어컨이 사무실에 밖에 없어요

⤷ The air conditioner isn’t anywhere but in the office.

⤷ Meaning, I only have an air conditioner at the office — not at home.

Just make sure to put a space between the other particle and 밖에.

This is because 밖 is a separate word and **밖에** is treated as an adverbial phrase whereas particles like 에 and 도 are attached to the noun.

But in casual writing like on social media, many native speakers do write **에밖에** without a space. It’s common, but not correct.

Slide 8

Let’s compare 만 with 밖에.

Now let’s compare **만** and **밖에**.

First, **밖에** **cannot** be used with the descriptive verb **‘아니다’**.
So,
❌ 여기서 나밖에 남자가 아니야 should be
✅ 여기서 나만 남자야 – *I’m the only man here.*

Also, **밖에** can’t be used in **propositive** or **imperative** sentences.
So,
❌ 이것밖에 하지 마 → ✅ 이것만 해 – *Only do this.*
❌ 오늘은 공부밖에 안 하자 → ✅ 오늘은 공부만 하자 – *Let’s just study today.*

Now, there are times when **밖에** sounds **more natural** than **만**.

Both mean *“I only eat vegetables,”* but **B** sounds more natural in many situations because **밖에** adds the nuance that *something is lacking or not enough*.
So, A might sound like: *“I only eat vegetables and I’m okay with that.”*
But B could sound like: *“I only eat vegetables... I wish I could eat other things too.”*

Also, **만** can be used in both **positive** and **negative** sentences, but **밖에** is used **only in negative sentences**.

For example:

* ✅ 집에 동생만 있어요 – *Only my brother is home.*
* ✅ 집에 동생밖에 없어요 – *No one else but my brother is home.*
* ✅ 집에 동생만 없어요 – *Everyone else is home except my brother.*
* ❌ 집에 동생밖에 있어요 – This is incorrect. **밖에** must be paired with a **negative verb**.

**Slide 9 – Script**
Now, let’s talk about how to say **“to someone”** in Korean — when you’re giving something *to a person or animal like your pet.*

In English, you can say:
“I gave Amira a candy” or “I gave a candy to Amira.”
But in Korean, you **must** use a particle to mark the recipient.

So, you’d say:

* 아미라**에게** 사탕을 줬어요
* 아미라**한테** 사탕을 줬어요

They both mean: *“I gave a candy to Amira.”*

Now here’s the difference:

* **한테** is much more common in everyday, spoken Korean.
* **에게** is more formal and usually appears in written Korean — in essays, news articles, or formal documents.

There’s also an **honorific** version:

* **께**

**께** is used when you’re speaking respectfully to or about someone higher in status — like a teacher, boss, or elder.

For example:

* 선생님**께** 사탕을 드렸어요
  (*I gave a candy to the teacher.*)

But you could also say:

* 선생님**에게** 사탕을 드렸어요
* 선생님**한테** 사탕을 드렸어요

All three are grammatically correct, but **께** is the most polite.
What really matters here is that you also switch the verb **주다 → 드리다** to keep the humble tone consistent.

So remember:

* 한테 → casual
* 에게 → formal/written
* 께 → honorific
  And don’t forget to adjust the verb when speaking politely.

**Slide 10 – Script**

Now let’s look at how to say **“from someone”** in Korean — like when you receive something **from a person.**

You’ll use: **에게서**, **한테서**, or **께**. The 서 here adds the meaning of "from" — but it's **optional**, so you might hear people say it with or without 서.

For example:

* 아미라**에게(서)** 사탕을 받았어요
* 아미라**한테(서)** 사탕을 받았어요
  Both mean: *“I received a candy from Amira.”*

Again, **한테서** is more common in spoken Korean, and **에게서** is more formal or written.

Now, the honorific form is a little different:
When receiving from someone you respect — like a teacher or parents — use **께**.
So:

* 부모님**께** 선물을 받았어요
  *“I received a gift from my parents.”*

But here’s something important:
**께서** and **께** are not the same thing.
**께서** is actually the **honorific subject marker** — it replaces **이/가** when the subject is someone of higher status.

For example:

* 선생님**께서** 저에게 사탕을 주셨어요
  (*The teacher gave me a candy.*)
  Here, **께서** is marking *“the teacher”* as the subject in an honorific way.

You can also say:

* 선생님이 저에게 사탕을 주셨어요
  (*Same meaning, just less honorific*)

But when you’re saying *you received* something from the teacher, it becomes:

* 선생님**께** 사탕을 받았어요
  (“I received a candy from the teacher”) — and **not** 께서.

So quick recap:

* **에게(서)** / **한테(서)** → from someone
* **께** → from someone respectfully
* **께서** → honorific subject marker (not "from")

**Slide 11 – Script**

Now let’s look at how **도**, **만**, and **밖에** combine with other particles to give more specific meanings.

This is where Korean gets really interesting — and more expressive.

Let’s start with **에**:

* **에도** = *Also at / Also in* (Time or Location)
* **에만** = *Only at / Only in*
* **에 밖에** = *Nowhere but (time or place)*

**에서** is for locations where actions happen:

* **에서도** = *Also at (location)*
* **에서만** = *Only at (location)*
* **에서 밖에** = *Nowhere else but (location)*

**부터** and **까지** deal with time and range:

* **부터도** = *Also from* (starting point)
* **부터만** = *Only from*
* **부터 밖에** = *From nowhere but*
* **까지도** = *Also until*
* **까지만** = *Only until*
* **까지 밖에** = *Until nowhere but* (as in, limited range)

**에게** and **한테** (to someone):

* **에게도 / 한테도** = *Also to someone*
* **에게만 / 한테만** = *Only to someone*
* **에게 밖에 / 한테 밖에** = *To no one but…*

So, by combining particles, you can be really specific about who, when, where, or how **only** something happened or how it’s the **only exception**.

This is a powerful way to make your Korean more natural and nuanced.

**Slide 12 – Script**

Let’s go over some example sentences to see how particles like **도**, **만**, and **밖에** combine with location/time particles like **에** and **에서**. These combinations help express more precise meanings such as *also at*, *only at*, or *nowhere else but*.

**1. 이 가게에도 사람이 많아요**

**“There’s a lot of people also at this store.”**

* Here, **에도** is a combination of **에** (location) + **도** (also).
* This sentence implies that **other places** also have many people, and this store is **one of them**.
* It’s like saying: "This store, too, is crowded—just like the others."

✅ Useful for adding something to a list or comparing multiple places.

**2. 이 공원에만 나무가 하나도 없어요**

**“There’s not even one tree only at this park.”**
*(This is the only park that has no trees.)*

* **에만** = **only at**
* The nuance is that **all other parks have trees**, but **this park is the exception.**

**Structure breakdown**:

* **에만** restricts the statement to *this location only*
* **하나도 없어요** adds the idea of *not even one*, emphasizing total absence

💡 This is a great example of using **에만** to highlight contrast or exception.

**3. 코알라는 호주에 밖에 안 살아요**

**“Koalas live only in Australia.”**

* **에 밖에** = nowhere else but this place
* **안 살아요** = do not live
* The entire sentence structure shows **exclusive location** with a **negative verb**, which is required when using **밖에**.

**Key grammar note**:

* **밖에** must be used with a **negative verb** like **안 살아요**, **없어요**, or **못 해요**.
* So this literally means: **“Except for Australia, they don’t live anywhere else.”**

✅ This gives a stronger sense of limitation than just saying **호주에만 살아요**.

**4. 여기(에)서도 빵을 팔아요?**

**“Do they sell bread also here?”**
*(Do they have bread here too?)*

* **에서도** = **also at (this location where action happens)**
* **빵을 팔아요** = sell bread
* This sentence assumes bread is being sold at other places and asks if it’s sold **here too**.

**Note**: **여기서** or **여기에서** are both fine. **에서도** adds “also” to the location.

✅ Natural question when comparing different branches or locations of a store.

**5.저는 노래방에서만 노래를 해요**

**“I only sing at karaoke.”**

* **에서만** = only at (this location)
* **노래를 해요** = sing
* The sentence implies that **you never sing at home, in the car, etc.—only in karaoke rooms**.

**Structure breakdown**:

* **노래방에서만** = *only at karaoke*
* This type of sentence is used to emphasize a limited habit or behavior

💡 You can use **에서만** to stress exclusivity of an action’s location.

**6. 이 음식은 한국에서 밖에 못 먹어요**

**“You can only eat this food in Korea.”**

* **에서 밖에** = nowhere else but this location
* **못 먹어요** = can’t eat
* This literally means: *Except in Korea, you can’t eat this food.*

**Compare with**:

* If you said **한국에서만 먹어요**, it just means you *choose* to eat it only in Korea.
* But with **밖에 + negative**, it emphasizes **lack of access elsewhere** — it’s more factual or absolute.

✅ This is a great way to highlight something that’s unique to a place.

**Slide 14 – Script: Particle Combinations with 부터 and 까지**

In this slide, we’re looking at how **부터** (starting from) and **까지** (up to/until) combine with **도**, **만**, and **밖에** to express more precise meaning around time or range.

Let’s go through the examples one by one.

**1. 월요일부터도 일이 많았어요**

**“I had a lot of work even from Monday.”**

* **부터도** = *even from*
* The nuance here is: not only Tuesday or Wednesday—**even from Monday**, things were already busy.

✅ This implies that the busy schedule started earlier than expected.

**2. 지금부터만 열심히 하세요**

**“Just work hard starting from now.”**

* **부터만** = *only starting from*
* This suggests: “Don’t worry about before, just focus from now on.”
* It implies that **only the present and future matter** for your effort.

✅ Great expression for motivation or redirection.

**Structure breakdown**:

* **지금부터** = from now
* **만** = only → **지금부터만** = just starting now

**3. 어제부터 밖에 시간이 없었어요**

**“I only had time starting from yesterday.”**

* **부터 밖에** = *not before — only starting from*
* This sentence implies you didn’t have time **until yesterday**; yesterday was the **earliest** you could start.

**Important note**:

* **밖에** must pair with a **negative verb**: **없었어요** = didn't have

✅ This structure is useful when explaining a delay or limited availability.

**4. 밤 열 시까지도 공부했어요**

**“I studied even until 10 p.m.”**

* **까지도** = *even up to*
* Emphasizes how **late** you studied. Maybe people didn’t expect you to study that long, but you did — **even until 10pm**.

✅ Useful for highlighting unexpected effort or dedication.

**Structure breakdown**:

* **밤 열 시까지** = until 10 p.m.
* **도** = even → **까지도** = even up to this time

**5. 여기까지만 이야기할게요**

**“I’ll talk only up to here.”**

* **까지만** = *only until*
* You’re setting a clear boundary — not going beyond this point in what you say.
* Could be physical, like a section of a book, or figurative, like sensitive info.

✅ Common when setting limits in speech, explanation, or teaching.

**6. 3(삼) 페이지까지 밖에 못 읽었어요**

**“I could only read up to page 3.”**

* **까지 밖에** = *up to… and no more than that*
* Indicates a limitation in **extent** — you didn’t go beyond page 3.

**Again**:

* **밖에** requires a **negative** verb. . **못 읽었어요** = couldn’t read

✅ Use this when expressing limitation in time, amount, distance, or range.

**Slide 16 – Script: 한테 + 도, 만, 밖에**

Now let’s take a look at how the particle **한테** (to someone) combines with **도**, **만**, and **밖에** to express different nuances.

These combinations allow you to add emotional or logical emphasis when talking about giving or saying something **to someone**.

**1. 친한 친구한테도 말 안 했어요**

**“I didn’t even tell my close friend.”**

* **한테도** = *even to someone*
* This emphasizes that you didn’t share it **even with** someone close—so likely, you didn’t tell *anyone*.

✅ Use this when expressing secrecy or how private something is.

🧠 Emotional nuance: “If I didn’t even tell my close friend, then I definitely didn’t tell others.”

**2. 그 얘기는 너한테만 말했어**

**“I told that story only to you.”**

* **한테만** = *only to someone*
* You’re emphasizing exclusivity — nobody else heard that story.

✅ Commonly used to express trust or to imply someone shouldn’t spread the information.

🧠 Emotional nuance: “I trusted you with this. Don’t share it.”

**3. 선생님한테밖에 질문을 못 했어요**

**“I couldn’t ask anyone but the teacher.”**
(= I didn’t/couldn’t ask anyone else.)

* **한테 밖에** = *to no one but…*
* You’re saying you had **no option** but to ask the teacher.

✅ Note: **밖에** is always paired with a **negative verb**, like **못 했어요**.

🧠 Contextual nuance: “I didn’t have the confidence or chance to ask anyone else but the teacher.”

**Slide 17 – Script: 만 + 을, 은, 이**

Now let’s take a look at how **만** combines with other particles like **을**, **은**, and **이**.

This helps us add emphasis or highlight contrast, depending on the sentence structure.

**1. 진실만을 말하겠습니다**

**“I will speak only the truth.”**

* **만을** = Only \_\_\_ + object marker **을**
* This combination emphasizes that you will say **nothing but** the truth.
* It’s more formal or dramatic than just **진실만 말해요.**

🧠 Nuance: You’re highlighting that **truth alone** is the object of your action. This is often used in public speaking, news, or formal pledges.

**2. 이것만은 꼭 기억해요**

**“At least this, make sure to remember.”**

* **만은** = Only \_\_\_ + topic marker **은**
* When **만** is combined with **은**, it puts contrastive or emphatic focus on the topic.

✅ Use it when you want to say, “Even if you forget everything else, **at least remember this.**”

🧠 Nuance: This structure is good for highlighting **the minimum** someone should do.

**3. 너만이 날 이해할 수 있어**

**“Only you can understand me.”**

* **만이** = Only \_\_\_ + subject marker **이**
* It emphasizes that the **subject** is the one and only person capable of doing something.

**Structure breakdown**:

* **너만이** = only you (as subject)
* **날** = 나 + 를 = me
* **이해할 수 있어** = can understand

🧠 Nuance: Strong emotional emphasis. Use this to highlight a unique bond or exclusive ability.

는 + Other Particles = Contrast

**Slide 18 는 + Other Particles = Contrast**

Okay, now, Earlier, you asked me how the topic marker can imply contrast—this part should help make that clearer."

When you attach the **topic marker 는** after other particles like **에, 에서, 부터, 까지, 한테**, you’re not just stating a fact— You’re **highlighting contrast**.

You're basically saying, “As for *this* specific time/place/person… something is true—but not necessarily for others.”

Let’s go through each one slowly.

**1. 에는 → As for this time/location…**

🧠 **Used with time or place** to highlight **a specific moment** compared to others.

📍 **12시에는 수업이 있어요**

“At 12 o'clock, I have class.”
💡 Implied: “But I might be free at other times.”

* **12시** = 12 o’clock
* **에** = time particle
* **는** = topic marker → shows contrast
* You're marking **this** time as different from others.

You can use this sentence when someone asks if you're free at 12. It's not that you don't want to meet them—you're just busy at that time. So, it subtly suggests: 'I'm busy at 12, but maybe try asking about 11 or 1 instead.

**2. 에서는 → As for this place…
📍 이 카페에서는 커피를 안 마셔요
“I don’t drink coffee at this café.”**

This sentence can imply two slightly different contrasts:

1. “I don’t drink coffee *here* (but maybe elsewhere).”
2. “I don’t drink *coffee* here (but I drink something else).”

💡 The contrast can be:

* Location-based: “I drink coffee, just not *at this café*.”
* Item-based: “At this café, I don’t drink *coffee*, but I drink something else.”

This is possible because:

* 에서 = location particle (where the action happens)
* 는 = topic particle that introduces contrast

🧠 Use this when comparing:

* Your actions across places (“Here I do X, but not there”)
* Your choices within the same place (“At this place, I do A but not B”)

**You're subtly drawing a boundary: “In this context (this place), this action doesn't happen.”**

**3. 부터는 → As for from here/time on…**

📍 **여기부터는 제 자리입니다**

“From here on out, it’s my seat (my space).”
💡 Implied: “Before this point, it wasn’t—but *this* is my boundary.”

* **여기부터** = from here
* **는** = marks the contrast → “from here is different”
* You’re setting a line. It’s great for personal space, rules, or zones.

Another example:

“From this chapter on, things get harder.”
That’s the kind of contrast you're expressing with **부터는**.

**4. 까지는 → As for until this point…**

📍 **지금까지는 많이 참았습니다**

“Until now, I’ve tolerated a lot.”
💡 Implied: “But not anymore. I’ve had enough.”

* **지금까지** = until now
* **는** = contrast → “That was then; now is different.”
* You're clearly drawing a line in time.

🧠 Use this when showing a **change of state**:

“I was patient—up to now. But now, something’s changing.”

**5. 한테는 / 에게는 → As for this person…**

📍 **왜 나한테는 과자밖에 안 줬어?**

“Why did you give *me* only snacks?”
💡 Implied: “You gave others better stuff!”

* **한테** = to (someone)
* **는** = contrast → me vs. others
* **과자밖에** = only snacks

You’re not just stating what you got—you’re comparing it to what **others got**.

This use of **한테는** expresses a **feeling of unfairness** or unequal treatment.

Slide 19

Now, let’s learn the **progressive tense** in Korean—basically, how to say **"I am doing something"** or **"I was doing something"**.

**🔧 How it works:**

Take an **action verb stem** and add **고 있다**.

Why **only action verbs**? Because you can’t be *beautiful-ing* or *good-ing*.
Words like 예쁘다 (to be pretty) or 좋다 (to be good) are **descriptive verbs**, not actions.
If you want to say something like “I’m becoming pretty,” that’s a different structure we’ll learn later.

**✅ All you have to conjugate is 있다**

Let’s look at how this works in different tenses:

**📍 Present tense:**

**한국에 살고 있어요**
= I am living in Korea.
→ 살다 (to live) + 고 있다 = 살고 있다
→ Conjugated: 살고 있어요

It shows that the action is **ongoing right now**.

**📍 Past tense:**

**운전하고 있었어요**
= I was driving.
→ 운전하다 (to drive) + 고 있다 = 운전하고 있다
→ Conjugated in past tense: 운전하고 있었어요

💡 Use this when you want to describe what you were doing at a moment in the past.

**📍 Future / Promise form:**

**나만 알고 있을게**
= Only I will be knowing. [I won’t tell anyone else.]
→ 알다 (to know) + 고 있다 = 알고 있다
→ Conjugated: 알고 있을게

“Only I will be knowing” is awkward in English but it works perfectly fine in Korean. It means I won’t tell anyone else. This is a very common phrase used when making a promise to keep a secret.

**💬 Summary:**

* **고 있다** = ~ing
* Action verbs only
* Just conjugate **있다** for different tenses
* Use it to describe what’s happening **right now**, **was happening**, or even what you’ll **keep doing**

**Slide 20**

Now here’s another way to say you’re doing something in Korean.

**💡 What's “중(中)”?**

**중** is a Sino-Korean word that means **“middle”** — like the middle of an action.

So **~는 중이다** literally means:

“to be in the middle of [doing something]”

This form also works with **action verbs only** (not descriptive verbs).

**🔧** How to use it:

Take the verb stem, attach 는, then add 중이다.
Then, conjugate 이다 as usual (예요/이에요/였어요, etc.).

📍 Present Tense Examples:

* 지금 가는 중이에요
  = I’m on my way / I’m going now.
* 저녁 먹는 중이었어요
  = I’m eating dinner.

👉 Emphasis here is that you’re in the middle of the action — it’s still ongoing.

So, if you said 중이었어요, it would mean that you were right up until this very moment, eating dinner, possibly still sitting at the dinner table.

⏳Now, I should note that this form cannot have a future tense.

* Although you can technically conjugate 이다 to 일 거야, this means you’re guessing. For example, 샤워하는 중 일거야 means I guess she’s taking a shower.
* So, for future actions, stick with 고 있을 거예요.

🈶 Special note about 하다 verbs:

With 하다 verbs, you can drop the ~하는 part and just say:

* 운전 중이에요 = I’m driving
  (Instead of 운전하는 중이에요 — both are fine, but this is shorter)

You’ll also see signs like:

* 영업 중 = OPEN (literally: "in the middle of business activity")
  🏪 You’ll see this sign in shops all over Korea!

Slide 21

**🎓 Lesson Script: 고 있다 vs 는 중이다 – Timeline Focus Only**

Let’s compare these two progressive forms just by how they reflect **time**.

**1. 고 있다 – Broader Timeframe**

This form describes actions happening **around the present**, not necessarily at the exact moment.

✅ Example:

👉 요즘 운동하고 있어 – I’m working out (these days)

You can use it even if you're **not doing it right this second**.
It still counts as “ongoing.”

**2. 는 중이다 – Real-Time Focus**

This form describes an action that is happening **right now** — in this exact moment.

✅ Example:

👉 친구랑 얘기 중이야 – I’m talking with a friend (at this very moment)

The word **중(中)** means "middle", so this literally says you're **in the middle of the action**.

**3. 🚫 No Future Tense for 는 중이다**

Which is why **는 중이다** can’t have a proper future tense.

You might hear: 샤워 중일 거야 “She’s probably taking a shower”

But here, **중일 거야** means **“I guess she’s in the middle of…”**, not “she *will be* in the middle of…”

🧠 It’s a **guess** in the present—not a plan for the future.

If you want to express a future intention, you’d use **고 있을 거야** or another future form.

Now, let’s learn how to say **“I want to do something”**

**👉** Action Verb Stem + 고 싶다

This literally means "want to do [something]." It’s similar to saying "I want to ~" in English.

🧍‍♀️🧍‍♂️ Who can you use this with?

This part is really important. You can only use this form with the first person and the second person.

Which means you can only say “I want to” or ask someone else “Do you want to?” or “What do you want to do?” only because Korean doesn’t allow you to assume what someone else wants.

If you really think about it, it makes sense logically because you can’t “know” what other people want you can only “assume” that.

We’ll learn that one in detail when we talk about “Compound verbs”. But basically, this 하다 after 싶어 indicates that they’re behaving in a way they want to do something.

Or we also have another form which is “They said that they wanted it”, called quoting form, but that’s for another lesson

This is a question, so it's second person. You're asking the listener about their own desire, and that's okay.

Slide 23

**Why do we say “피자가 먹고 싶어요”?**
Let’s talk about why we sometimes use **"가"** instead of **"를"** in sentences with **~고 싶다**.

Now, since 피자 is the object of the verb 먹다, you’d expect **피자** to be marked with **를**, right?

But, both of these sentences are actually **grammatically correct**:

1. **피자가 먹고 싶어요**
2. **피자를 먹고 싶어요**

But the nuance is slightly different. Let me explain why that is.

**✅ Sentence 1: 피자가 먹고 싶어요**

In this sentence, **피자** is marked with **가**, which means it’s the **subject** of the verb **싶다**.

Here’s the key point:
**싶다 is a descriptive verb**.
It works more like **"to be desirable"** or **"to be wanted."**

So, what’s the subject of 싶다?
👉 It’s the **pizza** itself.

Think of this sentence as:

“Pizza is wanted to be eaten (by me).” or more naturally: “Pizza is the thing that I want.”

So, using 가 is actually **more natural** in this case.

So when Koreans say **피자가 먹고 싶어요**, they’re not just listing pizza as an object—they’re putting **emphasis on the pizza itself**.

**✅ Sentence 2: 피자를 먹고 싶어요**

Now let’s look at this version.

Here, **피자** is marked with **를**, so it’s clearly the **object** of the verb **먹다**—to eat.

In this case, the verb **싶다** works more like an **auxiliary verb** attached to **먹다**.
So the structure focuses more on the **action** of eating.

The nuance is a little more neutral:

“I want to eat pizza.” It’s less about emphasizing pizza itself, and more about wanting to do the action of eating it. This version is still fine and natural, but it sounds just slightly more **mechanical or direct**.

**🧠 So, which one should you use?**

Honestly, both are fine.
But if you want to sound **more natural and native-like**, you'll hear Koreans use the **~가 먹고 싶어요** structure more often in daily conversation—especially when they're craving something or expressing a feeling.

So next time you say **you want to eat something**, try saying:

**치킨이 먹고 싶어요**
**떡볶이가 먹고 싶어요**

You're putting the spotlight on what you want—**that specific thing**

This form directly expresses the idea that you **dislike** or **don’t want to do** something, using the verb 싫다,

You can think of **~기 싫다** as "Doing [this] is unpleasant to me."

It’s especially common when you're feeling tired, annoyed, or just not in the mood.

**💬 Examples:**

* **가기 싫어요** — I don’t want to go
* **먹기 싫어요** — I don’t want to eat

* **운동하기 싫어요** — I don’t want to exercise

Now let’s talk about how to say **“I want something”** in Korean.

* Song lyrics
* Books or formal writing
* Situations that are more emotional, poetic, or dramatic

* **먹고 싶어요** (I want to eat)
* **마시고 싶어요** (I want to drink)

* **갖고 싶어요 (I want to have)**

The structure is the same—just switch the verb based on what you want to do.

For example, If you want to say “I want Kimchi.”, because you want to EAT Kimchi.

If you want a new phone, you say “I want to HAVE a new phone”, using 가지다.

Slide 33

Let’s clarify something important.

In English, we can say “have” for pretty much anything like:

* “I want to **have** dinner”
* “I want to **have** a dog”

But in Korean, the verb **가지다 does not** work the same way for everything.

So some people say:

* **햄버거를 가지고 싶어요**
* **강아지를 가지고 싶어요**

These sentences are **grammatically correct**, but they sound **awkward** and **unnatural** in daily Korean.

Why?

Because **가지다** carries a nuance of **possessing** something, almost like owning an object.

So when you say:

**햄버거를 가지고 싶어요**
It sounds like you want to *possess* a hamburger—not eat it.

And:

**강아지를 가지고 싶어요**
Sounds like you want to *own* a dog like a possession — not raise or care for it.

Slide 44

Now, Let's learn how to say **"Don't do something"** in Korean!

Take a **verb stem** and attach **지 말다** to tell someone **not to do** something.

Example:
**하다** (to do) → **하지 말다** (to not do)

**🧩 Conjugation of 말다**

**말다** is an **irregular verb** that ends in **ㄹ**, and so

| **Form** | **Sentence** | **Politeness Level** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **하지 마** | Don’t do it. | Informal Low |
| **하지 마요** | Don’t do it. | Informal High |
| **하지 마세요** | Don’t do it. | Informal High (Honorific) ✅ Most common |
| **하지 마십시오** | Don’t do it. | Formal High (Honorific) |

✅ You'll hear **하지 마세요** the most in everyday polite speech.

**💡 Note: What is 말다?**

* **말다** is a **helping verb**, which means it **cannot be used by itself**.
* It’s always used as part of another grammar form, like **지 말다**.
* Its meaning is:
  → to stop
  → to cease
  → to not do (something)

**🧠 Bonus Tip: Another use of 말다**

You’ll also see **말다** used in expressions like:

**이거 말고 저거** → *“Not this one, that one.”*

Here, **말고** means "not A but B".
This is another grammar form we’ll cover **in a future lesson**, so don’t worry about it for now!
