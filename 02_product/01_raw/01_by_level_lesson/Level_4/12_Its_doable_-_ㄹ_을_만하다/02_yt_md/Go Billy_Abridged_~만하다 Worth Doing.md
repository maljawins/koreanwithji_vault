## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the intermediate grammar form 만하다, which primarily conveys that an action is "worth doing" or "acceptable." The lesson is divided into two distinct parts: the more common usage where 만하다 follows a verb to describe worth or understandability, and a second usage where it follows a noun to compare sizes.

The pedagogical approach focuses on the nuance of "acceptability" rather than "high praise." By using this form, a speaker suggests that while something might not be the absolute best, it is certainly not bad and meets a certain threshold of quality or logic. The instructor provides clear conjugation rules for both action and descriptive verbs and concludes with the comparative "size" usage to give a complete picture of the word's functions in Korean.

---

### Conjugation and Meaning with Action Verbs
To create this form with a verb, you take the verb stem and attach the future tense marker. If the stem ends in a consonant, you add 을; if it ends in a vowel, you add ㄹ. For stems already ending in ㄹ, such as 살다 (to live), you simply use the stem 살 as is. This is followed by a space and then the word 만하다.

The instructor demonstrates this with several examples on screen. For 하다 (to do), the stem 하 becomes 할 만하다. For 먹다 (to eat), the stem 먹 becomes 먹을 만하다. While this form can technically be used with both action and descriptive verbs, it is most frequently paired with action verbs to mean "worth doing."

Beyond "worth doing," the instructor emphasizes that it can mean "acceptable to do" or "not bad." It is a common way to recommend something to others without overhyping it. For instance, saying a place is "worth living in" implies it is a viable, decent option. It can also translate to "doable" or "bearable," signaling that a situation is manageable.

---

### Usage as a Descriptive Verb
The instructor clarifies that 만하다 itself functions as a descriptive verb (adjective) and is used to describe a subject. Therefore, the thing being described usually takes the topic marker 은/는 or the subject marker 이/가.

Several practical examples illustrate this descriptive function:
- **부산은 갈만해요.** / Busan is worth visiting (going to).
- **우리 차는 오래됐지만 아직 쓸만해요.** / Our car is old, but it's still worth using (it still works).
- **조금 달긴 하지만 먹을만해요.** / It's a bit sweet, but it's worth eating (it's not bad).
- **예전 집은 조금 춥긴 했지만 위치가 좋아서 살만 했어요.** / The old house was a little cold, but the location was good, so it was worth living there (it was doable).

In the final example, the instructor shows that 만하다 can be conjugated into the past tense as 만 했다 to describe a past state of being "worth it" or "doable."

---

### Usage with Descriptive Verbs
When paired with descriptive verbs (adjectives), the meaning shifts slightly toward "understandable" or "natural." It suggests that a certain state or reaction is logical given the circumstances.

The instructor provides examples of this "understandable" nuance:
- **피곤할 만해요.** / It's understandable that (you) would be tired.
- **영수는 성격이 좋아서 친구가 많을만 하다고 생각해요.** / Since Young-su has a good personality, I think it's only natural that he has many friends.
- **그 집은 수영장도 있고 커서 비쌀만해요.** / That house has a pool and is big, so it's natural that it would be expensive.

---

### Comparison of Size: Noun + 만하다
A second, distinct form involves attaching 만하다 directly to a noun without a space. This version is used to indicate that something is the same size as the preceding noun. It literally means to reach the extent or level of that noun's size.

The instructor notes that this is the origin of common expressions like 이만 (this much/this amount) and 그만 (that much). This explains why 그만하세요 literally means "do just that much (and stop)."

Visual examples of size comparison include:
- **아이 얼굴이 사과만 하네요.** / The kid's face is the size of an apple.
- **제 다리는 코끼리 다리만 해요.** / My legs are the size of elephant legs.
