## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces three advanced future tense forms that go beyond the basic `-ㄹ/을 거예요` structure. These forms are `-겠다`, `-ㄹ/을게요`, and `-ㄹ/을래요`. Each carries specific nuances regarding the speaker's intention, relationship with the listener, and the context of the statement. The lesson is structured to break down each grammar point by its various usages, conjugation rules, and pragmatic differences, such as showing consideration for the listener or expressing a firm determination.

---

### The Versatile Marker 겠다

The first major form covered is `-겠다`, which can be attached to both verb and adjective stems. While `겠다` is the base form, it is almost always conjugated in the present tense as `겠어요` (polite) or `겠어` (casual), or with the formal ending as `겠습니다`. A unique grammatical constraint is that `겠다` cannot be conjugated into past or future forms itself because it already inherently contains the future meaning.

The instructor outlines five distinct usages for this marker. The first is expressing a firm will or determination. In this context, the subject must be the first person (I or we). While `겠어요` is grammatically correct, the instructor notes that `겠습니다` is much more common for expressing serious resolutions. Examples include:
> **이제부터 공부를 열심히 하겠습니다.** / From now on, I will study hard.
> **올해에는 매일 운동하겠습니다.** / I will exercise every day this year.
> **내일부터 술을 안 마시겠습니다.** / I will not drink alcohol starting from tomorrow.
> **담배를 끊겠습니다.** / I will quit smoking.

The second usage involves conveying information about something that is about to happen, typically found in formal announcements or weather forecasts. Unlike the first usage, the subject can be anything, not just "I." A common example heard on airplanes is:
> **비행기가 곧 착륙하겠습니다.** / The plane will land soon.
The instructor explains that `착륙하다 (to land)` comes from `도착 (arrival)` and `육지 (land)`.

The third usage is for guessing or making assumptions about a situation ("must be" or "it seems"). For this usage, `겠어요` is more common than `겠습니다` unless the setting is extremely formal. It can also be combined with past tense stems to guess about past events.
> **피곤하겠어요.** / You must be tired.
> **바쁘시겠어요.** / You must be busy.
> **재미있었겠어요.** / That must have been fun.

The fourth usage is as a rhetorical question to emphasize that something is obviously not true.
> **배가 고프겠어요?** / (Do you think) I would be hungry? (Implying: Of course I'm not hungry because I ate a lot.)
> **누가 안 받겠어요?** / Who wouldn't receive it? (Implying: Everyone would want to receive such a good gift.)

The fifth usage is as a polite question ending, often used by service workers to ask "Would you like to...?" or "Would you mind...?"
> **주문하시겠어요?** / Would you like to order?
> **어디 가시겠어요?** / Where would you like to go?

---

### Making Promises with ㄹ/을게요

The second form, `-ㄹ/을게요`, is used exclusively with verbs and only when the subject is "I." It cannot be used with adjectives or third-person subjects. The primary function is making a promise or informing the listener of an action the speaker is about to take while being considerate of the listener's reaction or the shared context.

When making a promise, it sounds softer and more personal than the firm `겠습니다`.
> **내일 전화할게.** / I'll call you tomorrow. (Promise)
> **이제부터 안 그럴게.** / I won't do that from now on.
> **내일은 꼭 끝낼게요.** / I will definitely finish it tomorrow.

The second function is informing someone of an immediate or near-future action. The instructor emphasizes that this form sounds "considerate" because it implies the speaker is checking in or letting the listener know their plans so the listener isn't caught off guard.
> **잠시만 화장실 좀 다녀올게요.** / I'll go to the bathroom for a second.
> **제가 설명해 드릴게요.** / I will explain it for you.
> **저 샤워 좀 할게요.** / I'm going to take a shower (letting roommates know the bathroom will be occupied).

---

### Expressing Choice and Invitation with ㄹ/을래요

The final form, `-ㄹ/을래요`, is also used only with verbs. Like `-ㄹ/을게요`, when used in a statement, the subject must be "I." However, the nuance is different: it expresses a personal choice or a decision made in a casual, everyday context. It is less "serious" than `겠습니다` and less "considerate" than `할게요`. It simply states what the speaker wants to do.

> **나는 안 갈래.** / As for me, I'm not going. (My choice)
> **그냥 포기할래요.** / I'll just give up.
> **오늘 저녁 안 먹을래요.** / I'm not going to eat dinner today.

The instructor compares `할게요` and `할래요` using the shower example. Saying `샤워 할게요` to a roommate is polite because it informs them of the bathroom's status. Saying `샤워 할래요` sounds more like a personal declaration of intent ("I'm gonna shower") and can come across as slightly more blunt or less mindful of others.

When used as a question, `-ㄹ/을래요?` functions as an invitation or a suggestion, asking "Would you like to...?" or "Do you want to...?"
> **영화 볼래?** / Do you want to watch a movie?
> **집에서 쉴래요?** / Do you want to rest at home?
> **사진 볼래요?** / Do you want to see the photo?
