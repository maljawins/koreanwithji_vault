## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces three distinct ways to express the English concept of "would have" in Korean. The lesson progresses from the most literal and grammatically straightforward method to more nuanced forms used for making assumptions, and finally to a conversational shorthand that relies heavily on context. By combining past tense markers with future tense endings, learners can describe hypothetical past situations, express strong assumptions about what must have happened, or emphasize that a past action was a certainty given specific conditions.

---

### The Literal "Would Have": ~ㅆ을 것이다

The first and most direct way to say "would have" is to combine a past tense verb stem with the standard future tense ending `~을 것이다`. To form this, you first conjugate a verb into the past tense, such as `하다 (to do)` becoming `했다 (did)`. You then remove the final `다` to get the past tense stem `했`. Because this stem ends in a consonant, you attach `~을 것이다`. In standard polite speech, this is frequently conjugated as `~었을 거예요` or `~았을 거예요`.

This form literally translates to "will have been" or "will have done," but in natural English conversation, it functions as "would have." It is a neutral, straightforward expression that can be used across all speech levels, from formal `~었을 것입니다` to casual `~었을 거야`. The instructor provides several examples to illustrate its use in hypothetical scenarios:

> **그건 아빠가 이미 했을 거예요.** / Dad would have already done that.
> **철수가 했으면 더 잘했을 거예요.** / If Cheol-su had done it, he would have done it better.
> **진작 알았으면 같이 갔을 거예요.** / I would have gone together if I'd known beforehand.
> **한 시간 전에 출발했어도 늦었을 거예요.** / Even if I left an hour earlier, I would have been late.

---

### The Assumption "Would Have": ~ㅆ겠다

The second method involves attaching the future tense marker `겠다` to a past tense verb stem. While `겠다` can indicate future intent, its most common use in non-formal speech is to express a guess or assumption, similar to "must be" or "I bet." When combined with the past tense, it creates the meaning "must have been" or "would have definitely been."

The instructor highlights a key nuance: while `~었을 것이다` sounds like a neutral prediction or a literal "would have," `~었었겠다` (often shortened to `~었겠다`) carries a stronger sense of personal conviction or reaction to a situation. It implies the speaker is making a very certain guess based on evidence. For example, if someone describes a fancy meal they ate, you would use this form to react:

> **프랑스 코스 요리요? 정말 맛있었겠네요!** / A French course meal? That must have been delicious!

Other examples of this "assumption" nuance include:
> **시험이 시작됐겠네요.** / The test must have started (I assume).
> **더 빨리 끝났겠지.** / It would have finished faster (I'm sure of it).

---

### The Conversational "Would Have": Past Tense + ~지

The final form is not an official grammar structure but a common way people speak in casual or intimate settings. It involves using the simple past tense, often ending with `~지` or `~잖아`, to express "would have." Literally, the speaker is saying "I did it" or "It was," but in the context of a hypothetical "if" statement, it functions as a strong "would have."

The instructor emphasizes that this form is strictly conversational and requires clear context to avoid being misunderstood as a simple statement of fact. For instance, saying `나도 갔지` without context just means "I went too." However, if preceded by an "if" clause, it becomes a way of saying "Of course I would have gone." It carries an emphatic nuance, suggesting the outcome was so obvious it's as if it already happened.

> **미리 말해줬으면 나도 갔지.** / If you told me in advance, (of course) I would have gone.
> **몰랐어. 냉장고에 넣어야 한다고 말해줬으면 넣었지.** / I didn't know. If you told me I had to put it in the fridge, I would have (put it in).
> **그렇게 맛있는 줄 알았으면 나도 먹었지.** / If I knew it was that good, I would have eaten it too.
