## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor addresses a common frustration for Korean learners: encountering multiple Korean words that translate to a single English word. Specifically, the video focuses on two pairs of time-related adverbs: `벌써` vs. `이미` (both meaning "already") and `지금` vs. `이제` (both meaning "now"). While these words are often interchangeable in a dictionary sense, they carry distinct pragmatic nuances that dictate when a native speaker would choose one over the other.

The lesson is divided into two parts. First, the instructor provides a quick linguistic breakdown of the differences between these specific word pairs. Second, he introduces a broader pedagogical strategy for mastering such nuances: the "Contextual Boundary" approach. This method encourages students to learn words only within the specific sentence structures and situations they have actually observed, rather than relying on broad English definitions.

---

### Already: 벌써 vs. 이미
While both words translate to "already," the choice depends on the speaker's emotional state and their prior knowledge of the situation.

The word `벌써` is primarily used when the speaker is surprised or when something happened sooner than expected. The instructor demonstrates this with an animation of a person looking shocked at a laptop. If you see someone finish a task much faster than anticipated, you would ask:
> **벌써 다 했어요?** / You finished it already? (Expressing surprise)

In contrast, `이미` is used when the speaker is already aware of the situation or is stating a fact without a sense of shock. It often implies that the state is settled and known. For example, if someone calls to ask about an event that has already concluded, a staff member would say:
> **오늘 행사는 이미 끝났습니다.** / Today's event has already finished. (Stating a known fact)

The instructor further illustrates this with the time. Saying `벌써 4시예요!` implies "Wow, it's 4 o'clock already! Time flies!" whereas `이미 4시예요` carries a nuance of "It is already 4 o'clock (and we knew this/it's too late)."

---

### Now: 지금 vs. 이제
Both words mean "now," but they differ based on whether the "now" is a static point in time or a transition from a previous state.

`지금` refers to "now" in the sense of "at this very moment" or "currently." It is a snapshot of the present. If you want to know the current time, you ask:
> **지금 몇 시예요?** / What time is it now?

`이제` refers to "now" in a transitional sense, implying a change from how things were before. It is often used when moving from one action to the next or acknowledging a new state of affairs. If it was 3 o'clock and an hour has passed, you might say:
> **이제 4시예요.** / Now (after the passage of time) it is 4 o'clock.

The instructor uses a transition from one segment of the video to the next as a real-world example, saying `이제` to signal that the introductory explanation is over and the main discussion is beginning.

---

### The Contextual Boundary Approach
The instructor argues that confidence in word choice cannot come from a single dictionary definition. Instead, it must be built through "Contextual Boundaries." When you learn a new word like `이제`, you should initially only allow yourself to use it in the exact same kind of context as the sample sentence you found.

For example, if you learn the sentence:
> **우리 이제 뭐 해요?** / What do we do now?

You can safely swap out small parts of that specific structure:
- **저 이제 뭐 해요?** / What do I do now?
- **우리 이제 뭐 먹어요?** / What do we eat now?
- **우리 이제 뭐 봐요?** / What do we watch now?

However, you should avoid using `이제` in a completely different structure, like "I am waiting for my friend now," until you have seen a native speaker use it in that specific way. By sticking to confirmed patterns, you build a "database of correct usages" in your head. This mimics how children learn; for instance, a child learns to use `추워 (cold)` for the weather and `차가워 (cold)` for cold water by observing that they are never swapped, even if both translate to "cold."
