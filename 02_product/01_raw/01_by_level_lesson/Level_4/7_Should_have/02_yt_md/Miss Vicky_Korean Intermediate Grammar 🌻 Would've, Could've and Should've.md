## Teaching Points

### Overview
This lesson covers the construction and usage of hypothetical past conditionals in Korean, specifically focusing on the equivalents of "would have," "could have," and "should have." The instructor frames these as expressions for actions or events that did not actually occur in the past but are being discussed as possibilities, regrets, or hypothetical scenarios. The lesson is divided into a theoretical teaching phase followed by an active practice session involving dictation and sentence-building scenarios.

The learning arc begins with the basic structure of combining past tense verbs with future tense endings to create the "would have" meaning. It then branches into the nuances of "could have" (possibility) and the multiple ways to express "should have" depending on whether the speaker is being objective, expressing deep regret, or scolding another person.

---

### Would Have: Hypothetical Past Assumptions
The expression "would have" refers to actions that didn't actually happen in the past. In Korean, this is constructed by combining a past tense verb with the future tense ending `-(으)ㄹ 것이다`, which is typically conjugated as `-(으)ㄹ 거예요` in polite speech (존댓말) or `-(으)ㄹ 거야` in casual speech (반말). The instructor explains that this combination makes logical sense because you are applying a future-leaning assumption to a past event.

This structure is almost always paired with an "if" clause. While the standard "if" is `-(으)면`, hypothetical past situations require the past tense "if" form: `-았/었으면` or `-았/었다면`. The instructor notes a nuance between the two: `-(으)면` is a broad term used for both real conditions (cause and effect) and imaginary ones, whereas `-다면` is specifically reserved for hypothetical or imagined situations. For example, when giving a real-life instruction like "Tell me when you are finished," `다 하면 말해` is more natural than using `-다면`. However, for a purely hypothetical situation like "If I had no family," both `가족이 없으면` and `가족이 없다면` are acceptable.

A third "if" variation introduced is `-았/었더라면`. This form naturally carries an emotional nuance of either regret or relief without needing extra words to convey those feelings. For instance, `좀 더 열심히 했더라면` (If I had worked harder) implies regret, while `구급차가 일찍 안 왔더라면` (If the ambulance hadn't come earlier) implies relief that the negative outcome didn't happen.

---

### Could Have: Possibility and Capability
The "could have" expression is a variation of the "would have" structure. To express that something was possible but didn't happen, you combine the "can" grammar `-(으)ㄹ 수 있다` with the hypothetical past ending, resulting in `-(으)ㄹ 수 있었을 거예요`. For the negative "couldn't have," you use `-(으)ㄹ 수 없었을 거예요` or `-지 못했을 거예요`.

The instructor highlights a specific nuance regarding certainty. If a speaker wants to sound absolutely certain that a result would have occurred without a doubt, they may drop the future-leaning `-(으)ㄹ 거예요` and simply use the past tense. For example, `금메달을 땄어요` instead of `땄을 거예요` conveys "I definitely would have won the gold medal."

A second usage of "could have" refers to things one was capable of doing in the past but chose not to for a specific reason. In these cases, the speaker uses the simple past tense of "can": `-(으)ㄹ 수 있었어요`. Adding the particle `도` to create `-(으)ㄹ 수도 있었어요` makes the statement sound less definitive, suggesting it was just one of many possibilities (e.g., "I could have studied, maybe, but I was sick").

---

### Should Have: Objective Reflection, Regret, and Scolding
The "should have" grammar is the most complex part of the lesson because Korean uses different structures based on the speaker's intent and emotion. The instructor identifies three main versions:

First is the objective reflection: `-았/었어야 했다 (됐다)`. This is used for neutral, objective suggestions about what should have been done. It stems from the "have to" grammar (`-아야 하다`), but both parts are put into the past tense. For "shouldn't have" in this neutral tone, the most natural form is `-지 말았어야 했어요`. Alternatively, one can use the prohibition structure `-으면 안 됐다` (e.g., `커피를 마시면 안 됐어요` for "I shouldn't have drunk coffee").

Second is the expression of regret: `-(으)ㄹ 걸 그랬다`. This is the most common form used in daily life when the speaker is reflecting on their own actions. It always implies "I regret not doing X." In casual speech, this can be shortened to just `-(으)ㄹ 걸`. For "shouldn't have" as a regret, the form is `-지 말 걸 그랬어요`.

Third is the scolding tone: `-지 그랬어요`. This is used exclusively for addressing someone else ("You should have done X"). It carries a slightly harsh or critical tone. For example, `더 공부하지 그랬어` means "You should have studied more (and I'm scolding you for it)." To make this suggestion softer and less critical, the instructor suggests using `-았/었으면 좋았을 거예요` (It would have been nice if you had...), which focuses on the alternative rather than the failure.

---

### Nuance Note: 괜히 vs. -(으)ㄹ 걸 그랬다
During the practice session, the instructor clarifies the difference between the adverb `괜히` and the "should have" regret grammar. `괜히` is used when you regret an action you *did* perform (e.g., `괜히 마셨어요` - "I shouldn't have drunk it/I regret drinking it"). In contrast, `-(으)ㄹ 걸 그랬어요` is used when you regret *not* doing something (e.g., `공부할 걸 그랬어요` - "I should have studied").
