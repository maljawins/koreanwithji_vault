**Future Tense**

Let’s learn about future tense in Korean.

한국어에는 미래에 대한 말을 하는 방법이 크게 이렇게 4가지 form이 있다.

ㄹ/을 거다 (것이다)

ㄹ/을게(요)

ㄹ/을래(요)

겠다

And in this lesson, we’re going to focus about the first three forms in detail and discuss the last one 겠다 in the next lesson.

Yes, the future tense has more variations than the present or past tense. And you might be wondering, “How come there are so many?”

**Why so many?**

That’s because 미래라는 것 자체가 본질적으로 불확실성이라는 성격을 내포하고 있고 상황에 따라 쓰이는 말도 달라지기 때문이다.

이건 한국어 뿐만 아니라 다른 언어들 또한 마찬가지이다. 영어와 비교해보자.

"I'm coming to the party" doesn't mean you are currently on the move to the party in the moment. It just means "I'll be there"

And "I'm going to come to the party" This doesn't mean you're currently going “in order” to come to the party. It just has a slightly more emphasis on the future timeline than the previous one.

"I will come to the party", however, shows a firm determination, it sounds almost like “I’m going to be there no matter what.”

And "I should come to the party" And this one can have two meanings, right?

It’s either I "HAVE TO" or “I’m pretty sure I’ll be there”. But in this case, it most likely has the second meaning.

이런 식으로 “미래”라는 것의 본질적인 불확실함으로 여러가지 상황이 생길 가능성이 있고 주어진 상황에 따라 표현하는 방식이 달라지기 때문에 여러 form들이 있는 것이다.

So, just like this, each of the three future forms we’re about to learn expresses different ideas.

And before we continue, I encourage you to revisit the lesson “TSV sentence structure #1” in level 1 and review the “rule of three” because it will be very relevant in this lesson.

Let’s get into it.

**ㄹ/을 - The Future Branch**

Display on screen

ㄹ/을 거다 (것이다)

ㄹ/을게(요)

ㄹ/을래(요)

As you can see, they all start with the same “branch” – ㄹ/을. Yes, it’s the same ㄹ/을 you saw when we learned about ㄹ/을까? – Shall we? in the lesson “suggestions & commands”

What ㄹ/을 does is it shows you’re talking about something that **hasn’t happened yet**.

It implies **possibility, uncertainty or** **future action like [something TO BE DONE]**

So, **we’ll call it "the future branch" although indicating future is not the only function it has.**

**And ㄹ and 을 are 받침-sensitive.**

And if the stem ends in 받침, you add 을. And if the stem ends in a vowel, you simply add a ㄹ 받침 to the stem. And since they all start with the same future branch, the grammar rule is consistent for all the three forms.

**How to make it (Grammar rule)**

가다 to go ends in a vowel, so you add ㄹ which then becomes 갈

갈 거예요 / 갈게요 / 갈래요

앉다 to sit, ends in a 받침, so it becomes 앉을

앉을 거예요 / 앉을게요 / 앉을래요

**What if the stem already has ㄹ받침?**

Now, what if the stem already has a ㄹ 받침 like these verbs?

Show 알다 / 팔다 / 살다

They’re called irregular verbs ㄹ or simply irregular ㄹ. By the way, we’re going to learn all about the irregular verbs really soon.

But for making a future tense, it’s actually easier because all you have to do is just add “거예요”, “게요” and “래요”.

So, 팔다 – to sell becomes 팔 거예요 / 팔게요 / 팔래요.

Pretty easy.

Now that we got the grammar out of the way, let’s talk about how to USE them.

**ㄹ/을 것이다 & ㄹ/을 거다**

Starting with the most “standard” future tense - ㄹ/을 것이다 and ㄹ/을 거다

[Show 저는 사과를 먹을 거예요 – I’m going to eat an apple]

Couple more things to note. The longer version 것이다 is almost never used. So we’re just going to stick with 거다, which is the more natural way to say this.

And also, note that 거 is pronounced like 꺼.

And 이다 always conjugates in the “present” tense because we already have our future branch ㄹ/을.

**ㄹ/을 거다 – Different meanings**

Now, ㄹ/을 거다 form could have different meanings depending on WHO or WHAT will do the action.

And this is where the rule of three comes in. The keyword to remember about this form is “willingness”. In level 1, we talked about “feelings” and just like feelings, willingness is something you only have control over your own and not anyone else’s.

Therefore, when it’s “I” or “We” who will do the action, it simply indicates future action and nothing more, no additional nuance attached.

For example, “저희는 한국에 갈 거예요” means “We will to go to Korea.”

What about when it’s “You”? Well, in English, you CAN say “You WILL go to Korea” It’s an odd thing to say like “I decided that for you. You WILL go to Korea. And I’m not taking no for an answer.” but still it’s not technically an incorrect sentence.

However, in Korean, the rule of three doesn’t allow that. **I** can only ask **YOU** “한국에 갈 거예요?” – “Will you go to Korea?” or “Are you going to Korea?” more naturally.

Now, what about for a 3rd person?

You would think this form doesn’t work for a 3rd person, but to your surprise, you CAN make a statement, but not a question – I’ll get to that shortly. And when this happens however, it indicates “guess” not “willingness” You could say it sort of works like “should” or “must” in English.

And also, since it’s no longer about “willingness to do something”, this time you can use descriptive verbs as well.

지혜가 알 거야 means “Jihye SHOULD know” or “Jihye must know.”

거기가 아닐 거예요 means “that place SHOULDN’T be it” or more naturally, “I don’t think that’s the right place.”

Now why did I say it doesn’t work as a question? Because you have to change the form to ㄹ/을까(요)?

That’s right. The same form we learned in the lesson “suggestions & commands”

So, “지혜가 알까요?” means “Would Jihye know?” Or “Do you think she knows?” in natural English.

And “거기가 아닐까요?” means “Wouldn’t that be the place?” or “Don’t you think that’s the place?”

Let’s look at how this form conjugates in different speech levels.

**을 것이다 Example 1**

가 ends in a vowel, so you should add ㄹ 거 after it and conjugate 이다.

So it’s 갈 거야 in 반말, informal low, 갈 거예요 in informal high, 갈 겁니다 in formal high.

Repeat after me. 나는 파티에 갈 거야 / 저는 파티에 갈 거예요 / 저는 파티에 갈 겁니다.

**을 것이다 Example 2**

Let’s make it into an honorific.

Honorific suffix always comes first after the stem so 시 is added after the stem.

So, it becomes 가실 거야 가실 거예요 and 가실 겁니다

**을 것이다 Example 3**

Let’s make a negative sentence with the verb “앉다 to sit” using the 지 않다 form.

Like we learned, all you have to do is simply add 지 않 after the stem so it becomes 앉지 않

Then we add 을 거 to make a future tense.

And then we conjugate 이다.

So, I won’t sit in Korean is 앉지 않을 거야 거예요 and 겁니다

**을 것이다 Example 4**

Let’s try and make this into an honorific then.

We take “앉지 않다” and change it to “앉지 않으시다”

And then we add ㄹ 거다 since 시 ends in a vowel.

So this becomes 앉지 않으실 거야 / 거예요 / 겁니다

**ㄹ/을게요- 01**

Now let’s move on to ㄹ/을게요

This is called promise form. And it’s used to inform the listener about your promise.

So, you can use this form to say “you’ll do something as a favor”, sort of, even when it’s not necessarily for the other’s sake, perse. It could be something really trivial like “I’ll clean the toilet” or “I’ll do the dishes”.

It has a tone like that kindly informs someone about your decision. And the closest thing in English would be “Let me”

There are a few constraints when it comes to this form.

One. And you can’t use the formal speech with this form because 게(요) is a context marker in and of itself and if you remember, the formal speech ending ㅂ/습니다 is also a context marker. And you can only use one context marker in a sentence.

Two. This form can only be in a statement because making a promise is, by nature, a statement.

Three, the rule of three applies here again because you can’t make promises on someone else’s behalf which means the subject can only be I or we.

And lastly, you can only use action verbs in this form, not descriptive verbs. You can only promise to “do something” and not “to be a certain way” Because action is something that’s observable by the other person but “BEING a certain way” is subjective by nature. And you can’t promise something that’s deemed subjective. The listener must be able to objectively tell if you are or have delivered on your promise.

So to say something like “I’ll be pretty” using 게요, you actually have to say “I’ll BECOME pretty” in Korean which we’re going to learn in level 3.

**ㄹ/을게요 – Example**

예시 파트 Draft 보강 필요

In 운동하다 – to exercise, 하 doesn’t have 받침. So it becomes 운동할게요.

And 놓 in 놓다 – to put ends in a 받침. So it becomes 놓을게요

**ㄹ/을래요 – 01**

Next, we have 을래요 form. This one has a much more affirmative and unapologetic tone than 게요 like “I’m doing it.” As in, and “I’m doing it for myself not for anyone else.”

And the keyword to remember about this form is “intention.”

For example, 오늘은 집에 있을래 means “I’m staying home today.”

I’m not asking for your permission nor am I making any promise. I’m simply letting you know.

A few rules about this form.

First, the rule of three applies again since it’s about “intention”. You obviously don’t know about anyone else’s intention but your own. So, as a statement, it can only be about “me - 나 or 저.” However, it is not compatible with “We – 우리 or 저희”. Something very notable because the rule of three allows “we” in most cases. And I think this has something to do with the assertive nature in the tone, it’s rather individualistic by nature, if you will.

Third, you can ASK about someone’s intention. So, it works a question.

Fourth, it doesn’t work with a 3rd person because the rule of three tells us so.

Fifth. It only works with action verbs. Same logic as before. Intention is all about “doing”, not “being”.

And lastly, this form is also a context marker as a whole, so it can’t have formal speech.

**ㄹ/을래요 – Example**

예시 문장 보강 필요

**So “**나가다 – To go out, To leave” ends in a vowel, so it becomes 나갈래요.

여기서 나갈래요 means I’m getting out of here.

And 있다 – to be or “to stay” in this context, ends in a 받침. So it becomes 있을래요

여기 있을래요 means “I’m staying here.”

**How to express the idea of ㄹ/을게요 & ㄹ/을래요 FORMALLY.**

We just established 을게요 and 을래요 can’t have formal high speech. But that doesn’t mean we don’t have a way to express these ideas in formal speech level.

**To formally make “a promise”, you can use “겠습니다”. So this is equivalent to ㄹ/을게요.**

We’re just about to learn all about 겠 in the next lesson. So for now, just know that the tone matches.

You’ll hear this expression a lot “잘 먹겠습니다”.

Literally, this means “I’ll eat well” but it’s something Koreans say instead of “Bon Appétit”. “잘 먹을게요” also works but it feels slightly less polite in a sense. But both phrases express pretty much the same idea.

**And to express a similar vibe of “할래요”, you can say “할 겁니다” instead.** It sounds a bit more deterministic and affirmative than ㄹ/을 거예요.
