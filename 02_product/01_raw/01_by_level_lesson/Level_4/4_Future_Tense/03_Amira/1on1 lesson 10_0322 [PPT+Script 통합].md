## Lesson Overview

This lesson focuses on the complexities of the Korean future tense and the mechanics of verb negation. Ji introduces the concept that the future is inherently uncertain, which is why Korean employs multiple endings to convey different nuances depending on the speaker's intent, the context, and the relationship with the listener. The lesson covers four primary future tense endings: ㄹ 거다, ㄹ게요, ㄹ래요, and 겠다, explaining how each shifts the meaning from a standard prediction to a promise or a statement of intention.

In addition to the future tense, the lesson provides a comprehensive guide to verb negation using 지 않다 and 안. Ji explains the structural differences between these two forms and provides rules for distinguishing between Native Korean and Sino-Korean verbs, which dictates which negation form is most appropriate. The session also includes a detailed review of previous student sentences, correcting common errors in honorifics, particle usage, and spelling.

Throughout the lesson, Ji emphasizes "feeling the language" rather than just memorizing rules. By using metaphors like a tree (where the stem is the base, the branch is the tense, the leaf is the context, and the fruit is the respect level), Ji helps the student visualize how Korean verbs are built. The lesson concludes with a deep dive into the special cases of 알다 (to know) and 모르다 (to not know), showing how the addition of 겠 changes a blunt statement into a respectful, thoughtful response.

    - Correction: 요리하셨어요
    - Breakdown: 요리하 (stem) + 시 (honorific) + 었 (past) + 어 (plain context) + 요 (respect).
- **Ji's teaching:** In the sentence "Grandma cooked the meat in the kitchen in the morning," the verb was conjugated incorrectly. It should be 요리하셨어요. Ji breaks it down: 요리하 is the stem, 시 is the honorific suffix, 었 is the past tense suffix, and 어 is the plain context marker followed by the polite 요.

### Slide 4 — Sentence Review: Spelling
- **On screen:** 
    - Original: 친구가 침실에서 공보했어
    - Correction: 공부했어 (highlighted '부' vs '보').
- **Ji's teaching:** This was a simple spelling error. The word for "study" is 공부, so it should be 공부했어, not 공보.

### Slide 5 — Sentence Review: Location Particles
- **On screen:** 
    - Original: 고양이의 우유는 주방에서 바닥에 있었어
    - Correction: 고양이의 우유는 주방 바닥에 있었어
    - Rule: 에서 = Where something happens; 에 = Where something exists.
    - Note: 에 is added to the final noun only.
- **Ji's teaching:** There are two errors here. First, 에서 is used for where an action happens, but since the milk was just existing on the floor, you should use 에. Second, remember that the location marker should only be added to the final noun in a compound location. So, instead of "kitchen-at floor-at," it should be "kitchen floor-at" (주방 바닥에).

### Slide 6 — Sentence Review: 사다 vs 이다
- **On screen:** 
    - Original: 엄마가 가게에서 옷을 사싰입니다
    - Correction: 사셨습니다
    - Rule: 이다 → 이 + ㅂ니다 = 입니다.
    - Rule: Previous syllable ends in 받침 → 습니다; ends in vowel → ㅂ니다.
- **Ji's teaching:** Ji notes a confusion between the verb 사다 (to buy) and 입니다 (the formal high of 이다, "to be"). You shouldn't mix them. For 사다 in the past honorific formal high: 사 (stem) + 시 (honorific) + 었 (past). Because 었 ends in a ㅆ 받침, it must be followed by 습니다, resulting in 사셨습니다.

### Slide 7 — Sentence Review: Traveling Egypt
- **On screen:** 
    - Original: 언니가 지난주 이집트에서 영행하셨습니다.
    - Correction: 언니가 지난주에 이집트를 여행했습니다.
    - Note: It's more natural to use the object marker 를 for 여행하다. Honorifics for a sister can be "a bit much."
- **Ji's teaching:** First, a spelling fix: 여행하다, not 영행하다. Second, while the honorific 하셨습니다 is grammatically correct, using it for your sister is often too much unless she is much older; usually, you are close enough to skip it. Third, while 에서 is grammatically okay for location, the object marker 를 sounds more natural with 여행하다. In Korean, this verb feels like "to experience," as in "she experienced Egypt by traveling there." You have to "feel the vibe" for this one.

### Slide 8 — Sentence Review: Giving a Book
- **On screen:** 
    - Original: 저는 오늘 사무실에서 친구한테 책을 조ㅓ요
    - Correction: 줘요 (Present) or 줬어요 (Past).
    - Rule: 주 + 어요 = 줘요.
- **Ji's teaching:** The compound vowel ㅗㅓ doesn't exist. Since the stem 주 ends in ㅜ, it must be followed by 어, which combines to make the 줘 sound. You can say 줘요 for "I give" or 줬어요 for "I gave."

### Slide 9 — Sentence Review: Subject vs. Object
- **On screen:** 
    - Original: 어제 집에서 친구가 만났어 (My friend met [someone]...)
    - Correction: 어제 집에서 친구를 만났어 (I met a friend...)
    - Visual: Highlights 가 (Subject) vs 를 (Object).
- **Ji's teaching:** The original sentence is grammatically correct but likely doesn't mean what you intended. 친구가 만났어 means "The friend met [someone]," but it doesn't say who. If you meant "I met my friend," you must use the object marker 를 to mark the friend as the person being met.

### Slide 10 — Sentence Review: Honorifics with Family
- **On screen:** 
    - Original: 언니가 도서관에서 책을 가방에 넣으셨습니다
    - Correction: 언니가 도서관에서 책을 가방에 넣었습니다
    - Note: Using honorifics for a sister of similar age can make her feel "really old."
- **Ji's teaching:** Again, using the honorific 시 with 언니 is usually unnecessary for a close family member of similar age. If your sister heard you say this, she might feel old!

### Slide 11 — Verb Negation: Two Ways
- **On screen:** 
    - Stem + 지 않다 / 안 + verb.
    - 지 않다: More formal, used with any verb.
    - 안 + V: Less formal, used with Native verbs and 3-syllable Sino-Korean verbs.
    - Example: 변하다 (Sino) → 안 변했어 (O); 공부하다 (Sino) → 안 공부해요 (X) → 공부하지 않아요 (O) or 공부 안 해요 (O).
- **Ji's teaching:** There are two ways to say "don't," "doesn't," or "didn't." 지 않다 is slightly more formal and very easy because it works with every verb. 안 is less formal but has more restrictions based on the type of verb.

### Slide 12 — Native vs. Sino-Korean Verbs
- **On screen:** 
    - Over 50% of words are Sino-Korean (한자).
    - Native: 2-syllable verbs (가다, 먹다), non-하다 verbs (배우다, 가르치다), and some specific 하다 verbs (좋아하다, 사랑하다).
    - Sino: Most 하다 verbs.
    - Rule: You CAN use 안 with 1-syllable + 하다 Sino verbs (취하다, 변하다).
- **Ji's teaching:** To use negation correctly, you need to distinguish between Native and Sino-Korean words. Most 2-syllable verbs and non-하다 verbs are Native. While most Sino-Korean verbs end in 하다, some common 하다 verbs like "to love" or "to like" are actually Native. Ji recommends using a government-provided dictionary to check for Chinese characters (한자) if unsure.

### Slide 13 — Using 지 않다
- **On screen:** 
    - Stem + 지 않다.
    - Vowel or 받침 doesn't matter.
    - Examples: 하지 않다, 가지 않다, 앉지 않다, 믿지 않다.
    - Rule: Just conjugate 않다 according to standard rules.
- **Ji's teaching:** This form is simple. You don't change the stem at all. Just attach 지 않다 and then conjugate the 않다 part. It's more formal than 안 and universal.

### Slide 14 — Example: 가지 않다 (Present Informal)
- **On screen:** 
    - 가 + 지 않 + 아 = 가지 않아(요).
    - Visual: Tree diagram. Stem: 가+지 않; Branch: Present Tense (empty); Leaf: 아 (Plain context); Fruit: 요 (Respect).
    - Sentence: 저는 클럽에 가지 않아(요) (I don't go to clubs).
- **Ji's teaching:** To say "I don't go," take the stem 가, add 지 않, and then add 아 because 않 ends in a ㅏ vowel. If the "tense" position on the branch is empty, it's present tense.

### Slide 15 — Example: 먹지 않다 (Formal High)
- **On screen:** 
    - 먹 + 지 않 + 습니다 = 먹지 않습니다.
    - Visual: Tree diagram. Stem: 먹+지 않; Branch: Tense (empty); Leaf to Fruit: 습니다 (Formal high).
    - Sentence: 제 여자친구는 해산물을 먹지 않습니다 (My girlfriend doesn't eat seafood).
- **Ji's teaching:** For formal high, since 않 ends in a 받침, we add 습니다.

### Slide 16 — Example: 믿지 않다 (Past Informal)
- **On screen:** 
    - 않 + 았 + 어(요) = 않았어(요).
    - Visual: Tree diagram. Stem: 믿지 않; Branch: 았 (Past Tense); Leaf: 어 (Plain context); Fruit: 요 (Respect).
    - Sentence: 처음에는 걔는 저를 믿지 않았어(요) (At first, he/she didn't trust me).
- **Ji's teaching:** To say "didn't," conjugate 않다 into the past tense: 않았어요. Ji notes that the 는 after 처음에 (at first) implies a change—they didn't trust me then, but they do now.

### Slide 17 — Example: 믿지 않다 (Past Honorific Formal)
- **On screen:** 
    - 믿지 않 + 으시 + 었 + 습니다 = 믿지 않으셨습니다.
    - Visual: Tree diagram. Stem: 믿지 않; Branch 1: 으시 (Honorific); Branch 2: 었 (Past Tense); Leaf to Fruit: 습니다 (Formal high).
    - Sentence: 사장님은 종교를 믿지 않으셨습니다 (The owner didn't believe in religion/didn't have a religion).
- **Ji's teaching:** Here we combine everything. Honorific 으시 comes first, then past tense 었, then formal 습니다. This results in 믿지 않으셨습니다.

### Slide 18 — Using 안 + Verb
- **On screen:** 
    - Add 안 before the stem.
    - Rule: Can be used with pure Korean verbs & 3-syllable Sino-Korean verbs.
    - Splitting: For Sino-하다 verbs, put 안 before 하다 (공부 안 했어요).
- **Ji's teaching:** 안 is very easy—just put it before the verb. However, for Sino-Korean 하다 verbs with more than 3 syllables, you must split them. You can't say 안 공부했어요; you must say 공부 안 했어요 (Study [object] + not + did).

### Slide 19 — Example: 살다 (To Live)
- **On screen:** 
    - 안 + 살 + 아(요) = 안 살아(요).
    - Sentence: 저는 아파트에 안 살아(요) (I don't live in an apartment).
    - Note: Both 에 and 에서 can be used for 살다.
- **Ji's teaching:** 살다 is a pure Korean verb. Ji explains that while 에 is usually for stationary existence (like "to be at"), living is both stationary and an action, so both 에 and 에서 are correct. 에서 just puts slightly more emphasis on the location.

### Slide 20 — Example: 도착하다 (To Arrive)
- **On screen:** 
    - 도착 + 안 + 했어요 = 도착 안 했어요.
    - Sentence: 비행기가 아직 도착 안 했어요 (The airplane didn't arrive yet).
- **Ji's teaching:** 도착하다 is a Sino-Korean verb. To negate it with 안, you place it right before the 하다 part.

### Slide 21 — Intro to Future Tense
- **On screen:** 
    - Several forms exist; meanings depend on context.
    - 4 common endings: 1. ㄹ/을 것이다 (ㄹ/을 거다), 2. ㄹ/을게(요), 3. ㄹ/을래(요), 4. 겠다.
- **Ji's teaching:** Ji warns that the future tense is the most difficult because of the many endings. Each one carries a different nuance.

### Slide 22 — Why so many endings?
- **On screen:** 
    - Comparison to English: "I'm coming," "I'm going to come," "I will come," "I should come."
    - Future tense is inherently uncertain, so language changes based on the situation.
- **Ji's teaching:** Just like in English, where "I'm coming" vs. "I will come" conveys different levels of intent or timing, Korean uses different endings to handle the "uncertainty" of the future.

### Slide 23 — The Standard Future: ㄹ 것이다 / 거다
- **On screen:** 
    - ㄹ 받침 signifies something incomplete ("something to ___").
    - 것이다 is shortened to 거다 (standard for conversation).
    - 거 is pronounced 꺼.
    - 먹을 것이다 → 먹을 거다 → 먹을 거예요.
- **Ji's teaching:** This is the most standard future tense. The ㄹ 받침 itself indicates the future, so you never conjugate the 이다 part into the future separately. In conversation, we almost always use the shortened 거다. Note the pronunciation: 거 sounds like 꺼.

### Slide 24 — Nuances of ㄹ 거다
- **On screen:** 
    - 1. Simple future: "I will eat an apple."
    - 2. [To be something to do]: "This is something to eat."
    - 3. [Should] - Prediction: "Jihye should know" (Pretty sure).
- **Ji's teaching:** Depending on the context and particles, this ending can mean "I will do it," "It is a thing to be done," or a prediction like "It should be..." For example, 사과를 먹을 거예요 is "I will eat the apple," but 이것은 먹을 거예요 is "This is something to eat."

### Slide 25 — Example: 가다 (Standard Future)
- **On screen:** 
    - 가 + ㄹ거 + 야 = 갈 거야 (Informal Low).
    - 가 + ㄹ거 + 예요 = 갈 거예요 (Informal High).
    - 가 + ㄹ거 + ㅂ니다 = 갈 겁니다 (Formal High).
- **Ji's teaching:** Since 가 doesn't have a 받침, we add ㄹ 거. Ji has the student repeat the sentences for each speech level.

### Slide 26 — Example: 가다 (Honorific Future)
- **On screen:** 
    - 가 + 시 + ㄹ거 + 예요 = 가실 거예요.
    - Sentence: 아빠가 집에 가실 거야 (Dad will go home).
    - Note: 가 makes it sound like "Don't worry, dad will be there."
- **Ji's teaching:** The honorific 시 comes immediately after the stem.

### Slide 27 — Example: 앉다 (Negative Future)
- **On screen:** 
    - 앉지 않 + 을거 + 예요 = 앉지 않을 거예요.
    - Sentence: 나는 소파에 앉지 않을 거야 (I will not sit on the sofa).
- **Ji's teaching:** To combine negation and future, we use the 지 않 form, then add 을 거 (because 않 has a 받침), then conjugate 이다.

### Slide 28 — Example: 앉다 (Honorific Negative Future)
- **On screen:** 
    - 앉 + 지 않 + 으시 + 을 거 + 예요 = 앉지 않으실 거예요.
- **Ji's teaching:** The order is: Stem + Negation + Honorific + Future + Respect.

### Slide 29 — Irregular ㄹ Verbs
- **On screen:** 
    - Verbs like 알다, 살다, 팔다.
    - When ending starts with ㄴ, ㅂ, ㅅ, ㄹ: the stem's ㄹ is removed.
    - 알 + ㄹ 거예요 = 알 거예요.
    - When ending starts with 으: the 으 is removed.
    - 알 + 으시 + ㄹ 거예요 = 아실 거예요.
- **Ji's teaching:** If a stem already ends in ㄹ, it's an "irregular ㄹ." When adding the future ㄹ, the original one drops out and the new one takes over. Similarly, the 으 in honorific 으시 drops out, becoming just 시.

### Slide 30-31 — Example: 팔다 (To Sell)
- **On screen:** 
    - 팔 + ㄹ거 + 예요 = 팔 거예요.
    - 팔 + 으시 + ㄹ거 + 예요 = 파실 거예요.
- **Ji's teaching:** Ji demonstrates the irregular change. For the honorific, "Grandma will sell Kimchi" becomes 할머니가... 파실 거예요.

### Slide 32 — Future Tense: ㄹ게요 (Promise)
- **On screen:** 
    - Subject is ALWAYS 1st person (I or We).
    - Used only with Action verbs and Declarative sentences.
    - No Formal High (use 겠습니다 instead).
    - Pronounced 께.
- **Ji's teaching:** This is the "Promise" form. You are informing the listener of a promise or a favor. You can't promise for someone else, so the subject must be "I" or "we." It doesn't work for questions or descriptive verbs.

### Slide 33 — Usages of ㄹ게요
- **On screen:** 
    - 1) To make a promise/favor: "I will exercise from today."
    - 2) To kindly inform of a decision ("Let me"): "I will explain."
- **Ji's teaching:** It often sounds like "Let me" in English. It's a way of kindly letting the other person know what you've decided to do for their sake or as a commitment.

### Slide 34-35 — Examples: 운동하다 & 놓다
- **On screen:** 
    - 운동할게요 (I will exercise).
    - 놓을게요 (I will put/leave [the money] here).
- **Ji's teaching:** For stems with a 받침 like 놓, use 을게요. For those without, like 운동하, use ㄹ게요.

### Slide 36 — Future Tense: ㄹ래요 (Intention)
- **On screen:** 
    - Subject is "I" or "You."
    - CAN be used for questions: "Do you want to ___?"
    - No 3rd person subjects.
    - Action verbs only.
- **Ji's teaching:** This form is about intention. In a statement, it's "I intend to." In a question, it's "Do you intend to?" or "Do you want to?"

### Slide 37 — Nuance of ㄹ래요
- **On screen:** 
    - Slightly more affirmative than ㄹ게요.
    - Sounds like "I'm doing it" but less aggressive.
    - Example: 오늘은 집에 있을래 (I'm staying home today).
- **Ji's teaching:** Ji explains that 뭐 할래? can mean "What are you going to do?" or "Do you want to do something?" depending on the intonation.

### Slide 38-39 — Examples: 나가다 & 있다
- **On screen:** 
    - 나갈래요? (Do you want to get out of here?)
    - 있을래요 (I'm going to stay here).
- **Ji's teaching:** Ji shows how these are used in a dialogue where one person wants to leave and the other intends to stay.

### Slide 40-41 — Future Tense Comparisons
- **On screen:** 
    - Table comparing ㄹ게 (Considerate/Promise) vs ㄹ래 (Affirmative/Intention).
    - Full comparison table including standard ㄹ 거다.
- **Ji's teaching:** Ji provides these tables for review. Note that ㄹ게요 and ㄹ래요 don't have formal high versions; instead, they use 겠습니다 or 할 겁니다 to convey similar tones.

### Slide 42 — The Conditional: 겠다
- **On screen:** 
    - Treated like its own verb.
    - Conjugated ONLY in present tense.
    - Used "like" a future tense when talking about the future in the present moment.
    - 4 usages: 1) Willingness, 2) Formal Notification, 3) Conjecture, 4) Question.
- **Ji's teaching:** 겠다 is technically a "conditional," not a future tense, but it functions like one in specific situations.

### Slide 43-44 — 겠다 Usage #1: Willingness
- **On screen:** 
    - Tone of determination.
    - 1st person subjects + Action verbs.
    - 겠습니다 is equivalent to ㄹ게요 but more formal.
    - Sentence: 제가 하겠습니다 (I will do it).
- **Ji's teaching:** This shows firm determination. Expressing your willingness is like making a promise to yourself and others.

### Slide 45-46 — Examples: 끊다 & 처리하다
- **On screen:** 
    - 내일부터 술을 끊겠어(요) (I will quit drinking from tomorrow).
    - 그 업무는 제가 처리하겠습니다 (I will take care of that task).
- **Ji's teaching:** Ji encourages memorizing 겠습니다 as a whole because it is extremely common in formal work situations.

### Slide 47-48 — 겠다 Usage #2: Formal Notification
- **On screen:** 
    - Used on news/weather forecasts.
    - Often 3rd person subjects.
    - Sentence: 열차가 5분 후에 서울역에 도착하겠습니다 (The train will arrive at Seoul station in 5 minutes).
    - Sentence: 비가 오겠습니다 (It will rain).
- **Ji's teaching:** This is the "announcer voice." Since Korean doesn't have a word for "it" in "it rains," we say "the rain comes" (비가 오다).

### Slide 49-51 — 겠다 Usage #3: Conjecture
- **On screen:** 
    - "I guess," "must," "might."
    - Subjects can be anyone BUT me.
    - Sentence: 피곤하시겠어요 (You must be tired).
    - Sentence: 정말 귀엽겠어요 (He/She must be so cute).
    - Sentence: 귀신 나오겠어요 (A ghost might show up).
- **Ji's teaching:** You use this to guess about others. It makes no sense to guess about yourself! In the ghost example, it's used jokingly to say "it looks like a ghost might appear here."

### Slide 52-53 — Past Stem + 겠다 ("Must have")
- **On screen:** 
    - Past stem + 았/었 + 겠다 = "Must have."
    - Sentence: 날씨가 추웠겠어요 (The weather must have been cold).
    - Sentence: 재미있었겠어요 (It must have been fun).
- **Ji's teaching:** To say someone "must have" experienced something, you use the past tense stem before 겠다.

### Slide 54-55 — 겠다 Usage #4: Questions
- **On screen:** 
    - 1) To confirm (Formal): 정말 괜찮으시겠어요? (Are you sure you're going to be okay?)
    - 2) Rhetorical: 전화를 받겠어? (Would he answer the call? [Why would he?])
- **Ji's teaching:** Ji points out that 상금을 받으시겠습니까? (Would you like to receive the prize money?) is a famous line from *Squid Game*.

### Slide 56-57 — 겠다 Summary Tables
- **On screen:** 
    - Declarative and Inquisitive usage tables.
- **Ji's teaching:** Ji provides these for the student to review the subjects and speech levels compatible with each usage.

### Slide 58-59 — Special Case: 알다 vs 알겠어요
- **On screen:** 
    - 알아요: I certainly know (facts).
    - 알겠어요: I think I know now / I understand (new info/respectful).
    - Sentence: 이제 알겠어요 (Yes, now I understand).
    - Sentence: 알겠습니다 (I got it/I understand - respectful reply to command).
- **Ji's teaching:** Adding 겠 implies you've thought about it. 알아요 is for things you just know (like a singer's name). 알겠어요 is for new information or concepts you've just processed. It is also much more respectful when replying to a teacher or boss.

### Slide 60-61 — Special Case: 몰라요 vs 모르겠어요

## Teaching Points

- **Verb Negation:**
    - **지 않다:** The universal, more formal negation. Attach to the stem and conjugate 않다.
    - **안:** The informal negation. Placed before the verb. For Sino-Korean 하다 verbs, it must be split (Noun + 안 + 하다).
    - **Native vs. Sino:** Native verbs are usually 2 syllables or non-하다. Sino verbs are usually 하다. Use a dictionary to check for 한자 (Chinese characters) to be certain.

- **Future Tense Nuances:**
    - **ㄹ 것이다 / 거다:** The standard future. Used for simple predictions or facts.
    - **ㄹ게요:** The "Promise" form. Used only by the 1st person to inform the listener of a commitment or favor.
    - **ㄹ래요:** The "Intention" form. Used for "I want to/intend to" or "Do you want to?" (1st and 2nd person only).
    - **겠다:** The "Conditional." Used for determination (Willingness), formal announcements (Notification), guessing about others (Conjecture), or formal confirmation (Questions).

- **The "Thoughtful" 겠:**
    - Adding 겠 to 알다 (to know) or 모르다 (to not know) signals that the speaker is thinking before answering. This makes the response more respectful and less blunt. 알겠어요 is "I understand/I've processed that," while 모르겠어요 is "I don't think I know/I'm not sure."

- **Honorific Order:**
    - When building complex verbs, the honorific suffix (시/으시) always comes immediately after the stem, before tense markers or negation suffixes.
