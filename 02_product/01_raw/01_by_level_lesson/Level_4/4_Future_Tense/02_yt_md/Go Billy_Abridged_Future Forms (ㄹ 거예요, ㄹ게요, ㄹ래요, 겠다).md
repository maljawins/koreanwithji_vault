## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the concept of expressing the future in Korean, noting that while there are several specific future tense forms, the present tense is often used to discuss upcoming events. For example, saying `내일 가요 (I go tomorrow)` is a perfectly natural way to say "I will go tomorrow." However, using only the present tense can be ambiguous, as it might imply a general action rather than a specific future commitment. To provide clarity and convey specific nuances like intention, formality, or promises, Korean utilizes four primary future forms: `ㄹ 거예요`, `겠다`, `ㄹ게요`, and `ㄹ래요`.

The lesson focuses on the conjugation rules, pronunciation shifts, and pragmatic differences between these four forms. The instructor emphasizes that choosing the right form depends on who the speaker is talking to, whether they are making a promise to someone else, or simply stating a plan. Visual aids on the whiteboard demonstrate how verb stems transform based on whether they end in a vowel or a consonant, as well as how to handle irregular verbs.

---

### The Standard Future: ㄹ 거예요
The most common and versatile future tense is derived from the form `거시다` (or `것이다`). To conjugate this, the speaker takes a verb stem and attaches `을` if it ends in a consonant, or the letter `ㄹ` if it ends in a vowel. In standard polite speech (요 form), this becomes `ㄹ 거예요`. For example, the verb `가다 (to go)` becomes `갈 거예요`, and `먹다 (to eat)` becomes `먹을 거예요`. The instructor notes that while written as `예요`, it is universally pronounced as `거예요` (with a double 'ㄲ' sound).

Specific irregular rules apply to this form. If a verb stem ends in `ㄹ`, `비읍 (ㅂ)`, or `ㅎ`, these letters are often removed or transformed before adding the ending. For instance, `살다 (to live)` becomes `살 거예요` (the original `ㄹ` is dropped and the suffix `ㄹ` is added back). For descriptive verbs ending in `ㅂ` like `덥다 (to be hot)`, the `ㅂ` is removed and replaced with `우`, resulting in `더울 거예요`. To make these sentences negative, one can simply place `안` before the verb or use the `지 않다` ending, such as `안 놀릴 거예요` or `놀리지 않을 거예요 (I will not make fun of [someone])`.

---

### Formality and Assumptions: 겠다
The form `겠다` is another way to express the future, but it carries a slightly more formal tone than `ㄹ 거예요`. It is frequently used in professional settings, such as when speaking to a boss or a teacher. Conjugation is straightforward: simply attach `겠다` to any verb stem regardless of whether it ends in a vowel or a consonant. For example, `하다 (to do)` becomes `하겠어요` or the more formal `하겠습니다`. A common set phrase using this form is `잘 먹겠습니다 (I will eat well)`, said before starting a meal.

Beyond just "will," `겠다` has a unique usage with descriptive verbs to mean "must be" or "looks like." When the instructor sees someone eating something delicious, he might say `맛있겠네요!`, which translates to "That must be delicious!" or "That looks good!" rather than "It will be delicious." This can even be used in the past tense to make assumptions about past events, such as `맛있었겠네요 (That must have been delicious)`. While `ㄹ 거예요` emphasizes the subject (who is doing it), `겠다` places more emphasis on the action itself.

---

### Promises and Favors: ㄹ게요
The form `ㄹ게요` is used specifically when the speaker is doing something for the benefit of the listener or as a response to a situation. It functions like a promise or a helpful "I'll do it." The conjugation follows the same vowel/consonant rules as `ㄹ 거예요`: `할게요 (I'll do it)`, `먹을게요 (I'll eat it)`. Crucially, this form can only be used for the first person (I/we). You cannot use it to describe what a third party will do.

The instructor explains that `ㄹ게요` often implies a favor. If a mother tells her child to do homework, the child might say `할게요` to signal "I'll do it for you, Mom." This form is frequently paired with the helper verb `주다 (to give/do for someone)` to emphasize the favor aspect. For example, `제가 해 줄게요` means "I will do it for you (as a favor)." It is a very social ending that acknowledges the relationship between the speaker and the listener.

---

### Intentions and Suggestions: ㄹ래요
The final form, `ㄹ래요`, expresses a strong intention or a desire to do something. It is translated as "I intend to" or "I'm gonna." Like `ㄹ게요`, it is generally restricted to the first person in statements and the second person in questions. It cannot be used for third parties or inanimate objects like the weather. For example, `갈래요` means "I intend to go."

In conversation, `ㄹ래요?` is the standard way to say "Wanna...?" or "Do you want to...?" It is more active than the `고 싶다` (want to) form. While `가고 싶다` expresses a vague desire (e.g., "I want to go to Korea but I have no money"), `갈래요` implies a concrete intention (e.g., "I want to go and I'm planning on it"). It is also used to suggest activities, such as `같이 밥 먹을래? (Wanna eat together?)`. The instructor cautions that this form is casual and should be used with friends or people you are close to, rather than in highly formal situations.
