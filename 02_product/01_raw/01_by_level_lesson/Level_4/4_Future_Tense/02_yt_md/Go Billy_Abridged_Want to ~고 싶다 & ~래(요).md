## Teaching Points

### Overview
This lesson covers two primary ways to express "want to" in Korean: the basic form ~고 싶다 and the more intentional, conversational form ~(으)ㄹ래(요). While both can be translated as "want to" in English, they carry distinct nuances regarding the speaker's certainty, the social setting, and the grammatical person being discussed.

The instructor begins by establishing that these forms are used with action verbs, not descriptive verbs (adjectives). The lesson moves from the structural mechanics of conjugation to the pragmatic differences between simply having a desire (~고 싶다) and expressing a firm intention to act on that desire (~(으)ㄹ래(요)).

---

### The Basic "Want To": ~고 싶다
The most fundamental way to say you want to do something is by taking an action verb stem and attaching 고 싶다. The word 싶다 is then conjugated like a regular descriptive verb to match the desired politeness level, such as 싶어요 (polite) or 싶습니다 (formal).

The instructor provides several common examples of this construction. The verb 보다 (to see) becomes 보고 싶다, which literally means "want to see" but is the standard way to say "I miss you" in Korean. It is also used for wanting to watch media, as in 영화를 보고 싶어요 (I want to see a movie). The verb 가지다 (to have/possess) becomes 가지고 싶다, often shortened to 갖고 싶다. However, the instructor notes that the choice of verb depends on the context of "having." For instance, if you want to "have" a pet, you use the verb 키우다 (to raise), resulting in 고양이 키우고 싶어요 (I want to have/raise a cat). If you want a cheeseburger, you would likely use 먹다 (to eat) or 사다 (to buy) instead of "possess."

A key characteristic of ~고 싶다 is that it expresses a pure desire regardless of possibility or intention. You can say you want to do something even if it is impossible or if you have no actual plan to do it. For example, one might say 한국말 잘 하고 싶어요 (I want to speak Korean well) while simultaneously admitting they have no intention of studying.

---

### Particle Usage: Object vs. Subject Markers
When identifying the thing you want, you can use either the object marker (을/를) or the subject marker (이/가). Both are grammatically correct, but they carry slightly different emphases. Using the subject marker, such as 피자가 먹고 싶어요, places more emphasis on the specific item desired (e.g., "It's pizza that I want to eat, not pasta"). While both are used, the subject marker is often preferred in natural conversation when expressing a strong craving or specific desire.

---

### Person Restrictions and ~고 싶어 하다
A critical rule in Korean grammar is that ~고 싶다 can generally only be used for the first person (I/we) or the second person (you, in questions). You cannot use it to state what a third person wants because, in Korean culture and language, you cannot "know" or assume the internal desires of another person.

To talk about a third person's wants, you must use the form ~고 싶어 하다. This transforms the internal state into an observable behavior (literally "to act like one wants to"). For example, to say "Hyunwoo wants to eat too," you would say 현우 씨도 먹고 싶어 해요. The only exception to this rule is in creative writing, where an omniscient narrator can state a character's desires directly, or when reporting something a person directly said about their own wants.

---

### Intentional "Want To": ~(으)ㄹ래(요)
The second form, ~(으)ㄹ래(요), is created by attaching 을래(요) to a verb stem ending in a consonant or ㄹ래(요) to a stem ending in a vowel. If a stem already ends in ㄹ, like 살다 (to live), you simply add 래(요) to get 살래요.

This form is strictly for informal or casual conversation. While it can be used in polite speech (with the 요 suffix), it is never used in formal settings like essays, speeches, or official announcements. The instructor suggests that this form likely originates from the intention form ~(으)려고 해요.

The primary difference between ~(으)ㄹ래(요) and ~고 싶다 is the element of "will" or "intention." While ~고 싶다 is just a feeling, ~(으)ㄹ래(요) implies "I want to and I'm going to." If you say 한국에 갈래요, it means you have the means, the plan, and the intention to go. You would not use this form if you lacked the money or time to actually make the trip. Like the previous form, this cannot be used for third-person statements. In questions, it functions as an invitation or a check on the listener's intention, such as 같이 공부할래요? (Do you want to study together? / Are you going to study with me?).
