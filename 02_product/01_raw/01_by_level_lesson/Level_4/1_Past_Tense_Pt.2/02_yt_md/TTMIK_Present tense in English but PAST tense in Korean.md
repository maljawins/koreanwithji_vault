## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor explores a unique linguistic difference between English and Korean regarding states of being. While English uses the present tense to describe current states like being "crazy" or "handsome," Korean often utilizes the past tense conjugation to express these same ideas. The lesson explains the underlying logic: these states are viewed as the result of an action or process that has already occurred. By understanding the literal meanings of the base verbs, learners can see why the past tense is the grammatically correct choice for describing a present condition.

---

### The Logic of 미쳤다 (To Be Crazy)
The instructor introduces the common expression for saying someone is crazy: **미쳤다**. Although this is translated as "is crazy" (present tense) in English, the Korean form is actually in the past tense. This is because the root verb **미치다** literally means "to go crazy" or "to become crazy," which is an action. Therefore, to describe someone as being in the state of "crazy" right now, Korean logic dictates that they must have already "become" crazy in the past. 

The instructor demonstrates this with the polite past tense form **미쳤어요**, which literally means "became crazy." This logic extends to the adjective form used before nouns. Instead of a present tense marker, the past tense conjugation **ㄴ** is used, resulting in **미친** (crazy).

---

### The Logic of 잘생겼다 (To Be Handsome)
A similar rule applies to the word for being good-looking or handsome: **잘생겼다**. The instructor breaks this down by explaining the verb **생기다**, which means "to come into existence" or "to be formed." To say someone is handsome, you are essentially saying they were "formed well" or "made well." 

Visually, the instructor emphasizes that the "forming" part of a person's appearance is an event that already happened in the past. Consequently, using the present tense form **잘생겨요** is incorrect when describing someone's current appearance. One must use the past tense **잘생겼어요** to express that someone "is" handsome right now. The instructor also notes that the adjective form follows this past-tense pattern: it is **잘생긴** (using the past tense marker **ㄴ**) rather than **잘생기는**.
