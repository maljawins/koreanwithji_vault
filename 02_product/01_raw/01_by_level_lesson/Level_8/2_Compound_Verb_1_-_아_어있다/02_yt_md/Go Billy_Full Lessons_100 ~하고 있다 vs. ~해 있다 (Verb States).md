## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces a specific grammar form used to describe the current state of an object or person resulting from a completed action. This lesson marks the 100th episode of the series and focuses on the distinction between the progressive tense (actions in motion) and the "state" form (the lasting result of an action). To fully grasp this concept, students should already be familiar with the progressive tense `~고 있다` and the formation of passive verbs.

The core of the lesson explains how to combine action verbs with `~아/어 있다` to express that something "is in the state of having been [verb-ed]." This is distinct from simply saying an action happened in the past; it emphasizes that the result of that action persists into the present moment.

---

### Prerequisites: Progressive Tense and Passive Voice
The instructor briefly reviews two foundational concepts. First, the progressive tense is used for actions currently happening, such as `지금 하고 있어요 (I am doing it now)`. Second, passive verbs are used when the subject is the recipient of an action. A comparison is shown: in the active sentence `원숭이는 사자를 먹었어요 (The monkey ate the lion)`, the monkey is the actor. In the passive version `사자가 원숭이에게 먹혔어요 (The lion was eaten by the monkey)`, the subject (the lion) is the one being acted upon. This lesson combines these ideas to describe ongoing states.

---

### Expressing States with ~아/어 있다
While the progressive tense `~고 있다` describes an action in the middle of occurring, the form `~아/어 있다` describes a state that remains after an action is finished. The instructor uses the verb `닫다 (to close)` to illustrate the progression of these forms:
- Active Past: `저는 문을 닫았어요.` / I closed the door.
- Progressive: `저는 문을 닫고 있었어요.` / I was closing the door.
- Passive Past: `문이 닫혔어요.` / The door was closed.
- **Resulting State:** `문이 닫혀 있어요.` / The door is (in the state of being) closed.

To conjugate this form, take the verb stem of an action verb and conjugate it as if adding the polite `~요` ending, then remove the `요` and attach the verb `있다 (to exist/to be)`. For example, the passive verb `닫히다 (to be closed)` becomes `닫혀`, and adding `있다` results in `닫혀 있다`. This indicates that someone closed the door at some point, and it remains closed now.

---

### Contrast: Action in Progress vs. Resulting State
The instructor highlights a common point of confusion using the verb `앉다 (to sit down)`. 
- `앉고 있다` means "to be in the process of sitting down" (the physical movement of lowering oneself into a chair).
- `앉아 있다` means "to be sitting" (the state of already being in the seat).

A practical example is provided: `철수가 저기에 앉아 있어요 (Chul-soo is sitting over there)`. If one were to say `철수가 저기에 앉고 있어요`, it would imply Chul-soo is currently in the middle of the slow-motion act of sitting down but hasn't finished yet.

---

### Common Examples and Negation
Several common verb pairs are demonstrated to show how this form functions in everyday speech:
- `열리다 (to open)` → `열려 있다 (to be open)`: `창문이 열려 있었어요.` / The window was open.
- `살다 (to live)` → `살아 있다 (to be alive)`: `벌레가 아직 살아 있어.` / The bug is still alive.
- `서다 (to stand)` → `서 있다 (to be standing)`: `철수가 김 선생님 옆에 서 있어요.` / Chul-soo is standing next to Mr. Kim.
- `깨어지다 (to break)` → `깨어져 있다 (to be broken)`: `유리가 깨져 있지 않아요.` / The glass isn't broken.
- `연결이 되다 (to become connected)` → `연결이 되어 있다 (to be connected)`: `선이 연결되어 있어요.` / The wires are connected.

To make this form negative, simply conjugate the final `있다` as negative, such as `~아/어 있지 않다`. The instructor notes that `유리가 깨져 있지 않아요` (the state of being broken does not exist) is slightly different from the simple past negative `깨지지 않았어요` (it did not break), though they often convey similar information in context.
