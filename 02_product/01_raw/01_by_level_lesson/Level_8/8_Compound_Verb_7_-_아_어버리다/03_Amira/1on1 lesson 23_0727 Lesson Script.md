버리다, on its own, means to “throw away” So, literally, “to throw away the action”

When you think about this form, I want you to remember two keywords

When you think about this form, I want you to remember two keywords, like when you're throwing something away: the **feeling of "away"** and the feeling of **"Screw it!"** In English, after saying "screw it," you'd often go and do something completely, like totally, right? So instead, think of it as being connected to the **"feeling" of "away."**

**잊어버리다**

We have two verbs that appear in this 아/어 버리다 structure.

The first one is **잊어버리다**.

잊어버리다 comes from 잊다 (to forget) + 버리다.

Memory is a binary concept. You either remember it or you don’t. So when you forget something, you “completely” forget it. It’s impossible to “partially” forget something.

So, 잊다 is rarely used on its own in the sense of "to completely forget."

It implies that the forgetting is done, your memory’s gone "away."

예문 3개

 비밀번호를 잊어버렸어요. (I completely forgot the password.)

 친구 생일을 잊어버려서 미안해요. (I'm sorry I completely forgot my friend's birthday.)

 가끔 내가 한국 사람이라는 것을 잊어버려요. (Sometimes I completely forget that I'm Korean.)

**잃어버리다**

The next one is 잃어버리다.

잃어버리다 comes from 잃다. Which means to lose, not in the sense of winning and losing but to lose something.

It’s the same logic as 잊어버리다. You either have your stuff, or you don’t. And when you lose something, you completely lose it.

So again, 잃다 is rarely used on its own.

예문 3개

지갑을 잃어버렸어요. (I completely lost my wallet.)

어제 산 열쇠를 잃어버려서 집에 못 들어갔어요. (I lost the key I bought yesterday, so I couldn't go home.)

어릴 때부터 키우던 강아지를 잃어버려서 많이 슬펐어요. (I was very sad because I lost the puppy I had raised since I was little.)

**The Plain Form Formal low**

Alright, let’s talk about the plain form, now.

The **Plain Form** is just another name for the **Formal Low Speech Level** among these four speech levels.

Understanding the plain form is **crucial** for learning the **quoting form**, which you've seen a lot recently in your listening practice!

The plain form is a prerequisite for that.

The plain form is primarily used in **writing** like articles, advertisements, essays, and journals, which is why I haven't covered it in detail until now.

It’s supposed to be directed to unknown people, so it sounds really “plain” in terms of respect level. It’s neither polite or casual. That’s why it’s called the "plain form"

However, that's not to say it's *never* used in speech.

When we use it in speech, it's considered 반말, and it's typically used in three main situations:

1. **Making announcements:** When you're speaking loudly to get attention, like with a megaphone.
2. **Talking to yourself:** When your speech isn't directed at anyone in particular.
3. **To tell a story:** Often with a rising intonation, similar to ~는 거야? or ~거든?.

**The plain form – Usages in Speech**

Let’s look at each case with examples.

**1. Making announcements:**

* 지금부터 회의를 시작한다! (The meeting starts now!) - *Formal announcement.*
* 모두 주목! 중요한 공지가 있다! (Everyone, pay attention! There's an important announcement!) - *Getting attention.*

**2. Talking to yourself:**

* 아, 배고프다. 뭐 먹지? (Ah, I'm hungry. What should I eat?) - *Thinking out loud.*
* 벌써 시간이 이렇게 됐나? 빨리 가야겠다. (Has it already gotten this late? I should go quickly.) - *Self-reflection*

**3. To tell a story (with rising intonation):**

* 어제 길 가다가 이상한 사람 봤다~? (Yesterday, as I was walking, I saw a strange person~?) - *Inviting the listener to hear a story.*
* 나 어제 친구랑 싸웠다~? (I fought with my friend yesterday~?) - *Setting up a narrative for a friend.*

**Plain form – Conjugation Statements – Present Tense**

So, this is how you make the plain form.

• **Present Tense:**

    ◦ **Action Verbs**:

        ▪ Take the verb **stem**.

        ▪ If the stem ends with a **vowel**, attach **-ㄴ다** (e.g., 보다 → 본다).

        ▪ If the stem ends with a **consonant**, attach **-는다** (e.g., 믿다 → 믿는다).

        ▪ **Exception for 'ㄹ' (l) ending stems**: If it ends in 'ㄹ', you first **remove the 'ㄹ'** and then attach **-ㄴ다** (e.g., 살다 → 산다).

    ◦ **Descriptive Verbs**:

        ▪ For descriptive verbs, you don’t have to do anything. It stays the same as the dictionary form.

예문 5개

 나는 매일 아침 일찍 일어난다. (I wake up early every morning.) - *Action verb*

 이 영화는 정말 재미있다. (This movie is really fun.) - *Descriptive verb*

 그는 한국어를 열심히 공부한다. (He studies Korean diligently.) - *Action verb*

 저 사람은 내 친구다. (That person is my friend.) - *이다 with vowel-ending noun*

 이것은 좋은 생각이다. (This is a good idea.) - *이다 with consonant-ending noun*

**Plain form – Conjugation Statements – Past Tense**

• **Past Tense Conjugation in the plain form is also very easy!**

**You just add 다 after the** past tense stem

 ◦ **'이다' (to be)**:

        ▪ After a **consonant**, the past plain form is **이었다** (e.g., 책이었다).

        ▪ After a **vowel**, the past plain form is **였다** (e.g., 의자였다).

예문 5개

* 어제 친구와 영화를 **봤다.**
  - I **watched** a movie with my friend yesterday.
* 날씨가 정말 **좋았다.**
  - The weather **was** really **good.**

그 이야기는 **사실이었다.**
- That story **was true.**

나는 어릴 때 **가난한 학생이었다.**
 - **I was a poor student** when I was young.

어제 밤에 **너무 피곤했다.**
 - I **was too tired** last night.

**Plain form – Conjugation Statements – Future Tense**

• **Future Tense:**

Future tense 또한 굉장히 쉽다. Originally it’s ㄹ/을 것이다, but most of the time, ‘것이다’ is just **shortened to 거다**.

**'이다' (to be)** becomes ‘일 거다’ but in when the noun that comes before 이다 ends in a vowel like 의자, we can shorten it to ‘의잘 거다’ only in spoken Korean.

예문 5개

나는 내일 도서관에 갈 거다. (I will go to the library tomorrow.)

이 문제는 쉬울 거다. (This problem will be easy.)

그는 좋은 의사가 될 거다. (He will become a good doctor.)

내년에는 더 행복할 거다. (I will be happier next year.)

이것은 좋은 추억일 거다. (This will be a good memory.)

**The Plain Form Question – Present**

The rules we just learned were for **declarative sentences**, meaning statements.

And as you read more Korean texts, you might occasionally see the plain form endings for **Question, Suggestion, and Command** sentence types.

However, in speech, the plain form is almost exclusively used for statements. So, I don't want you to try and memorize how to make these forms. Instead, I want you to simply **keep these tables** as a reference and just know that it’s usually 는가 for writing and 나 for talking to yourself.

지금 어디 가는가? (Where are you going now? - *Written/Formal tone*)

내가 너무 시끄러웠나? (Was I too loud? - *Self-talk*)

이 문제가 쉬운가? (Is this problem easy? - *Written/Formal tone*)

저 사람이 학생인가? (Is that person a student? - *Written/Formal tone*)

이게 진짜인가? (Is this real? - *Self-talk/Written*)

**The Plain Form Question – Past Tense**

It's similar to the present tense: ~는가? is mostly used in writing, and ~나? is often used when talking to yourself.

 어제 숙제를 다 했는가? (Did you finish all the homework yesterday? - *Written/Formal tone*)

 내가 어제 너무 많이 먹었나? (Did I eat too much yesterday? - *Self-talk*)

 그 영화가 재미있었나? (Was that movie fun? - *Self-talk/Written*)

 그 사람이 진짜 의사였나? (Was that person really a doctor? - *Written/Formal tone*)

 이게 꿈이었나? (Was this a dream? - *Self-talk*)

**The Plain Form Question – Future Tense**

 For Action verbs, you'll see ~ㄹ/을 건가?, which is a shortened form of ~ㄹ/을 것인가?.

 For Descriptive verbs, it becomes ~ㄹ/을까?.

 For 이다, it becomes ~일까? (or ~ㄹ까? for vowel-ending nouns in spoken Korean).

**내일 비가 올 건가? (Will it rain tomorrow? - *Written/Formal tone*)**

**내가 잘 할 수 있을까? (Will I be able to do well? - *Self-talk*)**

**이게 정말 가능할까? (Will this really be possible? - *Self-talk/Written*)**

**그가 내일 여기에 올 건가? (Will he come here tomorrow? - *Written/Formal tone*)**

**이게 답일까? (Will this be the answer? - *Self-talk/Written*)**

**The Plain Form - Suggestion & Commands**

Naturally, suggestions and commands can **only be in the present tense**, because you can only suggest or command something in the current moment. So there’s no past or future tense.

you can **only use action verbs** here, not descriptive verbs. Because you can't suggest or command a "state."

**For Suggestions:**

It's very simple! You just attach **~자** to the action verb stem. Exactly the same as the regular 반말

**For Commands**

The plain form command endings are almost never used in speech especially this 시오 ending.

It sounds way too serious and strict. So, we normally just use the polite ~세요 ending for commands in most situations.

You can find 시오 exclusively in very formal or strict instructions, like in exam questions or work manuals.

Let's look at an example from a math problem: 다음 연립방정식을 푸시오.

* 연립 means "connected" or "simultaneous."
* 방정식 means "an equation."
* 풀다 means "to solve."

So, 다음 연립방정식을 푸시오 is telling you to "Solve the following simultaneous equations."

This is the kind of strict, formal instruction where you'd see ~시오.
