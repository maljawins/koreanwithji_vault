## Lesson Overview
This lesson marks the first speaking practice session for the student, Amira, designed to build confidence in conversational Korean. Ji emphasizes a relaxed environment where speaking slowly and asking for help in English is encouraged. The session begins with detailed feedback on previous assignments, specifically addressing nuances in future-tense conjectures and the "how-to" usage of certain grammar patterns.

The core of the lesson introduces the compound verb form 아/어 버리다, clarifying its meaning beyond simple "unintentional" actions to encompass a sense of completion, relief, or even impulsive "screw it" energy. Following this, the lesson dives into the "Plain Form" (Formal Low speech level). While primarily a written form, Ji explains its specific roles in spoken Korean—such as making announcements or talking to oneself—and establishes it as the essential prerequisite for mastering the "Quoting Form," which is introduced briefly at the end of the session.

### Slide 8 — 아/어 버리다 (Theory)
- **On screen:** The main heading 아/어 버리다. Visuals include:
    - A note that 버리다 means "To throw away."
    - The "FEELING of away," comparable to "Totally" or "Completely."
    - A "Screw it!" prompt.
    - Comic panels: A pig looking back at 2023 saying "Before you know it, it's the end of the year" (어느덧 연말); a character yelling "Just go AWAY" (그냥 가버려!).
    - A character holding a credit card saying "Just Buy it!" (질러버려!).
    - Grammar note: 르 → ㄹㄹ (지르다 + 버리다 = 질러버려).
- **Ji's teaching:** This form was briefly mentioned in the "To end up" video I sent you. It might have sounded like it means "to end up," but it's not quite the same. 버리다 on its own means to "throw away," so literally, this is "to throw away the action." I want you to remember two keywords: the feeling of "away" and the feeling of "Screw it!" In English, after saying "screw it," you'd often do something completely or totally. That's the feeling here. It is not necessarily "unintentional" or "unfortunate." It can sound regrettable in the past tense because the action was impulsive, but it can also show something done "very intentionally." Look at the comic: 가버려! (Go away!) is 가다 + 버리다. It’s a strong command to "throw away the going." Then there's 질러버려!. 지르다 is hard to translate, but in the context of 소리를 지르다 it means to scream. As slang, 지르다 means to buy something impulsively. So "질러버려!" with a credit card means "Just buy it! Throw away your money and think about it later!"

### Slide 9 — 아/어 버리다 (Example Sentences)
- **On screen:** Three examples with color-coded usage labels:
    - 숙제를 다 해 버렸어요! (Positive usage - relief/completion).
    - 결국 다 먹어 버렸어요. (Neither positive nor negative - completely consumed).
    - 그냥 포기해 버렸어요. (Negative usage - effort "thrown away").
- **Ji's teaching:** Let's look at these examples. "숙제를 다 해 버렸어요!" implies a sense of completion and relief. The homework is "away" from your to-do list, so this is positive. "결국 다 먹어 버렸어요" means "In the end, I ate it all." This food is "away," completely consumed. It can be slightly regrettable if you were trying not to eat too much, but it's generally neutral. Finally, "그냥 포기해 버렸어요" means "I just gave up completely." This is a decisive, final action where the effort is "thrown away," making it a negative usage. The keyword is always "AWAY."

### Slide 10 — Common 버리다 verb: 잊어버리다
- **On screen:** 잊어버리다 (To completely forget). Note: 잊다 is rarely used on its own. A box explains: "You either remember or you don’t... you completely forget it, not partially."
- **Ji's teaching:** We have two verbs that almost always appear in this structure. The first is 잊어버리다 (잊다 + 버리다). Memory is binary; you either remember or you don't. When you forget, you "completely" forget. It’s impossible to "partially" forget. Therefore, 잊다 is rarely used alone. It implies the memory has gone "away." For example: 비밀번호를 잊어버렸어요 (I completely forgot the password) or 네 생일을 잊어버려서 미안해 (I'm sorry I completely forgot your birthday).

### Slide 11 — Common 버리다 verb: 잃어버리다
- **On screen:** 잃어버리다 (To completely lose something). Note: 잃다 is rarely used on its own. A box distinguishes 잃다 from 지다 (to lose a match). Example: 호주가 한국에게 축구 경기를 져버렸어요.
- **Ji's teaching:** The next one is 잃어버리다, from 잃다 (to lose an object). It follows the same logic as 잊어버리다—you either have your stuff or you don't. When you lose it, it's completely gone. Note that 잃다 is not for losing a game; for that, we use 지다. For example, "Australia completely lost a football match against Korea" would be 호주가 한국에게 축구 경기를 져버렸어요. For objects, we say 지갑을 잃어버렸어요 (I completely lost my wallet).

### Slide 12 — The Plain Form (Formal Low)
- **On screen:** A table showing the four respect levels. The Plain Form is identified as "Formal Low" (Formal + 반말/Low). It is noted as "More common in writings" (articles, essays, etc.) and "Prerequisite for the Quoting Form."
- **Ji's teaching:** Now let's talk about the Plain Form, which is the Formal Low speech level. Understanding this is crucial because it is a prerequisite for the Quoting Form, which you've seen a lot in your listening practice. It is primarily used in writing—articles, advertisements, essays—because it sounds neutral and "plain." It’s directed at unknown people. However, it is used in speech too, where it's considered 반말. We use it for making announcements, talking to yourself, or telling a story with a rising intonation.

### Slide 13 — The Plain Form: Usages in Speech
- **On screen:** Three categories of speech usage with examples:
    1. **Announcements:** 지금부터 회의를 시작한다! (We start the meeting now!).
    2. **Talking to yourself:** 아, 배고프다. (Ah, I'm hungry).
    3. **Storytelling:** 어제 길 가다가 이상한 사람 봤다~? (I saw a strange person...?).
- **Ji's teaching:** Let's look at the spoken cases. For announcements, you use it when speaking loudly to get attention, like "지금부터 회의를 시작한다!" For talking to yourself, it's for thoughts not directed at anyone, like "아, 배고프다. 뭐 먹지?" (Ah, I'm hungry. What should I eat?). Finally, for storytelling, you use a rising intonation to invite the listener into the narrative: "나 어제 친구랑 싸웠다~?" (I fought with my friend yesterday~?). This sets up the story for your friend.

### Slide 14 — Plain Form: Conjugation (Present Tense)
- **On screen:** A conjugation table for Present Tense:
    - **Action Verbs:** Stem + ㄴ다 (vowel) or 는다 (consonant). Special rule: Remove ㄹ then add ㄴ다 (살다 → 산다).
    - **Descriptive Verbs:** Same as dictionary form (귀엽다 → 귀엽다).
    - **이다:** (이)다.
- **Ji's teaching:** Here is how you conjugate the present tense. For action verbs, if the stem ends in a vowel, attach -ㄴ다 (본다). If it ends in a consonant, attach -는다 (먹는다). If it ends in 'ㄹ', remove the 'ㄹ' and attach -ㄴ다, so 살다 becomes 산다. For descriptive verbs, it's easy: they stay exactly like the dictionary form. For example, "이 영화는 정말 재미있다" (This movie is really fun). For 이다, it becomes 다 after a vowel (내 친구다) and 이다 after a consonant (좋은 생각이다).

### Slide 15 — Plain Form: Conjugation (Past Tense)
- **On screen:** Table showing Past Tense conjugation. Rule: Past stem + 다 (았/었다).
    - 이다 becomes 이었다 (consonant) or 였다 (vowel).
- **Ji's teaching:** Past tense is also very easy. You just add 다 after the past tense stem. For example, "어제 친구와 영화를 봤다" (I watched a movie with my friend yesterday). For 이다, it becomes 이었다 after a consonant, like 사실이었다 (it was true), and 였다 after a vowel, like 의자였다 (it was a chair).

### Slide 16 — Plain Form: Conjugation (Future Tense)
- **On screen:** Table for Future Tense. Rule: ㄹ/을 거다 (shortened from 것이다).
- **Ji's teaching:** Future tense is also straightforward. While the original form is ㄹ/을 것이다, we almost always shorten it to 거다 in the plain form. For example, "나는 내일 수영장에 갈 거다" (I will go to the swimming pool tomorrow). For 이다, it becomes 일 거다, though in spoken Korean, vowel-ending nouns can shorten further (e.g., 의잘 거다).

### Slide 17~19 — Plain Form: Questions (Present & Past)
- **On screen:** Tables for questions.
    - **Written:** 는가? / 은가? / 인가?
    - **Self-talk:** 나?
- **Ji's teaching:** These rules are for statements. In writing, you might see 는가? for questions, but in speech, the plain form is mostly for statements. When you do use it for questions in speech, it's usually the 나? ending for talking to yourself. For example, "내가 너무 시끄러웠나?" (Was I too loud?). In formal writing, you'd see "이 문제가 쉬운가?" (Is this problem easy?). Don't worry about memorizing these perfectly yet; just use the tables as a reference.

### Slide 20~21 — Plain Form: Questions (Future)
- **On screen:** Future question forms: 건가? (Action) and ㄹ/을까? (Descriptive/이다).
- **Ji's teaching:** For future questions, action verbs use 건가? (short for 것인가?), like "내일 비가 올 건가?" (Will it rain tomorrow?). Descriptive verbs and 이다 use ㄹ/을까?, such as "내가 잘 할 수 있을까?" (Will I be able to do well?) or "이게 답일까?" (Will this be the answer?).

### Slide 22 — Plain Form: Suggestion & Commands
- **On screen:**
    - **Suggestions:** Action verb + 자 (Same as regular 반말).
    - **Commands:** Action verb + (으)시오.
    - Note: Only works with action verbs.
    - Screenshot of a math problem: "다음 연립방정식을 푸시오." (Solve the following simultaneous equations).
- **Ji's teaching:** Suggestions and commands only exist in the present tense and only work with action verbs—you can't command a "state." Suggestions are simple: just add ~자, like regular 반말. Commands using ~(으)시오 are never used in speech; they sound too strict. You only see them in formal instructions like exam papers. For example, "다음 연립방정식을 푸시오" uses 풀다 (to solve) to tell you to solve the equations. In normal life, we just use the polite ~세요.

## Teaching Points
- **아/어 버리다 (Completion/Impulse):** This form emphasizes that an action is "thrown away" or completely finished. It often carries a nuance of relief (homework done), regret (ate too much), or a "screw it" impulsive energy (buying something). It is not strictly for "unintentional" actions; it can be very intentional.
- **잊어버리다 vs. 잃어버리다:** These two verbs almost always use the 버리다 form because forgetting and losing are considered absolute, "complete" actions. Note that 잃다 is for losing objects, while 지다 is for losing a game/match.
- **The Plain Form (Formal Low):**
    - **Usage:** Primarily for writing (news, essays). In speech, it is used for announcements, self-talk, and storytelling.
    - **Conjugation (Present Action):** Stem + ㄴ다/는다. (Special: ㄹ drops before ㄴ다).
    - **Conjugation (Present Descriptive):** Stays as the dictionary form.
    - **Conjugation (Past):** Past stem + 다.
    - **Conjugation (Future):** ㄹ/을 거다.
- **Quoting Form Construction:** To quote a statement, take the Plain Form of the verb and add 고. This makes the Plain Form a vital building block for intermediate Korean grammar.
- **Commands (~시오):** This is a very formal, written command form found in manuals or exams. It is not used in spoken conversation.
