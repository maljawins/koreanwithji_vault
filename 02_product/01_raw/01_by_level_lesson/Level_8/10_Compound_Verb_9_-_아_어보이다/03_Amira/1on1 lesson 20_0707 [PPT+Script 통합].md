## Lesson Overview
This 20th session marks a significant milestone in the curriculum, focusing on a comprehensive deep dive into compound verbs and the complexities of noun modifiers. The lesson is designed to transition from basic verb usage to more nuanced expressions that convey intention, state of being, and specific timelines. Ji emphasizes that reaching this point reflects substantial progress, as the topics covered—such as the versatile 아/어지다 form and the various past tense modifiers—require a strong grasp of foundational Korean logic.

The lesson begins with a detailed review of previous assignments, using the student's own notes to clarify the distinctions between intransitive and causative verbs (like 남다 and 남기다) and exploring advanced combinations like 하려다(가). The core of the session explores compound verbs formed with 주다 (to give), 보다 (to see/try), and 있다 (to exist), highlighting how these additions change the meaning of a base verb to imply favors, experiences, or maintained states.

A major portion of the lesson is dedicated to modifiers. Ji recaps present and future modifiers before introducing the three distinct forms of past tense modifiers: 은/ㄴ, 던, and 았던/었던. By using visual timelines and specific examples, the lesson illustrates how Korean distinguishes between completed actions, habitual past behaviors, and actions that were interrupted or remain uncompleted.

The session concludes with an introduction to comparison markers and the causative relationship between 나다 and 내다. These topics are set as self-study goals to encourage independent exploration of how Korean speakers express outcomes and relative differences.

- **On screen:** Title ~아/어있다 (계시다). Definition: To be in the state of a completed action. Formula: A.V.S + 아/어 + 있다. Example: 살다 → 살아있다 (To be alive). Image from the movie "#ALIVE" with the text "꼭 살아남아야 한다" (At any cost, you must survive).
- **Ji's teaching:** Alright, let’s get back to our compound verbs. We’ve talked about 아/어 있다 before, but I want to reinforce the restrictions. Remember, 아/어 있다 describes a state that continues after an action is finished. It’s like the "-en" in "broken."

### Slide 15 — ~고 있다 VS ~아/어있다
- **On screen:** Comparison table. ~고 있다 (ongoing action) vs ~아/어있다 (resulting state). Examples: 창문이 깨지고 있어요 (is breaking) vs 창문이 깨져있어요 (is broken). Image of a baby in a seat with a speech bubble: "저 지금 앉아있죠?" (I'm seated, right?). Box explains 깨지다 (intransitive) vs 깨다 (transitive).
- **Ji's teaching:** Look at the difference between ongoing actions and states. If a window is currently in the process of shattering, we use 깨지고 있어요. But if it's already broken and just sitting there in that state, it's 깨져있어요. Similarly, for the baby in the picture, we say 앉아있어요 because the baby is in the resulting state of having sat down.

### Slide 16 — ~아/어있다 Restrictions
- **On screen:** Title ~아/어있다 Restriction. Rule: Can only be used with verbs that can remain in that state (usually intransitive). Incorrect: 문을 닫아있다 (X). Correct: 문이 닫혀있다 (O). Rule: Use ~고 있다 with transitive verbs like 입다 (to wear). Example: 옷을 입고 있다 (O). Image of a closed pharmacy with a sign "보람약국."
- **Ji's teaching:** Look at slide 16. You can’t say "문을 닫아있다" (X). 닫다 is transitive—you close the door. In Korean, you can't "be in the state of having closed the door" using the active verb. Instead, you have to use the passive/intransitive version: 닫히다. So, "문이 닫혀있다" (O) is the correct way to say "The door is closed." If you want to use a transitive verb to show a state, you actually use ~고 있다. For example, "옷을 입고 있다." Even though you finished putting the clothes on, we use the "ongoing" form to show you are currently wearing them. It’s a bit of a quirk in Korean logic, but if you remember "Intransitive for 아/어 있다" and "Transitive for 고 있다," you’ll be fine.

### Slide 17 — ~아/어주다 (주시다 / 드리다)
- **On screen:** Title ~아/어주다. Definition: To do for someone. Formula: A.V.S + 아/어 + 주다. Honorific: 주시다. Humble: 드리다. Examples: 사줬어요 (bought me), 만들어 주셨어요 (made for me). Box explains 아/어 주세요 as a polite request. Image of a taxi driver.
- **Ji's teaching:** Next is 아/어 주다. This is one of the most common forms you’ll hear. It means doing something as a favor for someone. In English, we just say "I bought lunch." But in Korean, if I bought it *for you*, I’d say "사 줬어요." It adds a layer of kindness and relationship to the sentence. When we make requests, we use 아/어 주세요. This is much more polite than just using the honorific command ~세요. If you tell a taxi driver "공항으로 가세요," it sounds like a direct order: "GO to the airport." But if you say "공항으로 가 주세요," it’s like "Please do me the favor of going to the airport." It’s much softer.

- **Ji's teaching:** Let’s look at slide 18. 도와주다 (to help) is so common that we almost always learn it as a single word. Notice how we use 좀 to soften the tone. "물 좀 주세요" isn't just "Give me some water"; it’s "Could you please give me some water?" It makes the request feel less demanding.

### Slide 19 — Common 주다 verb: 알려주다
- **On screen:** 알려주다 (알리다 + 주다). Explains 알리다 is causative of 알다 (to know). Example: "나도 좀 알려줘" (Let me know, too). Box explains 이건 vs 이게.
- **Ji's teaching:** 알려주다 means "to let someone know" or "to inform." It comes from 알리다, which is the causative of 알다 (to know). So you are causing someone else to know something.

### Slide 20 — Common 주다 verb: 빌려주다
- **On screen:** 빌려주다 (빌리다 + 주다). Definition: To lend something to someone. Examples: "네 것 좀 빌려줄 수 있어?" (Could you lend me yours?), "빌려주시면 내일 갚겠습니다" (If you lend it, I'll pay you back).
- **Ji's teaching:** 빌려주다 means "to lend." It’s 빌리다 (to borrow) + 주다 (to give). So you are "giving the act of borrowing" to someone else.

### Slide 21 — Common 주다 verb: 보내주다
- **On screen:** 보내주다 (보내다 + 주다). Definition: To send to/for someone; To let go. Reminder: 어 cancels out with ㅐ/ㅔ (지내다 → 지내요). Example: "이제 집에 좀 보내주세요" (Now, let me go home please).
- **Ji's teaching:** 보내주다 is "to send for someone," but it can also mean "to let someone go." Like in the example: "너무 피곤해서요. 이제 집에 좀 보내주세요." (I'm so tired. Please let me go home now.)

### Slide 22 — Common 주다 verb: 보여주다
- **On screen:** 보여주다 (보이다 + 주다). Definition: To show something to someone. Box explains 보이다 vs 보다. 보이다 is causative of 보다. Example: "샘플은 안 보여주셨습니다" (As for the sample, they didn't show it).
- **Ji's teaching:** And 보여주다 is "to show." It comes from 보이다 (to be seen). Again, showing is an act of giving information or a sight to someone else. Notice the example "샘플은 안 보여주셨습니다." Here, the 은 marker implies contrast—maybe they showed the price or the catalog, but *as for the sample*, they didn't show it.

### Slide 23 — ~아/어 보다
- **On screen:** Title ~아/어 보다. Definition: To try (experience). Note: 보다 does NOT mean "to see physically" here. Note: Does NOT mean "to put effort" (노력하다). Examples: 입어 봤는데 (tried wearing), 물어볼까요? (should we try asking?). Box explains 아/어 봤다 (I have tried/experienced).
- **Ji's teaching:** Now let’s talk about 아/어 보다. This is often translated as "to try," but I want you to be careful. It doesn't mean "to put in effort"—that’s 노력하다. 아/어 보다 means "to do something and see what happens" or "to experience something." If you’re at a shop, the clerk might say "입어 보세요" (Try it on). They mean "Wear it and see how it looks."

### Slide 24 — Common 보다 verb: 물어보다
- **On screen:** 물어보다 (묻다 + 보다). Explains ㄷ irregular: 묻다 → 물 + 어 = 물어. Note: 묻다 is a homonym (to bury, to get stained). Examples: "저한테 물어보세요?" (asking me?), "선생님한테 여쭤볼까요?" (ask the teacher? - humble).
- **Ji's teaching:** 물어보다 is "to ask and see." We use the ㄷ irregular rule here, changing 묻다 to 물어. Note that 묻다 can also mean to bury or to get a stain, but those are regular verbs. When we mean "to ask," it's irregular.

### Slide 25 — 봐 봐 - 3 meanings
- **On screen:** Title 봐 봐. Meanings: 1. Look at that, 2. See? (I told you so), 3. Let me see. Image of a man with captions for these meanings.
- **Ji's teaching:** "봐 봐" is a super common expression in casual speech (반말). Depending on your tone and the context, it can mean "Look at that," "See? I told you so," or "Let me see."

### Slide 26 — 아/어본 적(이) 있다/없다
- **On screen:** Title 아/어본 적(이) 있다/없다. Definition: To have the experience of having tried something. 적 is a bound noun meaning "experience." Comparison: 한국에 가봤어? (Have you been?) vs 한국에 가본 적 있어? (Have you EVER been?). Box explains modifier forms of 보다: 보는 (present), 본 (past), 보던 (used to), 볼 (future).
- **Ji's teaching:** Because the 아/어 보다 form is about experience, we use it to say "I have done something before." This leads us to the "Have you EVER..." form. While "한국에 가봤어?" is a casual "Have you been to Korea?", "한국에 가본 적 있어?" puts a much stronger emphasis on the experience itself. It’s like asking if that experience exists in your life’s history.

### Slide 27 — ~아/어 보이다
- **On screen:** Title ~아/어 보이다. Definition: To seem / To look (used with Descriptive Verbs). Examples: 비싸 보이다 (seem expensive), 예뻐 보이다 (seem pretty). Example sentence: "SNS에서는 모두가 행복해 보이지만..." (On social media, everyone seems happy, but...). Box explains SNS is Konglish for Social Media.
- **Ji's teaching:** Here is a very important one: 아/어 보이다. This is the form that actually means "to seem" or "to look like." Remember how I told you last time that 아/어하다 does *not* mean "to seem"? This is the one you were looking for! It only attaches to Descriptive Verbs. 행복하다 (to be happy) becomes 행복해 보이다 (to look happy). Look at the example: "SNS에서는 모두가 행복해 보이지만..." On social media, everyone *looks* happy, but the truth is different. This is about your visual impression of a state.

### Slide 28 — ~아/어지다
- **On screen:** Title ~아/어지다. Definition: To become. 3 usages: 1. To become/get (D.V), 2. Passive verb (for verbs without 이/히/리/기), 3. Can & Can't (external factors). Examples: 피곤해져요 (get tired), 만들어졌어요 (is made), 안 일어나져요 (can't get up - due to hangover). Box explains 숨이 쉬어지다 (breath is breathed).
- **Ji's teaching:** Now let’s look at 아/어지다. This is a powerhouse in Korean grammar with three main usages. First, "to become" or "to get" with descriptive verbs. Second, it acts as a passive voice for verbs that don't fit the standard pattern, like 만들어지다 (to be made). Third, and this is really cool, it’s used for "Can and Can't" when the reason is an external factor. Look at the example: "숙취 때문에 아침에 안 일어나져요." It’s not that I’m choosing not to get up; it’s that because of this hangover, my body just *won't be gotten up*. It’s a very nuanced way to express inability.

### Slide 29 — 있다 / 없다 + 어지다
- **On screen:** Title 있다 / 없다 + 어지다. Rule: 없다 → 없어지다 (To disappear). Rule: 있어지다 is incorrect; use 생기다 (to appear/arise) instead. Examples: 재미있어지네요 (becomes fun), 맛있어졌어요 (became delicious).
- **Ji's teaching:** While 없어지다 is the standard way to say "to disappear," we don't say 있어지다 for "to appear." Instead, we use the verb 생기다. However, 아/어지다 works perfectly with descriptive verbs that end in 있다, like 재미있다 or 맛있다, to show something is *becoming* fun or delicious.

### Slide 30 — ~고 싶어지다
- **On screen:** Title ~고 싶어지다. Definition: To become wanting to (start wanting to). Examples: 마시고 싶어지네 (feel like having a beer), 가고 싶어져요 (end up wanting to go). Side note: Noun 이/가 땡기다 (slang for craving).
- **Ji's teaching:** We have 고 싶어지다, which means "to start wanting to." You didn't want it before, but now the feeling is growing. "맥주가 마시고 싶어지네" (Now I feel like having a beer). A common slang synonym for this is 땡기다, which literally means "to pull." It's like saying the food is pulling you in.

### Slide 31 — Modifier Recap: Present Tense
- **On screen:** Title Modifier Recap Present. Examples: 귀여운 고양이 (cute cat), 집에 가는 남자 (man who goes home). Table shows rules: Descriptive verbs + 은/ㄴ, Action verbs + 는. Highlights that 있다/없다 always take 는.
- **Ji's teaching:** Let’s do a quick recap of modifiers. Think of the verb as a branch on a tree. When we want to describe a noun, the verb has to change its shape to "hook" onto that noun. For the Present Tense: Descriptive verbs take 은/ㄴ (예쁜, 작은), while Action verbs take 는 (가는, 먹는). And remember, 있다 and 없다 always take 는 (있는, 없는).

### Slide 32 — Modifier Recap: Future
- **On screen:** Title Modifier Recap Future. Rule: 을/ㄹ shows something that hasn't happened yet (possibility/future action). Examples: 할 것 (thing to do), 먹을 (to eat).
- **Ji's teaching:** For the Future Tense, we use the 을/ㄹ branch. This refers to things that haven't happened yet or possibilities. Even though it's not strictly "future tense" by grammar books, we call it the future branch for easier understanding.

### Slide 33~34 — Modifier Recap: Examples
- **On screen:** (Slide 33) Present examples: "제가 사는 곳" (place I live), "걷는 것" (act of walking). (Slide 34) Future examples: "예쁠 것 같아요" (looks like it will be prettier), "읽을 것" (things to read).
- **Ji's teaching:** These examples show how the modifiers function in full sentences. Notice how 사는 (from 살다) describes the place, and 읽을 describes the things you will read on the way.

### Slide 35 — Past tense Modifier Overview
- **On screen:** Title Past tense Modifier. Definition: Noun that did / has / had. 3 forms: 1. A.V.S + 은/ㄴ (completed), 2. A.V.S + 던 (was doing/habitual), 3. V.S + 았/었 + 던 (completely finished/disconnected). Box explains 던 것, 던데(요), 던 중.
- **Ji's teaching:** This is the "Past Tense Modifier" section, and it’s where things get interesting. Korean has three ways to describe a noun in the past, and they each have a different "timeline" vibe. In most cases, they might seem interchangeable, but there are slight differences in the timeline.

### Slide 36 — A.V.S + 은/ㄴ
- **On screen:** Title A.V.S + 은/ㄴ. Rule: Simply indicates a completed action. Timeline diagram shows "Completed" before "Now," with an arrow suggesting it "May affect the present." Examples: 지금 내가 입은 옷 (clothes I'm wearing now) vs 어제 내가 입은 옷 (clothes I wore yesterday). Box explains D.V.S + 은/ㄴ (e.g., 바쁜 남자) is technically present but implies he *became* busy.
- **Ji's teaching:** A.V.S + 은/ㄴ simply indicates a completed action. It might still be relevant now, or it might not. "내가 입은 옷" could be the clothes I put on this morning and am still wearing, or it could be the clothes I wore yesterday. Context tells the story. For descriptive verbs, like 바쁜, it's technically present tense, but it implies the state of becoming busy happened in the past and continues now.

### Slide 37 — A.V.S + 았/었던 & A.V.S + 던
- **On screen:** Comparison of timelines. 1. Past stem + 았/었던: Happened and was completed in the past. Example: 맞았던 옷 (clothes that used to fit). 2. Present Stem + 던: Happened in the past but was NOT completed or was still happening. Example: 어제 먹던 피자 (pizza I was eating yesterday - leftovers). Image of a pizza.
- **Ji's teaching:** A.V.S + 던 comes from the "recall" marker 더. It describes an action that was happening in the past but was not completed, or something you used to do habitually. Look at the example: "어제 먹던 피자." This is the pizza I *was* eating yesterday (but didn't finish). It’s leftover pizza! On the other hand, V.S + 았던/었던 is for something that happened in the past and is completely finished and disconnected from the present. "예전에 맞았던 옷" means clothes that *used* to fit me, but definitely don't anymore. There is a clear gap between then and now.

### Slide 38 — D.V.S + 던 VS 았/었던
- **On screen:** Comparison for Descriptive Verbs. Rule: Koreans use Past Stem + 던 (았/었던) most of the time for D.Vs. Timeline diagrams show 컸던 (was big, don't know if still big) vs 크던 (was big, likely still big). Image of a small puppy (작았던 강아지) vs a big dog (지금은 커졌네요).
- **Ji's teaching:** For descriptive verbs, we almost always use 았던/었던. "작았던 강아지" (The puppy that was small). It emphasizes that the state of being small is over because now the puppy is big! If you use 크던, it implies the house was big and most likely still is, whereas 컸던 emphasizes the past state without confirming the present.

### Slide 41 — Comparison: Noun보다 더 / 덜
- **On screen:** Title More and less than. Rule: 보다 means "Than." 더 (More), 덜 (Less). Example: "얘는 저보다 키가 (더) 커보여요" (He looks taller than me). Image of children measuring height.

## Teaching Points
- **Compound Verbs with 주다, 보다, and 있다:**
    - **아/어 주다:** Indicates performing an action as a favor for someone. Using 아/어 주세요 is the standard way to make polite requests, sounding much softer than the command form ~세요.
    - **아/어 보다:** Expresses "trying" something in the sense of experiencing it or seeing the outcome (e.g., "try it on"). It is distinct from 노력하다 (to put in effort). The form 아/어본 적(이) 있다 is used to discuss life experiences ("Have you ever...").
    - **아/어 있다:** Describes a continuing state resulting from a completed action. It is strictly used with **intransitive** verbs (e.g., 앉아 있다 - to be seated). For transitive verbs, ~고 있다 is used to describe the state (e.g., 입고 있다 - to be wearing).
- **The Versatile 아/어지다:**
    - Used with Descriptive Verbs to mean "to become" or "to get" (e.g., 피곤해지다).
    - Functions as a passive voice for verbs that do not accept the 이/히/리/기 suffixes (e.g., 만들어지다).
    - Expresses a "Can/Can't" nuance where the ability is dictated by external or natural factors rather than willpower (e.g., 안 일어나져요 - can't get up due to a hangover).
- **Noun Modifiers (Present, Future, Past):**
    - **Present:** Descriptive verbs use 은/ㄴ; Action verbs use 는. 있다/없다 always use 는.
    - **Future:** Uses 을/ㄹ to indicate things yet to happen or possibilities.
    - **Past:**
        - **은/ㄴ:** Simple completed action; may or may not affect the present.
        - **던:** Indicates an action that was habitual or was in progress but not completed (leftovers/interrupted).
        - **았던/었던:** Indicates a past state or action that is completely finished and disconnected from the present.
- **Causative Pairs (나다/내다 and 남다/남기다):**
    - 나다 and 남다 are intransitive (happening naturally or remaining).
    - 내다 and 남기다 are their causative/transitive counterparts (producing an outcome or causing something to be left).
