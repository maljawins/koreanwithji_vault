**Slide 3~4**

Before we look at your notes, let’s quickly recap the self-study I assigned. I asked you to look at 아/어 가다 and 오다, as well as 아/어 놓다 and 두다.

Remember, 가다 and 오다 in Korean aren’t always a direct match for "go" and "come" in English. It’s all about the speaker’s position. When we combine them with other verbs like 가지다 (to carry) or 데리다 (to accompany), they turn into "bring" and "take." For example, 가져가다 means you’re taking something away from where you are now, while 가져오다 means you’re bringing it here to me.

And for 아/어 놓다 and 두다, these are all about preparation. You’re doing an action and leaving it in that state for a reason. Like the example on slide 4: "제가 설거지는 해 놓았으니까..." I’ve finished the dishes (and they are sitting there clean), so now we can just focus on cooking. It implies the action is done and the result is being maintained.

**Slide 5~7**

Now, let’s look at your assignment notes. You did an excellent job applying the D.V.S + 아/어지다 form!

You wrote "말이 길어졌어요." This is a great sentence. Literally, it means "My words became long," but we use it to say "I became talkative" or "The conversation dragged on." It perfectly shows that "becoming" nuance.

On slide 6, you noted 남다 and 남기다. This is a very important distinction. 남다 is intransitive—it just means "to remain" or "to stay." But 남기다 is the causative form, meaning "to leave something behind." So when you say "음식을 많이 남겼어요," it means you left a lot of food because you couldn't finish it. You *caused* the food to remain.

On slide 7, you looked at 아/어 보다 and the intention marker 려고. You can actually end sentences with 려고(요) in spoken Korean. If someone asks, "Why did you buy that book?" you can just say "공부해보려고요" (To try and study). It’s a very natural way to state your purpose without needing a full formal ending every time.

**Slide 8~10**

Let’s look at slide 8. You combined 려 and 다가 to get 하려다(가). This is a really advanced combination! It means "I was trying to do A, but then B happened." Your example, "빨리 걸어가려다(가) 넘어졌어요" (I was trying to walk quickly but then I fell down), is perfect. It shows the interruption of an intention.

On slide 9, you noted that 든 can come after a verb stem. This is used for "no matter" or "whether... or not." Like "좋든 싫든" (Whether you like it or not). It shows a sense of indifference or that the choice doesn't change the outcome.

Then on slide 10, we have (이)라는. This is a shortened form of (이)라고 하는. It’s how we say "something that is called..." For example, "스타크래프트라는 게임" (A game called Starcraft). It’s much more common to use this modifier form than the full phrase in daily conversation.

**Slide 11~13**

On slide 11, you practiced the plain form + 면 for hypothetical situations. "해봤다면" means "If you had ever tried doing it." And "가본다면" is "If you were to go." This is great for talking about dreams or "what if" scenarios.

Slide 12 covers (이)라도. This means "even if it’s just..." or "at least." I love the example with the mom: "다음에는 미리 말이라도 해줘" (At least tell me in advance next time). It’s like saying, "Even if you don't do anything else, at least do this one small thing."

Finally, slide 13 mentions 나다 and 내다. This is your self-study for today, but just a heads up: 나다 is things happening naturally (intransitive), while 내다 is you producing an outcome (transitive/causative). We’ll see this a lot with words like "results" or "money."

**Slide 14~16**

Alright, let’s get back to our compound verbs. We’ve talked about 아/어 있다 before, but I want to reinforce the restrictions.

Remember, 아/어 있다 describes a state that continues after an action is finished. It’s like the "-en" in "broken." But here’s the catch: it usually only works with **intransitive verbs**.

Look at slide 16. You can’t say "문을 닫아있다" (X). 닫다 is transitive—you close the door. In Korean, you can't "be in the state of having closed the door" using the active verb. Instead, you have to use the passive/intransitive version: 닫히다. So, "문이 닫혀있다" (O) is the correct way to say "The door is closed."

If you want to use a transitive verb to show a state, you actually use ~고 있다. For example, "옷을 입고 있다." Even though you finished putting the clothes on, we use the "ongoing" form to show you are currently wearing them. It’s a bit of a quirk in Korean logic, but if you remember "Intransitive for 아/어 있다" and "Transitive for 고 있다," you’ll be fine.

**Slide 17~19**

Next is 아/어 주다. This is one of the most common forms you’ll hear. It means doing something as a favor for someone.

In English, we just say "I bought lunch." But in Korean, if I bought it *for you*, I’d say "사 줬어요." It adds a layer of kindness and relationship to the sentence.

When we make requests, we use 아/어 주세요. This is much more polite than just using the honorific command ~세요. If you tell a taxi driver "공항으로 가세요," it sounds like a direct order: "GO to the airport." But if you say "공항으로 가 주세요," it’s like "Please do me the favor of going to the airport." It’s much softer.

Let’s look at slide 18 and 19. 도와주다 (to help) and 알려주다 (to let someone know) are so common that we almost always learn them as single words. Notice how we use 좀 to soften the tone. "물 좀 주세요" isn't just "Give me some water"; it’s "Could you please give me some water?" It makes the request feel less demanding.

**Slide 20~22**

Continuing with 주다 verbs on slides 20 to 22.

빌려주다 means "to lend." It’s 빌리다 (to borrow) + 주다 (to give). So you are "giving the act of borrowing" to someone else.

보내주다 is "to send for someone," but it can also mean "to let someone go." Like in the example: "너무 피곤해서요. 이제 집에 좀 보내주세요." (I'm so tired. Please let me go home now.)

And 보여주다 is "to show." It comes from 보이다 (to be seen). Again, showing is an act of giving information or a sight to someone else. Notice the example "샘플은 안 보여주셨습니다." Here, the 은 marker implies contrast—maybe they showed the price or the catalog, but *as for the sample*, they didn't show it.

**Slide 23~26**

Now let’s talk about 아/어 보다. This is often translated as "to try," but I want you to be careful. It doesn't mean "to put in effort"—that’s 노력하다.

아/어 보다 means "to do something and see what happens" or "to experience something."

If you’re at a shop, the clerk might say "입어 보세요" (Try it on). They mean "Wear it and see how it looks."

Because this form is about experience, we use it to say "I have done something before." This leads us to slide 26: 아/어본 적(이) 있다/없다.

This is the "Have you EVER..." form. While "한국에 가봤어?" is a casual "Have you been to Korea?", "한국에 가본 적 있어?" puts a much stronger emphasis on the experience itself. It’s like asking if that experience exists in your life’s history.

**Slide 27**

Here is a very important one: 아/어 보이다.

This is the form that actually means "to seem" or "to look like." Remember how I told you last time that 아/어하다 does *not* mean "to seem"? This is the one you were looking for!

It only attaches to **Descriptive Verbs**.
행복하다 (to be happy) -> 행복해 보이다 (to look happy).
다르다 (to be different) -> 달라 보이다 (to look different).

Look at the example: "SNS에서는 모두가 행복해 보이지만..." On social media, everyone *looks* happy, but the truth is different. This is about your visual impression of a state.

**Slide 28~30**

Now let’s look at 아/어지다. This is a powerhouse in Korean grammar with three main usages.

First, "to become" or "to get." We use this with descriptive verbs to show a change in state. "피곤해지다" (to get tired), "무거워지다" (to get heavy).

Second, it acts as a passive voice for verbs that don't fit the 이/리/히/기 pattern. "만들어지다" (to be made), "지워지다" (to be erased).

Third, and this is really cool, it’s used for "Can and Can't" when the reason is an external factor or a natural state. Look at slide 28: "숙취 때문에 아침에 안 일어나져요." It’s not that I’m choosing not to get up; it’s that because of this hangover, my body just *won't be gotten up*. It’s a very nuanced way to express inability.

And on slide 30, we have 고 싶어지다. This means "to start wanting to." You didn't want it before, but now the feeling is growing. "맥주가 마시고 싶어지네" (Now I feel like having a beer).

**Slide 31~34**

Let’s do a quick recap of modifiers. Think of the verb as a branch on a tree. When we want to describe a noun, the verb has to change its shape to "hook" onto that noun.

For the **Present Tense**:
Descriptive verbs take 은/ㄴ (예쁜, 작은).
Action verbs take 는 (가는, 먹는).
And remember, 있다 and 없다 always take 는 (있는, 없는).

For the **Future Tense** (Slide 32):
We use the 을/ㄹ branch. This refers to things that haven't happened yet or possibilities. "할 것" (thing to do), "읽을 책" (book to read).

These are the foundations for the more complex modifiers we’re about to see.

**Slide 35~38**

This is the "Past Tense Modifier" section, and it’s where things get interesting. Korean has three ways to describe a noun in the past, and they each have a different "timeline" vibe.

1. **A.V.S + 은/ㄴ**: This simply indicates a completed action. It might still be relevant now, or it might not. "내가 입은 옷" could be the clothes I put on this morning and am still wearing, or it could be the clothes I wore yesterday. Context tells the story.

2. **A.V.S + 던**: This comes from the "recall" marker 더. It describes an action that was **happening** in the past but was **not completed**, or something you used to do habitually. Look at slide 37: "어제 먹던 피자." This is the pizza I *was* eating yesterday (but didn't finish). It’s leftover pizza!

3. **V.S + 았던/었던**: This is for something that happened in the past and is **completely finished and disconnected** from the present. "예전에 맞았던 옷" means clothes that *used* to fit me, but definitely don't anymore. There is a clear gap between then and now.

For descriptive verbs (Slide 38), we almost always use 았던/었던. "작았던 강아지" (The puppy that was small). It emphasizes that the state of being small is over because now the puppy is big!

**Slide 39~42**

We’re almost at the end! For your self-study, I want you to look at 나다 vs 내다 and comparisons.

나다 is for things that happen or occur naturally (like a smell or a sound). 내다 is when you produce something (like a result or paying money).

For comparisons (Slide 41), we use the particle 보다. It means "than." "저보다 키가 커요" (Taller than me). Notice that we often add 더 (more) for emphasis, but it’s sometimes assumed.

And slide 42 introduces 만큼, which means "as much as." This changes depending on the modifier (present, past, future). "먹는 만큼" (as much as you eat), "했던 만큼" (as much as you did). Try to see if you can find these in your listening practice!
