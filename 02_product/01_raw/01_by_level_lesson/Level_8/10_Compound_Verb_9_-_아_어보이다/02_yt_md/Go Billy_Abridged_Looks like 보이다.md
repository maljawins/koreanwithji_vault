## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the grammar form used to express how something or someone appears based on visual evidence. This lesson focuses primarily on the structure `Descriptive Verb Stem + 아/어보이다`, which translates naturally to "looks like" or "appears to be" in English. The instructor emphasizes that while the English translation is often "looks," the literal meaning is rooted in the passive verb `보이다 (to be seen)`.

The learning arc begins with the basic conjugation rules for descriptive verbs, moves into the specific nuances of when to use this form versus other "thinking" or "guessing" forms, and concludes with how to handle action verbs and nouns. A key takeaway is that this form is specifically for physical appearances that are visible to the eyes, rather than abstract ideas or plans.

---

### The Basic Form: 아/어보이다
To create this form, you take a descriptive verb and conjugate it into the `아/어` form, just as you would for the polite present tense, but without adding `요`. You then attach the verb `보이다`. For example, the verb `작다 (to be small)` conjugates to `작아`, resulting in `작아 보이다 (to look small / to appear small)`. The instructor explains that `보이다` is the passive form of `보다 (to see)`, so the literal meaning is "to be seen as [adjective]."

This form can be used in various tenses by conjugating the final `보이다` part. In the present tense, it becomes `보여요`; in the past tense, it is `보였어요 (looked like / appeared)`; and in the future tense, it is `보일 거예요 (will look like / will appear)`.

---

### Usage Nuances and Physicality
A critical distinction made in the lesson is that `아/어보이다` must describe something physically visible. In English, we might say "That looks like a good plan," but because a plan is an abstract concept and not something you see with your eyes, you cannot use `아/어보이다` in Korean for that context. It is strictly for describing the physical appearance of a noun.

The instructor also points out that while this form is natural, it is often optional. In many cases where an English speaker says "looks like," a Korean speaker might use a different form to express their thought or guess. For example, instead of saying `맛있어 보여요 (it looks delicious)`, one might say `맛있겠네요 (it must be delicious)` or `맛있을 것 같아요 (I think it will be delicious)`. Similarly, for "you look tired," options include `피곤해 보여요 (you physically look tired)`, `피곤하나 봐 (it seems you are tired)`, or `피곤하겠다 (you must be tired)`. Use `아/어보이다` specifically when you want to emphasize that the physical appearance itself shows a certain characteristic.

---

### Alternative Form: 게 보이다
The instructor briefly introduces an alternative structure: `Descriptive Verb Stem + 게 보이다`. Using the previous example, `작다` would become `작게 보이다`. While this carries the exact same meaning as `아/어보이다`, the instructor notes that it is much less common in everyday speech and writing.

---

### Restrictions and Noun Comparisons
The verbs `이다 (to be)` and `아니다 (to not be)` cannot be used with this form. You cannot say `이어 보이다` or `아니어 보이다`. To say something "looks like [Noun]," you must use the particle `처럼 (like)` or `같이 (like)`. The structure is `Noun + 처럼 보이다`. For example, `다른 사람처럼 보여요` means "You look like a different person." While `같이` can replace `처럼`, it is less common in this specific construction.

---

### Handling Action Verbs and Complex Thoughts
If you want to use the "looks like" meaning with an action verb (e.g., "It looks like I can't do it"), you cannot attach `아/어보이다` directly to the action verb. Instead, you must use the `~것 같다` form (which means "it seems like" or "I think") and then treat `같다` as the descriptive verb. This results in the combined form `~것 같아 보이다`.

Example:
> **혼자서는 다 못할 것 같아 보였어요.** / It looked like I wouldn't be able to do it all by myself.
