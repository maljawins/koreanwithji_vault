## Teaching Points

### Overview
This review session focuses on several key verb structures in Korean: the favor-giving marker `아/어 주다`, the experiential and trial marker `아/어 보다`, the descriptive appearance marker `아/어 보이다`, and the structure for past experiences `ㄴ/은 적이 있다/없다`. The instructor uses a quiz-based format to guide students through translating English sentences into Korean, emphasizing the nuances of particle usage and conjugation rules.

The lesson is structured around three distinct quiz files. The first covers favors and actions done for others, the second focuses on trying new things and how things appear, and the third deals with specific past experiences. Throughout the session, the instructor provides detailed explanations on why certain particles like `에게` or `을/를` are chosen based on the specific verb, and how honorific forms like `드리다` (the honorific of `주다`) and `께서` (the honorific of `이/가`) are integrated into these complex structures.

---

### Doing Favors with 아/어 주다
The instructor explains that `아/어 주다` is used when an action is performed as a favor for someone else. When translating "I want to give you a hug," the verb `안다 (to hug)` is combined with `주다` to become `안아 주다`. The instructor notes a common point of confusion regarding particles: while `주다` (to give) usually takes the particle `에게` for the recipient, when it is combined with another verb to form a compound favor verb, the recipient often becomes the direct object of that action, thus taking the object marking particle `을/를`. For example, "hug the middle-aged woman" is expressed as `아주머니를 안아 주고 싶어요`.

However, this rule is not universal and depends heavily on the base verb. For the verb `말하다 (to tell/speak)`, the recipient still takes `에게` because in Korean, one "speaks to" someone rather than "speaking someone." Thus, "We should tell Jeff" becomes `제프에게 말해줘야 해요`. The instructor also introduces `알려주다 (to let someone know)` as a helpful alternative for "to tell" in the context of providing information.

Other examples of `아/어 주다` include:
- **Showing:** `보여주다` (from `보이다` + `주다`). "I don't want to show you my picture" is `너에게 내 사진을 보여주고 싶지 않아`.
- **Taking a photo:** `찍어 주다` (from `찍다` + `주다`). "Can you take a picture for us?" is `사진을 찍어 주시겠어요?`.
- **Lending:** `빌려주다` (from `빌리다` + `주다`). While `빌리다` means "to borrow," adding `주다` changes the meaning to "to lend."
- **Making:** `만들어 주다` (from `만들다` + `주다`). "My mom made it for me" is `엄마가 만들어 줬어요`.

---

### Trying and Experiences with 아/어 보다
The structure `아/어 보다` is used to express trying an action or having the experience of doing something. The instructor emphasizes that while English uses the general word "try" for many situations, Korean requires specific verbs. For example, "try Korean food" is `한국 음식을 먹어 보다` (try eating), and "try this dress on" is `이 드레스를 입어 보다` (try wearing).

The instructor highlights the distinction between different "wearing" verbs: `입다` is used for clothes, while `신다` is used for footwear. Therefore, "Try these shoes on" is `이 신발을 신어 봐요`. When asking about general travel experiences, such as "How many times have you been to Seoul?", the verb `가다 (to go)` is used: `서울에 몇 번 가봤어요?`.

An important stylistic note is the use of the adverb `한 번 (once/one time)` with `아/어 보다`. In this context, it doesn't literally mean "exactly one time" but serves to emphasize the attempt or the suggestion to try something, as in `그 노래를 한번 들어보세요 (Try listening to that song)`.

---

### Appearances with 아/어 보이다
When an adjective is combined with `보이다`, it expresses how something or someone looks. The adjective must be conjugated into its `아/어` form before adding `보이다`. The instructor provides the example "My grandmother looks healthy," which translates to `할머니께서 건강해 보이셔서 행복해요`. 

The instructor also introduces the structure `아/어 지다`, which means "to become" or "to turn into" a certain state. This is used to describe changes over time. For instance, "Korean is getting more difficult" is expressed as `한국어가 더 어려워지고 있어요`. The adverb `점점 (gradually)` is often used with this structure to emphasize a step-by-step change.

---

### Past Experiences with ㄴ/은 적이 있다/없다
This structure specifically refers to whether one has the experience of doing something in the past. It is formed by taking the verb **stem** and adding `ㄴ 적` (if no final consonant) or `은 적` (if there is a final consonant), followed by `있다` (to have) or `없다` (to not have).

The instructor clarifies a common mistake: this structure uses the verb stem, not the present conjugated form.
- "I have never learned a foreign language": `외국어를 배운 적이 없어요`.
- "I have never had an argument with Jeff": `제프랑 싸운 적이 없어요`.

When the action involves someone deserving respect, the honorific version of the verb should be used. For "I have helped Jeff's grandfather," instead of `도와준 적이 있어요`, the more polite form is `도와드린 적이 있어요`, using `드리다` instead of `주다`.

---

### Conjunction Review and "Conglish" Notes
The lesson concludes with a review of conjunctions like `~어도 (even if/still)`, `~지만 (but)`, and the difference between `~으니까` and `~어서` for expressing reasons. The instructor explains that `~으니까` is preferred when the following clause is an imperative (command) or a suggestion, such as `음식이 많이 있으니까 맛있게 드세요 (There is plenty of food, so please enjoy)`.

A brief cultural note is provided on "Conglish" (Korean-style English): in Korea, a laptop is called a `노트북 (notebook)`, while an actual paper notebook is called a `공책`.
