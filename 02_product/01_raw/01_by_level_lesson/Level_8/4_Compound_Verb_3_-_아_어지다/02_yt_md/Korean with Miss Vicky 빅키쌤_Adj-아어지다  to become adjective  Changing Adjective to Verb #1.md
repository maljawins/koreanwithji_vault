## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces the grammatical structure used to transform adjectives into verbs to express a change in state. This lesson focuses specifically on the auxiliary verb form **아/어지다**, which translates to "to become [adjective]" or "to get [adjective]." The core concept is that a state was not a certain way before, but it has transitioned into that state now.

The lesson follows a structured arc: first, the basic conjugation rules for regular and irregular adjectives; second, practical sentence building across different tenses; third, special cases involving adjectives ending in **있다** or **없다**; and finally, a distinction between adjectives that can use this form and verbs that already inherently contain the meaning of "becoming" or "arising."

---

### Basic Conjugation and Meaning of 아/어지다

The structure is formed by taking an adjective stem, conjugating it into the present informal form (adding **아** or **어**), and then attaching the auxiliary verb **지다**. This creates a new compound verb. Because the resulting word is a verb, it can be further conjugated into various tenses.

For example, the adjective **따뜻하다 (to be warm)** becomes **따뜻해지다 (to become warm)**. The instructor demonstrates this in a sentence: **봄이 되니까 따뜻해졌어요**, which means "Since spring has come, it became warm." Here, the past tense **졌어요** is used to show the completed change.

Another example is **맵다 (to be spicy)**. Following the **ㅂ** irregular rule, it becomes **매워지다 (to become spicy)**. The instructor provides a future tense example: **고추장을 넣으면 더 매워질 거예요**, meaning "If you put in red pepper paste, it will become spicier." The word **더 (more)** is often used with this grammar to emphasize the increase in the state.

---

### Expressing Gradual Change and Desires

The instructor introduces the grammar **-ㄹ/을수록**, which means "the more [A] happens, the more [B] happens," to show how a state changes over time. Using the adjective **슬프다 (to be sad)**, which conjugates to **슬퍼지다 (to become sad)**, she creates the sentence: **영화가 뒤로 갈수록 슬퍼져요**. This translates to "As the movie heads towards the end (the further back it goes), it becomes sad."

The lesson also covers how to use this with **건강하다 (to be healthy)**. To say "to want to become healthy," you combine the "become" form with the desire marker: **건강해지고 싶다**. A full advice sentence is provided: **건강해지고 싶으면 운동을 주기적으로 하고 소식하세요**, meaning "If you want to become healthy, exercise regularly and eat lightly." The instructor notes that **소식 (小食)** literally means "small eating."

---

### The Case of 낫다 (To be better)

A critical distinction is made for the word **낫다**. It has two meanings: a verb meaning "to recover" and an adjective meaning "to be better." Only the adjective form can combine with **아/어지다**. Following the **ㅅ** irregular rule, the stem becomes **나아**, resulting in **나아지다 (to become better/to improve)**.

The instructor compares two sentences to clarify the usage. To say a cold has recovered, you use the simple verb: **감기가 나았어요**. However, to say a skill has improved, you use the "become" form: **한국어 실력이 예전보다 나아졌어요 (My Korean skill has gotten better compared to before)**. She also mentions that **늘다 (to increase/improve)** is a very common alternative for skills: **한국어 실력이 많이 늘었어요**.

---

### Using 아/어지다 with -고 싶다

The expression **-고 싶다 (to want to)** is grammatically treated as an adjective in Korean. Therefore, it can be combined with **아/어지다** to form **-고 싶어지다**, which means "to start wanting to" or "to begin craving." This describes a change from not wanting something to suddenly wanting it.

Example sentences include: **집에만 있으면 안 좋은 음식을 먹고 싶어져요 (If I only stay at home, I start wanting to eat bad food)** and **갑자기 맥주를 마시고 싶어지네요 (I suddenly crave/want to drink beer)**. The instructor explains the etymology of **맥주 (beer)**, noting **맥** comes from barley and **주** refers to alcohol, similar to **소주** or **양주 (Western liquor)**.

---

### Adjectives with 있다 and 없다

Adjectives ending in **있다** or **없다** follow specific patterns when expressing a change in state.

For **있다** adjectives:
- **맛있다 (delicious)** becomes **맛있어지다 (to become delicious)**. Example: **설탕을 조금 더 넣으면 맛있어질 거예요 (If you put in a little more sugar, it will become tasty)**.
- **재미있다 (fun/interesting)** becomes **재미있어지다 (to become fun)**.
- **Caution:** Some **있다** adjectives sound awkward with **아/어지다**. For **자신감이 있다 (to be confident)** and **인기가 있다 (to be popular)**, it is more natural to use the verb **생기다 (to arise/come into existence)**. Instead of **자신감이 있어지다**, say **자신감이 생기다**. For popularity, **인기가 많아지다 (to become many/increase)** is the most natural expression.

For **없다** adjectives:
- Unlike **있다**, the base word **없다** frequently combines with this grammar to form **없어지다**, which means "to disappear" or "to be lost." Example: **고양이가 없어졌어요 (My cat disappeared)**.
- Other examples include **재미없어지다 (to become boring)**, **맛없어지다 (to become untasty)**, and **자신감이 없어지다 (to lose confidence)**.

---

### Verbs that Cannot Use 아/어지다

The instructor emphasizes that **아/어지다** is strictly for adjectives. Some words that feel like adjectives in English are actually verbs in Korean and thus cannot take this form because they already describe a change or an arising state.

- **화나다 (to get angry):** This is a verb (**화** anger + **나다** to arise). You do not say **화나지다**. You simply say **화가 났어요 (I got angry)**.
- **질투하다 / 질투나다 (to get jealous):** These are verbs. Note that **부럽다 (to be envious/jealous in a positive way)** is an adjective, so you *can* say **부러워지다**.
- **걱정하다 (to worry):** This is a verb. To say "I got worried," use **걱정됐어요 (from 걱정되다)** rather than trying to attach **아/어지다**.
- **배 아프다 (to be jealous - idiomatic):** Literally "stomach hurts," this is used figuratively for jealousy. Since **아프다** is an adjective, you can say **배가 아파지다** to mean "to become jealous."
