## Lesson Overview
This lesson marks a significant transition for Amira, moving from beginner-level Korean into intermediate concepts. The primary focus is a deep dive into the various types of Korean verbs, specifically focusing on the distinctions between transitive and intransitive verbs, the formation and usage of the passive voice, and an introduction to causative verbs. Ji emphasizes that understanding whether a verb can take an object is the foundational concept for mastering these advanced structures.

The second half of the lesson explores compound verbs, which are formed by combining two verbs using the [Stem + 아/어 + Verb] structure. Ji covers several essential patterns, including ~아/어하다 for describing the feelings of others, ~아/어 있다 for expressing a completed state of action, and ~아/어 주다 for performing actions as a favor for someone else. The lesson also introduces ~아/어 보다 for experiences and ~아/어 보이다 for appearances, while providing a framework for self-studying directional and preparatory compound verbs.

Throughout the session, Ji integrates detailed feedback from Amira’s previous assignments, correcting subtle spelling errors and refining her understanding of nuances like the difference between realization (~구나/군요) and confirmation (~지). The lesson concludes by reinforcing the "Comprehensible Input" method, encouraging Amira to use intermediate-level podcasts and videos to bridge the gap between reading speed and natural speaking speed.

### Slide 16 — Transitive & Intransitive Verbs
- **On screen:** "Can a verb take an object?" Transitive (타동사) can take an object (e.g., "raise" a glass). Intransitive (자동사) cannot (e.g., "rise"). A screenshot of Naver Dictionary shows how to identify these. A note states: "ALL descriptive verbs are INTRANSITIVE." An image of Leonardo DiCaprio raising a glass is used as an example.
- **Ji's teaching:** Now, we're going to talk about "Types of Verbs." To understand this, we first need to understand this core idea: Can a verb take an object? Think about "raise" and "rise." You can “raise” a glass, with glass being the object, but you can’t "rise" a glass. It can only just “rise” on its own. This is the difference between Transitive verbs and Intransitive verbs. Transitive verbs can take an object. Intransitive verbs can't; they describe an action that happens on its own, or a state of being. So, all descriptive verbs are intransitive. If you go to the Naver dictionary and switch it to 국어사전 (Korean dictionary), it will tell you if the verb is 타동사 (transitive) or 자동사 (intransitive). ### Slide 17 — Passive Voice
- **On screen:** Comparison of English and Korean passive voice. Active: "A dog bit the kid" (개가 애를 물었어요). Passive: "The kid was bitten by a dog" (애가 개한테 물렸어요). Note: 물다 (To bite) vs 물리다 (To be bitten). A box explains that the word before the verb is typically the most important information.
- **Ji's teaching:** Now that we understand transitive and intransitive verbs, let's talk about Passive Voice. In English, we usually use "be" verbs and add suffixes like "-en" or "-ed," like "The kid was bitten." But in Korean, the verb itself actually changes its dictionary form to become passive, like 물다 and 물리다. In passive voice, the particles change too! In active voice, the subject does the action. But in passive voice, the subject is the "RECEIVER" of the action. Now that the receiver is the subject, it’s typically marked by the subject marker, 이/가. On a side note, you could also say "개한테 애가 물렸어요." Do you remember when we talked about word order, and how the word closer to the verb is usually the most important information? This has a slightly different nuance. This might be said in response to "누가 물렸어요?" (Who was bitten?), emphasizing 애가 (the kid).

### Slide 18 — Particle: By
- **On screen:** 1. 에게, 한테 for People & Animals (e.g., 사랑받다). 2. 에 for Things, Organizations, Insects (e.g., 공격당하다, 쏘이다). Formal/Written: 에 의해(서).
- **Ji's teaching:** When we use passive voice, we often want to say who or what caused the action, right? In English, we use "by." In Korean, it depends on whether the "doer" is a person/animal or a thing. For people and animals, you use 에게 or 한테. For example, "저는 많은 분들에게 사랑받고 있어요" (I am being loved by many people). 사랑하다 (to love) becomes 사랑받다 (to be loved). For things, organizations, or even insects, you use 에. Like, "최근 이란이 미국에 공격당했어요" (Lately, Iran was attacked by the U.S.) or "벌에 쏘이면 병원에 바로 가야 합니다" (If you're ever stung by a bee...). There's also 에 의해서, which is very formal and mostly used in writing. You'll rarely hear it in everyday speech.

### Slide 19 — Passive Verb Principles
- **On screen:** "Only transitive verbs can be turned into passive verbs." Explains why 웃다 (intransitive) cannot be passive. "In principle, passive verbs are intransitive" (cannot take 을/를).
- **Ji's teaching:** Here's a super important rule: Only transitive verbs can be turned into passive verbs. Think about it logically: In an active sentence, the object receives the action. When we change it to passive, that original object becomes the subject. An intransitive verb doesn't have an object, so there's nothing to become the subject! For example, 웃다 (to laugh) is intransitive. You can't "laugh something," so you can't say "Something is laughed by you." It just doesn't make sense! And once a verb is turned into a passive verb, it technically becomes intransitive “in principle.” You’ll see what I mean by “in principle” later today. A passive verb cannot take an object marker (을/를). You can't say "애를 물렸어요" (X); you just say "애가 물렸어요" (O).

### Slide 20 — Passive Voice Usage: Can & Can't
- **On screen:** Passive voice is used for "Can & Can't" when it's not the person's fault. Examples: 보다 → 보이다 (visible), 듣다 → 들리다 (audible), 믿다 → 믿기다 (believable). Comparison to 을/ㄹ 수 없다 (lack of ability). Images show a blocked view at a concert and a person with a megaphone.
- **Ji's teaching:** In Korean, we use passive voice to express "can" and "can't"! The difference from using ~(으)ㄹ 수 없다 or 못 is that passive voice puts the focus on the situation rather than the person. It's used when something is impossible because of an external factor, not your lack of ability. Three common examples: 1. 보다 (to see) → 보이다 (to be visible). If someone is blocking your view at a concert, you say "안 보여요!" It's not your fault you can't see; the woman is blocking you! 2. 듣다 (to hear) → 들리다 (to be audible). A person with a megaphone asks, "이제는 들리세요?" (Is it audible now?). 3. 믿다 (to believe) → 믿기다 (to be believable). "진짜? 안 믿겨" (Really? It's unbelievable). It's not your fault you can't believe it; the info itself is just too wild.

### Slide 21 — Passive Verb Formation Patterns
- **On screen:** Three patterns: 1. Stem + [이/리/히/기]. 2. Noun + 하다 → [되다/받다/당하다]. 3. Stem + 아/어 + 지다. Notes: No fixed rules, learn as distinct verbs.
- **Ji's teaching:** I have to be honest: this topic is difficult because there are no clear, fixed rules. I highly recommend learning these verbs one by one. However, I can show you some common patterns to help you infer the meaning. Think of them as guidelines. There are generally three main patterns. We'll cover the 이/리/히/기 pattern and the Noun + 하다 changes today. The third pattern, 아/어지다, we'll cover in a separate lesson because it's super versatile.

### Slide 22 — Stem + 이다
- **On screen:** Table of verbs ending in [no 받침, ㅎ, ㅍ, ㄲ]. Examples: 보다 → 보이다, 쓰다 → 쓰이다, 쌓다 → 쌓이다, 덮다 → 덮이다, 섞다 → 섞이다.
- **Ji's teaching:** The 이다 pattern is typically used when the verb stem does not end in a 받침, or for stems ending in ㅎ, ㅍ, or ㄲ. For now, just focus on recognizing these patterns.

### Slide 23 — Stem + 리다
- **On screen:** Table of verbs ending in [ㄹ, ㄷ, 르]. Examples: 열다 → 열리다, 듣다 → 들리다, 부르다 → 불리다.
- **Ji's teaching:** The 리다 pattern applies when the active verb stem ends with a ㄹ batchim, like 열다 (to open) becoming 열리다. You'll also see this with some ㄷ irregular verbs (where ㄷ changes to ㄹ) and some 르 irregular verbs.

### Slide 24 — Stem + 히다
- **On screen:** Table of verbs ending in [ㄱ, ㅈ, ㅂ, ㄷ - regular]. Examples: 먹다 → 먹히다, 꽂다 → 꽂히다, 잡다 → 잡히다, 닫다 → 닫히다.
- **Ji's teaching:** The 히다 pattern commonly attaches to verb stems ending in ㄱ, ㅈ, ㅂ, and regular ㄷ verbs. For example, 먹다 (to eat) becomes 먹히다 (to be eaten) and 닫다 (to close) becomes 닫히다 (to be closed).

### Slide 25 — Stem + 기다
- **On screen:** Table of verbs ending in other batchims. Examples: 감다 → 감기다, 끊다 → 끊기다, 안다 → 안기다, 씻다 → 씻기다, 쫓다 → 쫓기다, 믿다 → 믿기다.
- **Ji's teaching:** Finally, the 기다 pattern is for pretty much all the rest of the batchim-ending verbs that don't fit the other categories, like 안다 (to hug) becoming 안기다.

### Slide 26 — Noun + 하다 Verbs
- **On screen:** Replacing 하다 with: 1. 되다 (To become - for things you can't "receive" like preparation/준비). 2. 받다 (To receive - for things like love/사랑). 3. 당하다 (To undergo against one's will - for things like being fired/해고). Note: 되다 is intransitive, but 받다/당하다 are transitive.
- **Ji's teaching:** Turning Noun + 하다 verbs is easier. It’s about: “Is the noun something you can directly 'receive'?” If the answer is no, like 준비 (preparation) or 취소 (cancellation), you use 되다. If you can “receive” it, like 사랑 (love) or 존경 (respect), use 받다. 당하다 is for things against your will, like 해고당하다 (to be fired). Note that 받다 and 당하다 are actually transitive verbs themselves, which is why I said "in principle" passive verbs are intransitive—these are the exceptions.

### Slide 27 — Intro to Causative Verbs
- **On screen:** "To CAUSE someone/something to [VERB]." Example: 웃다 (intransitive) → 웃기다 (transitive). "My friend made me laugh" (친구가 나를 너무 웃겨서...). List of examples: 죽이다, 알리다, 덥히다, 숨기다, 깨우다, 낮추다.
- **Ji's teaching:** Let's briefly touch on Causative Verbs. This is an advanced topic. A causative verb causes someone or something to do the action. For example, 웃다 is intransitive, but you can make someone laugh, so it becomes 웃기다. 웃기다 is now a transitive verb! Look at the example: "친구가 나를 너무 웃겨서 배가 아파요." You see the object marker 나를? Causative verbs look very similar to passive verbs, so it's best to learn them separately. Just be aware of the concept for now!

### Slide 28 — Compound Verbs
- **On screen:** [Stem] + 아/어 + [Another verb]. Examples: 좋아하다, 싫어하다. Focus on 알아보다 (To recognize OR To research). Context is key.
- **Ji's teaching:** Compound verbs are basically two verbs combined to create a new meaning. To use them, you just conjugate the second verb. There are a lot of these, and they aren't always logical, but if you know the base verbs, you can often guess the meaning. Take 알아보다 (알다 + 보다). It can mean "to recognize" (to know what you see) or "to research" (to look in order to know). You can easily tell which one it is by context. An infant "recognizes" mom; a student "researches" a book.

### Slide 29 — ~아/어하다: Describing Others' Feelings
- **On screen:** D.V.S + 아/어 + 하다. Used because "I can never KNOW how others feel." 1st person: 슬퍼요. 2nd person: 슬프세요? (Question) / 슬퍼하지 마세요 (Command). 3rd person: 슬퍼해요. Also applies to ~고 싶어하다.
- **Ji's teaching:** We use this form when talking about other people's feelings. In Korean, we assume we can't truly "know" what another person is feeling. 슬프다 means to be sad. If someone else is sad, we change it to 슬퍼하다, which is an action verb. It doesn't mean "to seem"; it means "to act" or "to behave" sad. For yourself, say "슬퍼요." For others, say "슬퍼해요." This is the same reason you can't use ~고 싶다 for a third person. You must say "지수는 김밥을 먹고 싶어해요" because you are observing her behavior.

### Slide 30 — ~아/어하다: Beyond Feelings
- **On screen:** 예뻐하다/귀여워하다 (to adore). 미워하다 (to hate - affectionate/love-hate). 좋아하다/싫어하다 logic. Lyric from Red Velvet's "Dumb Dumb": "미워도 싫지가 않잖아."
- **Ji's teaching:** There are cases where 아/어하다 isn't just for emotions. 예뻐하다 and 귀여워하다 mean "to adore" or "to cherish." This is also the logic behind 좋아하다 and 싫어하다 (to do the act of liking/disliking). 미워하다 (from 밉다) means "to hate," but it has an affectionate or "love-hate" feel. It's the weakest level of hate, used for people you care about. Kids say "엄마 미워!" but they don't actually hate her. The Red Velvet lyric "미워도 싫지가 않잖아" perfectly shows how you can "hate" someone without truly disliking them.

### Slide 31 — ~아/어하다: Example Dialogue 1
- **On screen:** A: 민준이가 새 장난감을 너무 좋아해... B: 그 로봇 몇 달 전부터 갖고 싶어 했잖아...
- **Ji's teaching:** Try to understand the meaning from context. Speaker A uses 좋아해 for Minjun's feeling as observed. Speaker B uses 갖고 싶어 했잖아 to describe how they observed Minjun acting like he wanted the robot for months.

### Slide 32 — ~아/어하다: Example Dialogue 2
- **On screen:** A: 제인이... 아쉬워하는 것 같더라. B: 맞아. 엄청 오고 싶어 했는데...
- **Ji's teaching:** 아쉬워하는 것 같더라 shows how speaker A observed Jane's demeanor, making her seem regretful. 오고 싶어 했는데 again highlights that speaker B observed Jane acting like she wanted to come.

### Slide 33 — ~아/어 있다: Completed State
- **On screen:** A.V.S + 아/어 + 있다 (계시다). Meaning: Completed action that continues to exist. 살다 → 살아있다 (To be alive). Example: "While grandmother is still alive..." (살아계실 때). Poster for the movie #Alive (#살아있다).
- **Ji's teaching:** This form means an action has been completed and continues to "exist" in that state. It's like the "-en" ending in English (e.g., "broken"). 살다 becomes 살아있다 (to be alive). 살아남다 (to survive) is also a compound verb meaning "to live and remain." The phrase "꼭 살아남아야 한다" means "You MUST survive!"

### Slide 34 — ~고 있다 vs ~아/어 있다
- **On screen:** ~고 있다 (ongoing action) vs ~아/어 있다 (resulting state). Examples: Window breaking (깨지고 있어요) vs. Window is broken (깨져있어요). Baby sitting down (앉고 있어요) vs. Baby is seated (앉아있어요).
- **Ji's teaching:** Let's compare these. "창문이 깨지고 있어요" means the glass is breaking right now. "창문이 깨져 있어요" means the glass is already broken. Similarly, for sitting: "아기가 앉고 있어요" is the action of sitting down; "아기가 앉아 있어요" means the baby is already seated. In English, you might use "sitting" for both, but Korean distinguishes them.

### Slide 35 — ~아/어 있다: Restrictions
- **On screen:** Can only be used with verbs that can remain in that state (usually intransitive). 문을 닫아있다 (X) vs 문이 닫혀있다 (O). Use ~고 있다 for transitive verbs like wearing clothes (옷을 입고 있다).
- **Ji's teaching:** An important restriction: this form is mostly for intransitive verbs. 닫다 (to close) is transitive, so "닫아있다" (X) makes no sense. You must use the passive/intransitive form: "문이 닫혀있다" (The door is in the state of having been closed). However, for wearing clothes, even though you stay dressed, "입어 있다" (X) sounds awkward. You use "옷을 입고 있다," which can mean both the action and the state.

### Slide 36 — ~아/어 주다: For Someone
- **On screen:** A.V.S + 아/어 + 주다 (주시다/드리다). Meaning: To do for someone as a favor. Examples: 사줬어요, 만들어 주셨어요. Polite requests: ~아/어 주세요 (Please). Comparison: 가세요 (Command) vs 가주세요 (Polite request).
- **Ji's teaching:** This means you’re giving the act of doing "FOR SOMEONE" as a favor. 주다 becomes 주시다 (honorific) or 드리다 (humble). "남자친구가 점심을 사줬어요" means he bought me lunch as a favor. "아/어 주세요" is used for polite requests. "공항으로 가세요" sounds like a command ("GO to the airport"), so we say "공항으로 가 주세요" instead, which is like saying "kindly give the act of going to the airport for me."

### Slide 37 — Common 주다 Verb: 도와주다
- **On screen:** 도와주다 (돕다 + 주다). 돕다 is a ㅂ irregular. Explains how 돕 + 아 becomes 도와. Usage of 좀 as a softener for requests.
- **Ji's teaching:** 도와주다 comes from 돕다 (to help). 돕다 is rarely used alone because helping is naturally "giving." It's a ㅂ irregular: replace ㅂ with 우, add 아, and "우아" said fast becomes "와." "저 좀 도와주세요" means "please help me." What is 좀? It's a shortened 조금 (a little). It softens the tone. "물 주세요" sounds stiff and rude; "물 좀 주세요" is much more polite, like "Could you give me some water, please?"

### Slide 38 — Common 주다 Verb: 알려주다
- **On screen:** 알려주다 (알리다 + 주다). 알리다 is causative of 알다. Meaning: To let someone know / inform. Note on 은/는 vs 이/가: "이게 비밀이에요" means "This is THE secret."
- **Ji's teaching:** 알려주다 comes from 알리다 (to inform), which is the causative of 알다 (to know). Informing is "giving" information. "이건 비밀이에요" uses the topic marker. If you said "이게 비밀이에요" with the subject marker, it would mean "This is THE secret," implying specific context like a secret they've been hiding.

### Slide 39 — Common 주다 Verb: 빌려주다
- **On screen:** 빌려주다 (빌리다 + 주다). Meaning: To lend. Examples: "Could you lend me yours?" (빌려줄 수 있어?), "If you could lend me 2000 won..." (빌려주시면).
- **Ji's teaching:** 빌리다 means to borrow. Combining it with 주다 means "giving the act of borrowing," which is to lend. "네 것 좀 빌려줄 수 있어?" (Could you lend me yours?) or "이천원만 빌려주시면..." (If you could lend me 2000 won...).

### Slide 40 — Common 주다 Verb: 보내주다
- **On screen:** 보내주다 (보내다 + 주다). Meaning: To send / To let go. Example: "Please let me go home" (집에 좀 보내주세요). Reminder: 어 cancels out when met with ㅐ or ㅔ.
- **Ji's teaching:** 보내주다 comes from 보내다 (to send). It can also translate as "to let someone go." For example, "너무 피곤해요. 이제 집에 보내주세요" means "I'm so tired, let me go home."

### Slide 41 — Common 주다 Verb: 보여주다
- **On screen:** 보여주다 (보이다 + 주다). 보이다 is causative of 보다. Meaning: To show. Note: 은 implies contrast (샘플은 안 보여주셨습니다 - "Well, about the sample, they didn't show it...").
- **Ji's teaching:** 보이다 is the causative of 보다, meaning "to show." Showing something is an act of giving, so "보여주다" is much more common than just "보이다." Think of it as one verb. If you say "샘플은 안 보여주셨습니다," the 은 implies contrast—maybe they showed you the price or a photo, but not the actual sample.

### Slide 42 — ~아/어 보다: To Try / Experience
- **On screen:** A.V.S + 아/어 + 보다. Meaning: To do and see how it goes. NOT "to put effort" (노력하다). Used for experiences. Examples: "I tried on the jacket" (입어 봤는데), "Have you eaten Gimbab?" (먹어 봤어?).
- **Ji's teaching:** This is often translated as "to try," but it doesn't mean putting in effort (that's 노력하다). It means to do something and see what it's like. "쟈켓 입어 봤는데" means I wore it to see how it looked. "직원한테 길을 물어볼까요?" means "Should we try asking the staff?" to see what they say. It's very common for experiences. "김밥 먹어 봤어?" (Have you tried Gimbab?).

### Slide 43 — Common 보다 Verb: 물어보다
- **On screen:** 물어보다 (묻다 + 보다). 묻다 is a ㄷ irregular (ㄷ → ㄹ). 여쭤보다 is the humble form. Note: 묻다 is a homonym (to ask, to bury, to get stained).
- **Ji's teaching:** 물어보다 comes from 묻다 (to ask). It's a ㄷ irregular: replace ㄷ with ㄹ and add 어. 여쭈다 is the humble form. We use 보다 because you have to wait and "see" the response. 묻다 has other meanings that are regular verbs: to bury (씨앗을 땅에 묻어 주세요) and to get stained (케첩이 묻었어요).

### Slide 44 — 봐 봐: Three Meanings
- **On screen:** 봐 봐 is common in 반말. 1. Look at that. 2. See? (I told you so). 3. Let me see.
- **Ji's teaching:** This is a super common expression. It has three meanings depending on your tone and delivery: "Look at that," "See? I told you so," or "Let me see."

### Slide 45 — ~아/어본 적(이) 있다/없다
- **On screen:** Emphasis on experience. 적 is a bound noun (experience). Comparison: "Have you been to Korea?" (가봤어?) vs "Have you EVER been to Korea?" (가본 적 있어?). Breakdown of modifiers: 보는, 본, 보던, 볼.
- **Ji's teaching:** This has an almost identical meaning to ~아/어보다 but puts more emphasis on the experience. 적 is a bound noun for "experience" (the regular word is 경험). 본 is the past tense modifier of 보다. "한국에 가본 적 있어?" is like saying "Have you EVER been to Korea?"

### Slide 46 — ~아/어 보이다: To Seem / Look
- **On screen:** D.V.S + 아/어 + 보이다. Meaning: To seem / look like a descriptive verb. Examples: 비싸 보이다, 예뻐 보이다, 행복해 보이다. Note: 보이다 is the passive of 보다. 다르다 is a 르 irregular (달라).
- **Ji's teaching:** THIS is the form that means "to seem" or "to look like," not 아/어하다. "행복해 보이다" means to look happy. "SNS에서는 모두가 행복해 보이지만..." (On social media, everyone seems happy, but they're not). "화장을 하니까 완전히 달라 보이네요" (You look completely different with makeup on!). 달라 comes from 다르다, which is a 르 irregular.

### Slide 47 — Self Study: ~아/어 가다 / 오다
- **On screen:** 가다 & 오다 are used differently than "Go & Come." Examples: 가져가다 (bring THERE) vs 가져오다 (bring HERE).
- **Ji's teaching:** I'd like you to learn this on your own. I'll send a video explaining the conceptual differences between 가다/오다 and English "come/go." Also, look into how "bring" and "take" are expressed using verbs like 가지다 or 챙기다 combined with 가다/오다.

### Slide 48 — Self Study: ~아/어 놓다 / 두다
- **On screen:** 놓다 (to place/put down). 두다 (to keep/leave). Implies completion in preparation for something. Example: "I finished the dishes, so now I just need to cook" (설거지는 해 놓았으니까...).
- **Ji's teaching:** This is also for self-study. These forms imply completion of an action in preparation for something else. "제가 설거지는 해 놓았으니까..." means I've finished the dishes [in preparation for cooking].

### Slide 49 — Comparison: Noun보다 더 / 덜
- **On screen:** 보다 is a particle meaning "Than." 더 (More), 덜 (Less). Example: "He looks taller than me" (저보다 키가 커보여요).
- **Ji's teaching:** 보다 here is a particle meaning "than," not "to see." 더 means more, and 덜 means less. We often omit 더. Instead of saying "덜 커요" (less big), we usually just say the opposite: "더 작아요" (smaller).

## Teaching Points

- **Transitive vs. Intransitive:** Transitive verbs (타동사) take an object (marked by 을/를). Intransitive verbs (자동사) do not. All descriptive verbs are intransitive.
- **Passive Voice Formation:**
    - **이/리/히/기:** Common for pure Korean verbs. Patterns depend on the final consonant (받침) of the stem.
    - **Noun + 하다:** Replaced by 되다 (for non-receivable nouns), 받다 (for receivable nouns like love), or 당하다 (for negative actions against one's will).
    - **Usage:** Passive voice is used to express "can/can't" when the situation is the cause (e.g., 안 보여요 - "It's not visible/I can't see").
- **Compound Verbs [Stem + 아/어 + Verb]:**
    - **~아/어하다:** Used to describe the feelings or desires of a 3rd person (e.g., 슬퍼해요, 싶어해요) because we cannot "know" their internal state, only observe their behavior.
    - **~아/어 있다:** Describes a state resulting from a completed action (e.g., 앉아 있다 - "to be seated"). Usually used with intransitive verbs.
    - **~아/어 주다:** Indicates an action done as a favor for someone. Using ~아/어 주세요 is the standard way to make polite requests.
    - **~아/어 보다:** Means to "try" something out or to have the "experience" of doing something.
    - **~아/어 보이다:** The correct form for "to seem" or "to look like" (e.g., 맛있어 보여요 - "It looks delicious").
- **Nuance and Realization:**
    - **~구나/군요:** Used for realization after thinking or processing information.
    - **~지:** Can be a shortened form of ~지 그랬어 (You should have...).
- **Cultural Vocabulary:** **눈치** is the essential Korean ability to read social cues and "sense" the atmosphere (분위기).
