## Teaching Points

### Overview

This lesson serves as a comprehensive introduction to Korean passive verbs (피동사). The instructor explains that while active verbs describe an action performed by a subject, passive verbs indicate that the subject is the receiver of the action. The class covers the fundamental sentence structures for passive voice, the various suffixes used to transform active verbs into passive ones, and the specific rules governing different verb categories, including transitive, intransitive, and -하다 ending verbs.

The learning arc begins with a comparison between English and Korean passive structures to establish a conceptual foundation. It then moves into the three primary formation rules: adding suffixes (-이, -리, -히, -기), using the -아/어지다 pattern, and utilizing -되다, -받다, or -당하다 for -하다 verbs. Throughout the video, the instructor emphasizes that while there are patterns based on final consonants (받침), many passive forms must be memorized individually due to lack of consistent logic or the existence of exceptions.

---

### Defining the Passive Verb (피동사)

A passive verb describes a situation where the subject receives an action rather than performing it. The instructor uses the example "Mosquitoes bite people" (active) vs. "People are bitten by mosquitoes" (passive). In Korean, the active sentence is **모기는 사람을 물어요** (Mosquitoes bite people). To make this passive, the verb **물다** (to bite) changes to its passive counterpart **물리다** (to be bitten).

The subject of the passive sentence must be the receiver of the action. Therefore, the person becomes the subject: **사람은 모기에 물려요** (People are bitten by mosquitoes). The instructor notes that the passive verb indicates the subject is the receiver, and the "actor" (the one doing the biting) is marked with specific particles.

---

### Passive Sentence Structure and Particles

The basic structure of a Korean passive sentence consists of the Subject (Receiver) marked by **은/는** or **이/가**, followed by the Actor marked by a particle meaning "by," and finally the Passive Verb. There are four main particles used to indicate the actor:

**에게** and **한테** are used when the actor is a person or an animal. **한테** is more colloquial, while **에게** is slightly more formal. **에** is used for non-living things, insects, or large groups like organizations and countries. **에 의해** is a very formal version of "by" used in written or highly official contexts.

---

### Rule 1: Adding Suffixes -이, -리, -히, -기

The first major rule for forming passive verbs is adding one of four suffixes to the active verb stem. While there is no absolute rule, certain patterns exist based on the final sound of the verb stem.

The suffix **-이** is typically added to stems ending in a vowel, or those ending in the consonants **ㅎ**, **ㄲ**, or **ㅍ**. Examples include:
- **보다 (to see) => 보이다 (to be seen)**
- **쓰다 (to write, use) => 쓰이다 (to be used)**
- **놓다 (to place) => 놓이다 (to be placed)**
- **쌓다 (to pile) => 쌓이다 (to be piled)**
- **섞다 (to mix) => 섞이다 (to be mixed)**
- **모으다 (to collect) => 모이다 (to be collected)**
- **묶다 (to tie) => 묶이다 (to be tied)**
- **덮다 (to cover) => 덮이다 (to be covered)**
- **깎다 (to peel, cut) => 깎이다 (to be peeled, cut)**

The suffix **-리** is generally used for stems ending in the consonant **ㄹ**. It also applies to certain irregular verbs that develop an **ㄹ** sound when conjugated. Examples include:
- **열다 (to open) => 열리다 (to be opened)**
- **듣다 (to hear) => 들리다 (to be heard)**
- **부르다 (to call) => 불리다 (to be called)**
- **팔다 (to sell) => 팔리다 (to be sold)**
- **밀다 (to push) => 밀리다 (to be pushed)**
- **풀다 (to untie) => 풀리다 (to be untied)**
- **물다 (to bite) => 물리다 (to be bitten)**
- **썰다 (to slice) => 썰리다 (to be sliced)**

The suffix **-히** is often added to stems ending in **ㄱ**, **ㄷ**, or **ㅂ**. Examples include:
- **먹다 (to eat) => 먹히다 (to be eaten)**
- **닫다 (to close) => 닫히다 (to be closed)**
- **잡다 (to catch) => 잡히다 (to be caught)**
- **뽑다 (to pull out, select) => 뽑히다 (to be pulled out, selected)**
- **얹다 (to put on) => 얹히다 (to be put on)**
- **씹다 (to chew) => 씹히다 (to be chewed)**
- **업다 (to carry on back) => 업히다 (to be carried on back)**
- **밟다 (to step on) => 밟히다 (to be stepped on)**

The suffix **-기** is commonly used for stems ending in **ㄴ**, **ㅁ**, **ㅅ**, or **ㅈ**. Examples include:
- **안다 (to hug) => 안기다 (to be hugged)**
- **끊다 (to cut, disconnect) => 끊기다 (to be cut, disconnected)**
- **쫓다 (to chase) => 쫓기다 (to be chased)**
- **찢다 (to rip) => 찢기다 (to be ripped)**
- **씻다 (to wash) => 씻기다 (to be washed)**
- **뺏다 (to take away) => 뺏기다 (to be taken away)**
- **담다 (to contain) => 담기다 (to be contained)**

The instructor points out that some consonants like **ㄷ** and **ㅈ** are tricky because they can take different suffixes depending on the specific word. For instance, **닫다** takes **-히** to become **닫히다**, but **뜯다** (to tear) takes **-기** to become **뜯기다**.

---

### Transitive vs. Intransitive Verbs

Only transitive verbs (verbs that have an object receiving the action) can be transformed into passive verbs using these suffixes. Intransitive verbs, which describe independent actions like **자다** (to sleep), **일어나다** (to wake up), **울다** (to cry), or **웃다** (to laugh), do not have a receiver that can become the subject of a passive sentence.

The instructor demonstrates transforming transitive sentences:
> **바람이 창문을 열었어요.** / The wind opened the window.
> **창문이 바람에 열렸어요.** / The window was opened by the wind.

> **친구가 매일 저를 꼬집어요.** / A friend pinches me every day.
> **저는 매일 친구한테 꼬집혀요.** / I am pinched by a friend every day.

> **점원이 영수증을 찢었어요.** / The clerk ripped the receipt.
> **영수증이 점원한테 찢겼어요.** / The receipt was ripped by the clerk.

---

### Rule 2: The -아/어지다 Formation

For transitive verbs that are exceptions and do not take the **-이/리/히/기** suffixes, the **-아/어지다** pattern is used. This is the same pattern used to turn adjectives into verbs (meaning "to become [adjective]"), but when applied to verbs, it functions as a passive marker. Common exceptions include:
- **만들다 (to make) => 만들어지다 (to be made)**
- **정하다 (to decide) => 정해지다 (to be decided)**
- **켜다 (to turn on) => 켜지다 (to be turned on)**
- **끄다 (to turn off) => 꺼지다 (to be turned off)**
- **깨다 (to break) => 깨지다 (to be broken)**

The instructor notes that about 90% of verbs can technically be attached to **-아/어지다** to mean an action "naturally happens." For example, **일어나지다** means "to wake up naturally." However, this is a nuanced usage distinct from the standard passive voice.

---

### Rule 3: Passive Forms for -하다 Verbs

Verbs ending in **-하다** (Noun + 하다) follow a logical set of rules for passive transformation by replacing **하다** with **되다**, **받다**, or **당하다**.

**-되다** is used when the subject is a non-living thing.
- **취소하다 (to cancel) => 취소되다 (to be cancelled)**
- **수정하다 (to revise) => 수정되다 (to be revised)**

**-받다** is used when the subject is a person and the action is positive or neutral.
- **초대하다 (to invite) => 초대받다 (to be invited)**
- **사랑하다 (to love) => 사랑받다 (to be loved)**

**-당하다** is used when the subject is a person and the action is negative or undesirable.
- **거절하다 (to reject) => 거절당하다 (to be rejected)**
- **무시하다 (to ignore) => 무시당하다 (to be ignored)**

---

### Passive vs. Causative Verbs (사동사)

The instructor briefly introduces causative verbs (**사동사**), which mean "to make someone do something." These are often formed using similar suffixes (**-이, -히, -리, -기, -우, -추**), which can lead to confusion. For example, **울다** (to cry) becomes **울리다** (to make someone cry), and **먹다** (to eat) becomes **먹이다** (to feed). Students are advised to focus on memorizing the meaning and usage of individual verbs rather than relying solely on formation rules to distinguish between passive and causative forms.
