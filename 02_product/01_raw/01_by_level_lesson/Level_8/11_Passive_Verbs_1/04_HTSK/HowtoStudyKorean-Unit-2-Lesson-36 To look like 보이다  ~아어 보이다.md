Lesson 36: To look like: 보이다 / ~아/어 보이다

Introduction

In this lesson, you will learn how to use 보이다 to say that something can/cannot be seen and to say that something/somebody looks like something. For example, “You look like a monkey!” The same pattern can be used to say that something smells/tastes like something. For example, “You smell like a monkey!” or “You taste like a monkey!” In addition, you will learn how to use ~아/어 보이다 to say that somebody looks like an adjective – for example “You look happy!”

To Look Like: ~ 같이 보이다

One of the most common words in Korean is 보다 which means “to see.” In [Lesson 14](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/unit1/unit-1-lessons-9-16/lesson-14-korean-passive-verbs/), you learned the difference between passive and active verbs in Korean (and English). The word 보이다 is the passive form of 보다 and is used to indicate that something can or cannot *be*seen. For example:

Play[TV가 안 보여](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Lesson36-1.mp3) = The TV can’t be seen
Play[소방서가 보여요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s1.mp3) = The fire station is seen

That being said, the above sentences could also be translated to:

Play[TV가 안 보여](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Lesson36-1.mp3) = I can’t see the TV
Play[소방서가 보여요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s1.mp3) = I can see the fire station

*A note for grammar nerds: Notice that the Korean sentences above are predicated by an intransitive verb (*보이다*), which means they cannot act on an object. The English translations directly above are predicated by a transitive verb (to see), which means they can act on an object. This is a great example that outlines the difficulty of translating sentences from Korean to English (or vice-versa). Literally, the first sentence above would translate “the TV can’t be seen,” but it is often used to mean “I can’t see the TV.”*

A person is often placed in these sentences to specifically indicate the person who can or cannot see something. These sentences follow the same Subject – Object – Adjective (or Passive Verb) structure that you learned about in [Lesson 15](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/unit1/unit-1-lessons-9-16/lesson-15/). For example:

Play[나는 TV가 안 보여](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s2.mp3) = I can’t see the TV
Play[저는 소방서가 보여요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s3.mp3) = I can see the fire station

This same idea can be applied to 듣다 (to hear) and 들리다 (to be heard). For example:

Play[저는 소리가 안 들려요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s4.mp3) = I can’t hear anything
Play[나는 네 목소리가 안 들려](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s5.mp3) = I can’t hear your voice
Play[목소리가 잘 들려](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Lesson36-7.mp3) = I hear you (your voice) well

Below are many other examples:

Play[입장료가 얼마인지 안 보여요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s6.mp3) = I can’t see how much the admission cost is
Play[나는 여기서 산이 안 보여](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s7.mp3) = I can’t see the mountains from here
Play[초등학교가 보여요?](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s8.mp3) = Can you see the elementary school?
Play[가격표가 안 보여요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s9.mp3) = I can’t see the price tags
Play[흡연 구역이 안 보여요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s10.mp3) = I can’t see the smoking area
Play[첫차가 출발하는 소리가 들렸어요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s11.mp3) = I heard the sound of the first car/bus departing
Play[뭔가 터지는 소리가 들렸어요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s12.mp3) = I heard something explode

You can also use 보이다 in sentences to indicate that something looks like something. By placing 같이 (which is the adverb form of the word 같다) after a noun and predicating the whole sentence with 보이다, you can create this meaning. For example:

Play[너는 원숭이 같이 보여](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Lesson36-15.mp3) = You look like a monkey
Play[저의 남자친구는 교수님 같이 보여요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Lesson36-16.mp3) = My boyfriend looks like the professor
Play[너는 고등학생 같이 보여](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Lesson36-17.mp3) = You look like a high school student

The noun before 같이 can also be a more complex noun that is being described by ~는 것. For example:

Play[네가 새로운 집으로 이사하고 싶은 것 같이 보여](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s13.mp3) = It looks like you want to move to a new house
Play[그가 공연에 가고 싶지 않는 것 같이 보여](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s14.mp3) = It looks like he doesn’t want to go to the performance
Play[경기를 이긴 것 같이 보여](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Lesson36-20.mp3) = It looks like you won the game
Play[그녀가 오늘 화장을 안 한 것 같이 보여요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s16.mp3) = It looks like she didn’t do her makeup today

The sentences above explain how you can say that one “looks like” something, but in practice, it is often more common to say that something is just “like” something. For example:

You are like a monkey, instead of:
You look like a monkey

Within the meaning of “you are like a monkey” is the inherent meaning that that person (along with other traits like acting like a monkey, smelling like a monkey) would also look like a monkey.

You actually learned how to do this in [Lesson 15](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/unit1/unit-1-lessons-9-16/lesson-15/), where you first learned how to use words like 같다, 다르다, and 비슷하다. In that lesson, you were presented with this sentence:

이 학교는 우리 학교와 같아요 = This school is the same as our school

I mentioned in that lesson that even though the word “같다” means “same”, when you want to indicate that something is the *same* as something else in Korean, it is more common to use the word “똑같다” (which typically translates to “exactly the same). The sentence above is better written as:

Play[이 학교는 우리 학교와 똑같아요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s17.mp3) = This school is *the same as* our school

When you want to express that one thing is “like” another thing, it is more natural to use the following form:

Play[너는 여자 같아](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s18.mp3) = You’re like a girl

Notice that the particle 와/과/(이)랑/하고 is not added in this sentence. Below are many more examples:

Play[그는 여우 같아요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s19.mp3) = He is like a fox
Play[그는 원숭이 같아요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s20.mp3) = He is like a monkey
Play[캐나다는 미국 같아요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s21.mp3) = Canada is like the US
Play[초등학생 같아요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s22.mp3) = You’re like an elementary school student
Play[진짜 의사 같아](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s23.mp3) = You’re really like a doctor

Next, let’s talk about how you can say something *tastes*or *smells* like something.

To Smell/Taste Like: 맛/냄새

The word 맛 is a noun which means “taste.” You often see this word as “맛있다,” which means “delicious,” but literally translates to “to have taste.” The word “냄새” is a noun which means “smell.” In the previous section you learned how to say:

“\_\_\_ looks like \_\_\_\_.”

In this section, you will learn how to say

“\_\_\_ tastes like \_\_\_\_.” and
“\_\_\_ smells like \_\_\_\_.”

The grammar within these principles is similar to what you were learning previously. What you need to do is place a noun (that has a taste or smell) before 맛 or 냄새, followed by “같다.” For example:

\_\_\_\_ 맛 같다 = tastes like \_\_\_\_
\_\_\_\_ 냄새 같다 = smells like \_\_\_\_

For example:

Play[김치 맛 같아](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Lesson36-29.mp3) = Tastes like Kimchi
Play[김치 찌개 냄새 같아](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Lesson36-30.mp3) = It smells like Kimchi Jjigae

Throw in a subject and you’ve got a full sentence:

Play[이 떡은 쓰레기 맛 같아](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Lesson36-31.mp3) = This 떡 tastes like garbage
Play[삼겹살은 베이컨 맛 같아요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s24.mp3) = 삼겹살 tastes like bacon

Pretty simple, but I thought you should know because I always wanted to know how to say these sentences when I was learning Korean.

To Look (Adjective): ~아/어 보이다

Earlier in this lesson, you learned how to express that something looks like a noun. However, there are many times when you would want to say somebody looks like an *adjective*. For example:

You look happy
You look sad
You look strong

In order to do this, you need to add ~아/어 to an adjective, and then place 보이다 after it. For example:

행복해 보이다 = to look happy
슬퍼 보이다 = to look sad
강해 보이다 = to look strong

Many examples:

Play[왜 그렇게 행복해 보여요?](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Lesson36-33.mp3) = Why do you look so happy?
Play[저의 여자친구는 어제 너무 슬퍼 보였어요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Lesson36-34.mp3) = My girlfriend looked really sad yesterday
Play[그 남자 종아리가 아주 강해 보여요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s25.mp3) = That man’s calf looks very strong
Play[이 원룸은 깨끗해 보여요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s26.mp3) = This (studio) apartment looks clean
Play[화장이 예뻐 보여요](https://www.howtostudykorean.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Lesson36s27.mp3) = Your makeup looks pretty
