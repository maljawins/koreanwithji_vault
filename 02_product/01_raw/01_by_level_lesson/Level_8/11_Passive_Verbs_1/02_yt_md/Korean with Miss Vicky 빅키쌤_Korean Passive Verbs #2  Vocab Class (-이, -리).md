## Teaching Points

### Overview

This lesson focuses on the vocabulary and practical usage of Korean passive verbs, specifically those formed with the suffixes -이 and -리. The instructor begins by establishing the grammatical foundation of passive verbs, explaining how they function similarly to active verbs in terms of tense and conjugation but differ significantly in negation and sentence structure. The core of the class is a deep dive into a comprehensive list of verbs, where the instructor provides active-passive pairs, common collocations, idiomatic expressions, and cultural context. Throughout the session, the instructor emphasizes that passive verbs are often more natural in Korean for situations where English might use active voice, such as describing states or sensory perceptions.

---

### Grammatical Functions and Tense Conjugation

Passive verbs can be conjugated into present, past, and future tenses just like active verbs. The present tense or present progressive is used to describe current states or general facts. For example, the instructor uses the sentence `영어는 세계 공용어로 쓰여요 (English is used as a lingua franca)` to show a general truth. The past tense is particularly important because it often describes the result of a finished action. In English, one might say "This book is written in Korean," but in Korean, this is expressed as `이 책은 한국어로 쓰였어요 (This book was written in Korean)` because the current state of the book is the result of the completed act of writing. Future tense is used for predictions or expectations, such as `미래에는 더 많은 신조어가 쓰일 거예요 (In the future, more newly coined words will be used)`.

---

### The -아/어 있다 Construction

A critical grammar point for passive verbs is the `-아/어 있다` construction, which expresses the continuing state of a completed action. This is frequently used with passive verbs like `열리다 (to be opened)`, `닫히다 (to be closed)`, and `놓이다 (to be placed)`. The instructor explains that while `놓였어요` describes the result of someone placing an object, `놓여 있어요` describes the object currently staying in that placed state. For example, `식탁에 숟가락이 놓여 있어요 (A spoon is [staying] on the table)`. This construction is essential for describing the physical arrangement of objects in a room.

---

### Negation and Sentence Structure

Negating passive verbs requires care, as using the `못` (cannot) negation often sounds awkward. Instead, the `안` or `-지 않다` negation is preferred. This is especially true for sensory passive verbs. To say "I can't see the screen," a Korean speaker says `화면이 안 보여요 (The screen isn't seen)` rather than using a "cannot" construction. Similarly, "I can't hear you" is expressed as `안 들려요 (You aren't heard)`. 

Regarding sentence structure, the receiver of the action becomes the subject and is marked with `이/가`. The original actor is marked with `에`, `에게`, or `한테`. While most passive verbs do not take objects, there is an exception when the subject is a person and you want to specify a body part or a specific item affected. For instance, `저는 모기에 발을 물렸어요 (I was bitten on the foot by a mosquito)`. Here, `발 (foot)` can take the object marker `을`, though `발이` is also acceptable.

### Vocabulary Deep Dive: The -이 Group

The instructor covers a wide range of verbs in the -이 group, starting with `놓다 (to place)` and its passive `놓이다 (to be placed)`. This is often used in the idiom `마음을 놓다 (to set one's mind at ease/be relieved)`. Next is `쌓다 (to pile up)` and `쌓이다 (to be piled up)`, commonly used with `경험 (experience)`, `스트레스 (stress)`, and `할 일 (tasks)`. The verb `모으다 (to collect/save)` and `모이다 (to be gathered)` are frequently used with `돈 (money)` and `사람 (people)`. 

Other verbs include `섞다 (to mix)` and `섞이다 (to be mixed)`, used for languages, ingredients, or shuffling cards. `묶다 (to tie)` and `묶이다 (to be tied)` are used for hair, shoelaces, or the idiom `손발이 묶이다 (to have one's hands and feet tied/be stuck)`. The antonym is `풀다 (to untie/solve)` and `풀리다 (to be untied/solved)`, used for hair, thread, or problems. `꼬다 (to twist/complicate)` and `꼬이다 (to be twisted)` apply to thread, tongues, or situations like "Murphy's Law." 

The instructor also distinguishes between `꺾다 (to break/snap/turn)`, `깎다 (to peel with a knife/cut price/shave)`, and `까다 (to peel with hands/criticize)`. Their passive forms are `꺾이다`, `깎이다`, and `까이다`. `따다 (to pick/unlock/get a number)` becomes `따이다`. `잠그다 (to lock)` becomes `잠기다 (to be locked/submerged)`. `차다 (to kick/dump)` becomes `차이다 (to be dumped)`. `치다 (to hit/run over)` becomes `치였다 (to be run over)`. `닦다 (to wipe/brush)` becomes `닦이다`. Finally, `바꾸다 (to change)` and `바뀌다 (to be changed)` are highlighted as a common point of confusion for learners.
