## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the concept of passive voice in Korean, contrasting it with the active voice. In an active sentence, the subject performs the action (e.g., "I ate the kimchi"), whereas in a passive sentence, the subject is the recipient of the action (e.g., "The kimchi was eaten"). The lesson emphasizes that while passive voice exists in Korean, it should be used judiciously; overusing it can make one's Korean sound unnatural or awkward.

The learning arc moves from the basic definition of passive voice to specific grammatical patterns for creating passive verbs. These include changing `하다` to `되다`, recognizing `나다/내다` pairs, using the `받다` (to receive) construction for certain emotional or social verbs, and learning irregular transformations like `보이다` and `들리다`. Finally, the instructor introduces the `~어/아지다` suffix as a more general way to form passives for verbs that do not have a dedicated passive form.

---

### Active vs. Passive Voice and Basic Particles
The instructor explains that in active voice, the subject does the verb. For example, in the sentence `저는 김치를 먹었어요 (I ate the kimchi)`, the subject `저 (I)` is the one performing the action of eating. In passive voice, the subject of the sentence is actually the object of the action. If we say "The kimchi was eaten," the subject is now `김치 (kimchi)`, but the kimchi isn't "doing" anything; it is being affected by the verb.

A critical grammatical rule for passive verbs is that they cannot take an object. Because the "object" has become the subject, you must use subject markers `이/가` or topic markers `은/는` instead of the object markers `을/를`. The instructor notes that if you see an object marker used with a verb, you can be 100% certain it is an active verb, not a passive one. To specify who performed the action in a passive sentence (the "by" agent), you use the particle `에게` or the more formal `~에 의해(서)`. For example:
> **김치가 철수에게 먹혔어요.** / The kimchi was eaten by Chulsoo.

---

### Passive Formation: 하다 to 되다
For many action verbs ending in `하다`, the passive form is created by replacing `하다` with `되다` (to become). This typically applies to verbs where the noun part and `하다` can be separated. For instance, `준비하다 (to prepare)` becomes `준비되다 (to be prepared)`. The instructor demonstrates this with a comparison:
- Active: `모임을 시작했어요` (I started the meeting)
- Passive: `모임이 시작됐어요` (The meeting was started)

However, this rule does not apply to all `하다` verbs. Verbs like `좋아하다 (to like)` are not separable and do not follow this `되다` pattern.

---

### Passive Pairs: 나다 vs. 내다 and Other Word-Specific Changes
Some Korean verbs come in natural active/passive pairs ending in `내다` (active) and `나다` (passive). A common example is `끝내다 (to finish something)` and `끝나다 (something finishes)`. The instructor explains that a student would say `수업이 끝났어요 (Class finished)` because the student doesn't control the class. Only a teacher, who has the authority to end the session, might say `수업을 끝냈어요 (I finished/ended the class)`.

Other verbs change their stems entirely or add specific suffixes to become passive. The instructor highlights `바꾸다 (to change [something])` versus `바뀌다 (to be changed)`. If a traffic light changes, you must use `신호등이 바뀌었어요` because the light is being changed by a system, not performing an active change on another object. Similarly, `쓰다 (to write)` becomes `쓰이다 (to be written)`.

---

### Passives with 받다 (To Receive)
Certain verbs, particularly those involving social interactions or emotions, become passive by using the construction `[Noun]을/를 받다`. This literally translates to "receiving" the action. Examples include:
- `사랑하다 (to love)` → `사랑을 받다 (to be loved / to receive love)`
- `존경하다 (to respect)` → `존경을 받다 (to be respected / to receive respect)`
- `버리다 (to throw away)` → `버림을 받다 (to be cast away / to be "thrown away" by someone)`

---

### Visible and Audible: 보이다 and 들리다
The verbs `보이다 (to be seen/visible)` and `들리다 (to be heard/audible)` are among the most common passive verbs. While they literally mean "to be seen" and "to be heard," they are the standard way to say "can see" or "can hear" in Korean.

The instructor clarifies the difference between the passive `안 보여요 (It isn't visible)` and the active potential `볼 수 없어요 (I cannot see)`. `볼 수 없어요` implies a lack of physical ability or a specific hindrance to the person (e.g., "I forgot my glasses"). In contrast, `안 보여요` is used when something simply isn't visible due to external circumstances, like being too far away at a concert.
- `잘 보여요?` (Can you see well? / Is it well visible?)
- `잘 들려요?` (Can you hear well? / Is it well audible?)

---

### The ~어/아지다 Suffix
For verbs that do not have a dedicated passive form or a `되다/받다` alternative, the suffix `~어/아지다` can be attached to the conjugated stem. The instructor uses `만들다 (to make)` as an example. To say "It is made of wood," you conjugate `만들다` to `만들어` and add `지다` to get `만들어지다`.
- `나무로 만들어졌어요.` / It was made of wood.

Other examples include `주다 (to give)` becoming `주어지다 (to be given)` and `당기다 (to pull)` becoming `당겨지다 (to be pulled)`. The instructor cautions that you cannot simply attach `~지다` to every verb; if a verb already has a specific passive form (like `밀다 [to push]` becoming `밀리다`), you must use that instead.
