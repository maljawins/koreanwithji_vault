Welcome. We’re finally going to talk about compound verbs in this lesson

But! To understand compound verbs, we first need to understand this core concept:

Can a verb take an object?

**Transitive & Intransitive verbs**

Now, you don't need to memorize these terms, but it's important that you understand the *concept* before we begin this class.

Take “Raise” and “Rise”

You can “raise” a glass, with “glass” being the object but you can’t “rise” a glass. It can only just “rise” on its own. **THIS** is the difference between Transitive verbs and Intransitive verbs.

If you remember 바꾸다 and 바뀌다 from the previous lesson.

* 바꾸다 is transitive. You can CHANGE something.
* But 바뀌다 is intransitive so it can't take an object. It simply CHANGES.

And 변하다, which is the Sino-Korean version of 바뀌다, is also intransitive.

To sum it up,

* **Transitive verbs can take an object.**
* **Intransitive verbs can't take an object. They describe an action that happens on its own, or a state of being. So, all descriptive verbs are intransitive.**

If you go to the Naver dictionary and switch to Korean, not English, it will tell you if the verb is 타동사 (transitive) or 자동사 (intransitive).

This concept will be relevant a bit later, so I’d recommend pausing the video for a moment to make simple notes on it.

**Compound Verbs**

Now that we understand transitive and intransitive verbs, let's quickly review the structure of compound verbs.

**[Verb Stem] + 아/어 + [Another verb].**

**Just like 좋아하다 and 싫어하다 we learned in level 1.**

Again, you only need to conjugate the *second* verb however you need to. So, you can have 좋아해요, 좋아했어요, 좋아합니다, and so on.

Like I said, there is a LOT of compound verbs in Korean. And the thing is, there are no clear-cut rules about which ones work together and which ones don't. What I mean is that you can’t just take any two verbs and jam them together to make your own word. It doesn’t work like that.

But if you know the meaning of both verbs, you can often make a pretty good guess about the new meaning.

For example, we have 알아보다 which is **알다** and **보다** combined. And it has two meanings

1. *To know what you see* → To recognize
2. *To look in order to know* → To inquire, to look into, or to research

✅ And you can easily figure out the meaning by looking at the context.

For example:

* **아기가 엄마를 알아보네요** – Oh, *the baby recognizes (his/her) mom.*
* **한국어 책을 알아보고 있어요** – *I’m researching Korean books.*

For the first one, you know right away it means "to recognize." Well, it could mean “research” if the baby’s going “Hmm, who should I be born to?” possibly going through a list of pregnant women to choose from. But this never happens.

And the second one is pretty obvious, too. You can technically “recognize” a book but you can just tell right off the bat that this one means to research.

So, when it comes to compound verbs, just like with almost everything else in Korean, context is super important. But all you need is a bit of common sense.

However, there ARE really common patterns so, we're going to focus on those today.

**아/어하다**

Let’s do a quick recap on 아/어하다. Remember, we learned it in the lesson “TSV sentence structure” in level 1.

We learned that **we can't truly "know" what another person is feeling, even when it seems super obvious.**

In English, you can directly say "you're sad," or "she's sad." But in Korean, you have to change the verb into this form to talk about **other people's feelings**.

For example, 슬프다 means to be sad. And if someone else is sad, we have to change 슬프다 to 슬퍼하다. Note that it’s now an action verb.

Now, some people say that you can translate this form as “to seem” like “she SEEMS sad.” But this is completely wrong. We actually have a separate form for that, which is 아/어 보이다. So, again, 아/어하다 does NOT mean “to seem”

Instead, you should think of 하다 as "to act". So, 슬퍼하다 could translate to "to **act** in a sad way" or "to behave in a sad way. I don’t *know* for sure if she’s sad but I can tell she’s sad from the way they’re behaving – is what it means.

Let’s break it down by person:

**1st Person (I / We):** Obviously, I know how I feel! So, you can definitely say 저는 슬퍼요 (I'm sad) or 저희는 슬퍼요 (We're sad). No need for 아/어하다 here.

**2nd Person (You):** It’s perfectly natural to ASK “Are you sad?” So, you can say 슬프세요? But if you're telling someone “Don’t be sad”, you **cannot** say 슬프지 마세요. Instead you have to say “슬퍼하지 마세요”

For 3rd person, even if someone is literally crying right in front of you, you still couldn't say 얘가 슬퍼요 (He/She is sad) directly. You have to say 얘가 슬퍼해요 instead.

This is the very same reason why you **cannot** use ~고 싶다 (to want to) with a third person. You can't *know* what someone else wants. So, if you wanted to say "Jisoo wants to eat kimbap," saying 지수는 김밥을 먹고 싶어요 would be wrong.

Instead, it **should be 지수는 김밥을 먹고 싶어해요** because you’re saying what you’re observed from the way Jisoo’s *behaves* and figured she acts like she wants 김밥.
