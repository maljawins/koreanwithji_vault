### Overview
The instructor introduces a more advanced way to express hypothetical conditions in Korean. While a previous lesson covered the standard `(으)면` form, which can translate to both "when" and "if," this lesson focuses on the `다면` form. This specific conjugation is used to create a "strong if," emphasizing that the situation being discussed is purely hypothetical or less likely to happen.

The lesson structure moves from a direct comparison between the standard and strong forms to specific conjugation rules across different tenses. The instructor also highlights important exceptions for the copula verbs and introduces an adverbial phrase that further intensifies the conditional nature of the sentence.

### Regular "If" vs. Strong "If"
The instructor begins by contrasting the standard `(으)면` form with the new `다면` form. In the sentence `내가 한국에 가면 한국말을 많이 배울 거야` (If or when I go to Korea, I will learn a lot of Korean), the use of `가면` is neutral and can imply either a likely "when" or a simple "if." 

However, by changing the verb to the strong form, `내가 한국에 간다면 한국말을 많이 배울 거야` (If I go to Korea, I will learn a lot of Korean), the speaker places a much stronger emphasis on the "if." This version feels more like a hypothetical scenario rather than a planned event. Visually, the instructor shows how the conjugation shifts from the simple verb stem to a more complex structure to signal this shift in nuance.

---

### Conjugation Rules and Tenses
The `다면` form is built upon the "plain form" of verbs and adjectives. Because it relies on the plain form, it is highly versatile and can be used in the past, present, and future tenses. The instructor notes that if a student already knows how to conjugate the plain form, adding `면` to the end is incredibly simple.

In the present tense, verbs take the `ㄴ/는다면` ending, such as `공부한다면` (if you study). Adjectives simply add `다면` to the descriptive stem, such as `춥다면` (if it is cold). For the past tense, the plain past form is used, resulting in endings like `만났다면` (if you met). For the future tense, the `(으)ㄹ 거라면` structure is used, as seen in `배울 거라면` (if you are going to learn).

---

### Exceptions for 이다 and 아니다
There are specific exceptions when using the "strong if" with the verb `이다` (to be) and `아니다` (to not be). For `이다`, the form changes to `이라면` after a consonant or simply `라면` after a vowel. This is a departure from the standard `다면` pattern. Similarly, the negative copula `아니다` always changes to `아니라면`. The instructor demonstrates this with the example `한국어를 배울 거라면` (if you are going to learn Korean), where the future tense marker combined with the copula follows this specific rule.

---

### Strengthening the Hypothesis with 만약에
To make a hypothetical situation feel even more remote or emphasized, Koreans often add the word `만약에` at the beginning of the sentence. `만약에` translates to "in case" or "by chance." While it can be used with the standard `(으)면` form, it is most frequently paired with the `다면` form to maximize the "strong if" feeling. 

The instructor provides a dramatic example: `만약에 내일 지구가 멸망한다면 뭐 할 거야?` (What would you do if the world ended tomorrow?). To illustrate this, the instructor performs a short skit where he reacts to the end of the world by frantically planting a small stick in the ground, referencing the idea of planting an apple tree even if the world ends tomorrow.
