## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces an idiomatic grammar form that is often misunderstood by learners because its literal meaning is the opposite of its actual usage. The form `~을/ㄹ 수가 있어야죠` appears to express ability or necessity, but in practice, it is used to emphasize that something is absolutely impossible or unavoidable. This lesson is specifically designed for intermediate and advanced learners due to its idiomatic nature and specific social requirements for natural usage.

The learning arc begins with a breakdown of the literal components of the phrase to show why it is confusing. The instructor then explains the "hidden" negative meaning, provides concrete examples of how it functions in real sentences, and concludes with the specific grammatical and conversational rules required to use the form correctly.

---

### Construction and Literal Meaning
The form is constructed by taking a verb stem and attaching `을` (if it ends in a consonant) or `ㄹ` (if it ends in a vowel). This is followed by `수가 있어야죠`. In casual speech, the ending may change to `있어야지`, and the `죠` can be written in its full form as `지요`. 

Literally, the components break down as follows: `수 있다` means "can" or "to be able to," and `있어야죠` is a shortened version of `있어야 되죠`, where `야 되다` means "must," "have to," or "need to." Therefore, the literal translation of the entire form would be "I should be able to [verb]" or "I have to be able to [verb]." However, the instructor emphasizes that this literal interpretation is almost never how the phrase is actually used in conversation.

---

### The Idiomatic Meaning: Emphatic Negation
The true function of `~을/ㄹ 수가 있어야죠` is to add emphasis to a sentence where the speaker actually means they cannot do something. Despite containing the word `있다` (to exist/to have), the form functions as a stronger version of `수 없다` (cannot). When a speaker uses this form, they are rhetorically stating that they "should" be able to do something, with the heavy implication that they actually cannot.

The instructor demonstrates this with a food example. If someone says they cannot stop eating pizza because it is their favorite, they might use the phrase:
> **제가 제일 좋아하는 피자가 있으니까 안 먹을 수가 있어야죠.** / Because my favorite pizza is here, I can't not eat it (How could I possibly not eat it?).

In this case, `안 먹을 수가 있어야죠` literally sounds like "I should be able to not eat it," but it actually means "I can't not eat it" or "I have no choice but to eat it." It functions as a more emphatic version of `안 먹을 수가 없죠`.

---

### Usage Requirements and Constraints
Because this is an idiomatic expression, it cannot be used freely in any context. The instructor outlines two strict requirements for the form to sound natural. First, it must be combined with an explanation or a reason. This is typically done using connective forms like `~어서/아서` (because), `~니까` (since), or `~데` (background information). A speaker would never use the form in isolation; the sentence must include the cause for the impossibility.

Second, this form is almost exclusively used as a reply to someone else. It is not a phrase used to start a conversation out of the blue. It serves as a reactive explanation to a question or a statement made by another person. For example, if someone asks why you answered the phone during a meeting, you might reply:
> **계속 연락이 오는데 안 받을 수가 있어야죠.** / They keep contacting me, so how could I not answer? (I had to answer).

---

### Adding Emphasis with 어디
A common variation of this expression involves adding the word `어디` (literally "where") directly before the verb. While `어디` usually functions as a question word, in this idiomatic context, it serves purely as an intensifier to add even more emphasis to the speaker's frustration or the impossibility of the situation.

The instructor shows how this transforms the previous examples:
- `어디 안 받을 수가 있어야죠.` (How on earth could I not answer?)
- `어디 안 먹을 수가 있어야죠.` (How on earth could I not eat it?)

When using this form, the intonation should remain flat or slightly falling. Even though it functions rhetorically like a question in English ("How could I?"), in Korean, it is a statement of fact and should not be spoken with the rising intonation typically used for questions or seeking approval.
