## Lesson Overview
This lesson marks the 24th session in the curriculum and the penultimate class of the summer intensive series. The primary objectives are to provide detailed feedback on recent assignments, conduct an intensive speaking practice session using a new "mock-up dialogue" format, and introduce the comprehensive "Quoting Form" (Indirect Quotation) in Korean. 

The lesson begins with a discussion regarding the student's transition to self-study, as the teacher (Ji) prepares to take a break to develop an online course. Ji emphasizes that while the student's sentence structure is nearly perfect, attention is needed for specific spelling nuances and the use of plain form suggestions and commands. The speaking practice is designed to overcome previous hesitations by providing structured scripts (blue for the student, black for the teacher) covering topics like travel, language learning, personal hobbies, and future goals.

The core grammar focus is the Quoting Form, summarized by the mnemonic **다냐자라**. Ji explains how this form is used not just for reporting speech, but for quoting thoughts, expressing expectations, and even conveying annoyance or emphasis when used as a sentence ending. The lesson covers the four main types of indirect quotes (statements, questions, suggestions, and commands), the "Direct Quotation" form using **(이)라고**, and various contractions and connectors that allow for more natural, fluid Korean speech.

### Slide 20 — The Quoting Form
- **On screen:** Introduction to Indirect Quotation. Mnemonic: **다 냐 자 라 + (고)**.
    - **다** (Statement)
    - **냐** (Question)
    - **자** (Suggestion)
    - **라** (Command)
- **Ji's teaching:** Ji introduces the quoting form, which is used for quotation marks or quoting thoughts. He teaches the mnemonic **다냐자라** (Da-Nya-Ja-Ra) and encourages Amira to say it with a rhythm so it sticks. He explains that verbs following **고** are usually "message" verbs like **말하다** (say), **듣다** (hear), **전하다** (tell), or **생각하다** (think).

### Slide 21 — Quoting Thoughts
- **On screen:** Comparison of two forms:
    1. **다 냐 자 라 + 고 생각하다** (Literally quoting a thought). Example: **해야겠다고 생각했어요** ("I thought 'I should do it'").
    2. **A.V.S + ㄹ/을 줄 알다** (About expectation). Example: **해야 할 줄 알았어요** ("I thought I should do it" - as in, I expected I would).
- **Ji's teaching:** Ji explains the nuance between literally quoting what you said to yourself versus expressing an expectation. If someone asks "Why did you do it?", you would use the expectation form (**~줄 알았어요**) to explain your reasoning.

### Slide 22 — Direct Quotation – “___” (이)라고
- **On screen:** Rules for direct quotes:
    - Use quotation marks.
    - Always use **(이)라고** (from **이다**).
    - **받침**-sensitive.
    - Examples: 엄마가 "와서 밥 먹어!" **라고** 말했어요.
- **Ji's teaching:** Unlike English, where you can't "hear" quotation marks, in Korean, the **라** sound in **라고** makes direct quotes audibly distinguishable. Ji notes that this is why many Koreans struggle with English punctuation—they are used to having a specific spoken particle to mark a quote.

### Slide 23 — Vocabulary: 뜻
- **On screen:** **뜻** : Definition / Meaning. Example: "Frog **는 무슨 뜻이에요?**"
- **Ji's teaching:** A quick vocabulary check for the word "meaning" or "definition."

### Slide 24 — The Quoting Form: Statements
- **On screen:** Conjugation table for statements:
    - Action Verbs: **ㄴ/는다고**
    - Descriptive Verbs: **다고**
    - **이다/아니다**: **이라고 / 아니라고**
    - Future: **ㄹ/을 거라고** (shortened from 것이라고).
- **Ji's teaching:** Ji simplifies the rules: for statements, just use the plain form + **고**. The most important exceptions to remember are **이다** and **아니다** changing to **이라고** and **아니라고**. He also shows how this is used for formal self-introductions: "**저는 아미라라고 합니다**" (I am called Amira).

### Slide 25 — Statements – Example Sentences
- **On screen:**
    - 친구가 내일 시험이 **있다고** 했어요. (My friend said there's an exam tomorrow.)
    - 선생님이 숙제가 많을 **거라고** 말해 줬습니다. (The teacher told me there will be a lot of homework.)
    - 그 사람은 자기가 학생이 **아니라고** 주장했다. (That person claimed he is not a student.)
- **Ji's teaching:** Ji reviews these examples to show how the rules from the previous slide are applied in full sentences.

### Slides 26~27 — The Quoting Form: Questions
- **On screen:** Rule: **Stem + 냐고**.
    - Present/Past: **V.S + 냐고**
    - Future: **거냐고**
- **Ji's teaching:** Quoting questions is easier than statements because you generally just add **냐고** to the stem. Ji goes through examples like **가냐고** (asked where I'm going) and **행복하냐고** (asked if I'm happy).

### Slides 28~30 — Suggestions & Commands
- **On screen:**
    - Suggestions: **Stem + 자고** (e.g., **먹자고**).
    - Commands: **Stem + (으)라고** (e.g., **오라고**, **하라고**).
- **Ji's teaching:** Ji explains that suggestions and commands only exist in the present tense for quoting. He highlights the negative command form: **~지 말라고** (e.g., **가지 말라고** - told me not to go).

### Slide 31 — Special Case: 주다 - to give
- **On screen:**
    1. **달라고**: When the item is given to the **SPEAKER** (Self).
    2. **주라고**: When the item is given to **someone else**.
    - Pronoun table for "Self": **지** (casual), **자기** (neutral), **본인** (formal), **당신** (honorific).
- **Ji's teaching:** This is a crucial exception. If you say "Tell her to give **me** a call," you use **달라고**. If you say "Tell her to give **you** a call," you use **주라고**. Ji also warns about the word **당신**; while it means "self" or "you," it is mostly used by older married couples (over 50) and can be rude if used elsewhere.

### Slide 32 — Special Usage: "Even though / Even if"
- **On screen:** When followed by a verb unrelated to messages, the quoting form means "Just because ___, it doesn't mean ___."
    - Example: **힘들다고 [해도] 포기하면 안 돼요.** (Just because it's hard, it doesn't mean you should give up.)
- **Ji's teaching:** Ji explains that in this usage, the word **해도** (even if) is often dropped, but the **고** must remain. It's a very common way to express that a certain fact isn't a valid excuse for a result.

### Slide 33 — Usages – as an ending
- **On screen:**
    1. **As a question:** To ask "What did you say?" (**뭐라고요?**).
    2. **As a statement/exclamation:** To emphasize repeated info ("I said I'm going!" - **간다고요!**).
- **Ji's teaching:** Ji notes that literally translating "What did you say?" as **뭐를 말했어요?** sounds awkward. Koreans always use the quoting form ending **뭐라고요?**. Using it as a statement can sound a bit annoyed, like "I already told you!"

### Slide 34 — 다냐자라 + 니까
- **On screen:** More aggressive version of the quoting ending.
    - Example: **언제 만나냐니까!** (I asked when we're meeting! [Why aren't you answering?])
- **Ji's teaching:** Adding **~니까** instead of **~고** makes the repetition more aggressive. It implies the speaker is frustrated that the listener hasn't responded to the previous statement or question.

### Slide 35 — Quoting form + Connectors
- **On screen:** Dropping **[고 하]** to shorten forms:
    - **다고** + **는** → **단**
    - **다고** + **면** → **다면**
    - **다고** + **니** → **다니**
    - Rule: Add **[+ㅣ]** to the vowel (다→대, 냐→냬, 자→재, 라→래).
- **Ji's teaching:** Ji explains how to make the "Short Quoting Form" by dropping the middle parts. He points out that **왜냐하면** (because) actually comes from **왜냐고 하면** (if one asks why).

### Slide 36 — 하면 VS 한다면
- **On screen:**
    - **하면**: Simple "If/When."
    - **한다면**: Hypothetical "If you were to say..." or "Let's say."
- **Ji's teaching:** Ji clarifies a common point of confusion. **한다면** is more hypothetical than **하면**. For example, "If you were to go to Korea" (**간다면**) implies it might or might not happen, whereas **가면** is a simpler condition.

### Slides 37~39 — Connectors – Example sentences
- **On screen:** Examples for various connectors:
    - **~는**: **못 한다는** 말씀이세요? (Are you saying I can't...?)
    - **~는데**: **가고 싶다는데** (He says he wants to go, but...)
    - **~더니**: **싫다더니** (You said you didn't want to, but then...)
    - **~면서**: **괜찮다면서** (While saying he's okay...)
- **Ji's teaching:** Ji walks through these to show how the shortened quoting forms blend seamlessly with standard Korean connectors to create complex nuances.

### Slide 40 — Short quoting form (Quotation Endings)
- **On screen:** Table for ending sentences with quotes (cannot quote yourself):
    - Present: **대, 냬, 재, 래**
    - Past: **댔, 넸, 쟀, 랬**
    - Future: **할 거래, 할 거냬**
    - **뭐래?** (What are they saying? / What did you say?)
- **Ji's teaching:** This "Short Quoting Form" is primarily for speech. Ji explains that you can quote anyone *except* yourself using these endings. He highlights **뭐래?** as a very common, slightly informal way to ask "What did they say?" or "What are you talking about?"

### Slide 41 — Short Quoting form Example sentences
- **On screen:**
    - **먹고 싶냬** (They asked what we want to eat).
    - **더울 거랬어요** (They said it will be less hot).
    - **줄 거래요** (The owner says he'll give it).
- **Ji's teaching:** Ji demonstrates how these endings function as complete sentence closers in casual or polite speech.

## Teaching Points
- **The "다냐자라" System:** This is the foundation of indirect quotation. **다** (statements), **냐** (questions), **자** (suggestions), and **라** (commands).
- **Quoting Thoughts:** Use the quoting form (**~다고 생각하다**) to report internal monologue. Distinguish this from expectations (**~ㄹ 줄 알았다**).
- **Direct vs. Indirect:** Direct quotes use **(이)라고** and quotation marks. Indirect quotes use the **다냐자라 + 고** structure.
- **The 주다 Exception:** When quoting a command to "give," use **달라고** if the recipient is the speaker, and **주라고** if the recipient is someone else.
- **Hypothetical "If":** **한다면** (shortened from **한다고 하면**) creates a more hypothetical or "let's say" scenario compared to the simple **하면**.
- **Short Quoting Form:** In speech, the quoting form is often shortened by adding an "e" sound (**ㅣ**) to the vowel: **대, 냬, 재, 래**. This is used to report what others said but cannot be used to quote oneself.
