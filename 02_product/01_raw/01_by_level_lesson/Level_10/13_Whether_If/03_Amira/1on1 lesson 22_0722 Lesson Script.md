**■ ㄴ/은가 보다 & ~나 보다**

Okay, our first form for today: **~는/은/ㄴ가 보다 and ~나 보다**.

These forms are used when you want to express a guess or an inference.

Grammar aspects 설명
It has the same rule as “나요?” “가요?” these context markers for kind question.

So, for an action verb, you attach 나 보다, and for descriptive verb, you attach 니은 가 보다 or 은가 보다 depending on whether the stem ends in a 받침 or not.

Now, the verb 보다 **must always be conjugated in the present tense** when using this form. You **cannot** say ~은가/나 봤어요 (X). This grammar is about making a conjecture *now*, based on your current observation.

Meaning 설명

The core meaning of these forms is “It must” or "I guess," "It seems," or "It looks like.

But, there's a big difference between “나 보다” and ~것 같다, which we already know.

 **~것 같다 (Thoughts & Opinion)**

* This is mainly used to express your thoughts or opinions.
* Your guess **doesn't have to be based on your current observation**. For example, it could be based on a general idea or something you heard. It's closer to the English "I think."

 **~나 보다 (Inference & Drawing Conclusion)**

* On the other hand, this form expresses a guess that you've made **based on facts you are currently observing**.
* So, it's closer to "It must be" or "It looks like."
* It shows **higher certainty** than ~것 같다.
* For example, 비가 오는 것 같아요. (I think it's raining - this has less certainty) versus 비가 오나 봐요. (It must be raining - this has more certainty, because you're hearing the rain or seeing wet ground).

만화 설명

저기요 / 저기요 : Hey there (Excuse me)

내가 목소리가 작은가봐

 (Excuse me... My voice must be small.)

 *This is an inference based on current observation: No one heard me say "저기요," so I'm guessing my voice is too quiet.*

**■ 줄(을) 알다 & 모르다**

Alright, the next form we’re going to learn is **ㄹ/을 줄 알다 and 모르다**. Many learners find this form a bit tricky because they don't know what "줄" means. But even Koreans don't know what "줄" means.

And that means you don’t need to, either.

"줄" is a **bound noun**. And you won't see "줄" in any other forms verbs besides 줄 알다 (to know) and 모르다 (to not know). And it only has two meanings: **"that"** or **"how."**

Since "줄" is a bound noun, it needs a **modifier** before it, just like any other noun. So, you'll see modifiers like ~는, ~ㄴ/은, ~ㄹ/을, or ~던 before "줄."

Note that ~던 줄 is almost never used.

The meaning of the entire form changes slightly depending on **the tense of the modifier** before "줄," and also on **the tense of 알다 or 모르다** itself.

Also, the meaning of 알다 can sometimes be interpreted as "think that" rather than the literal "know that," depending on the context. The keyword here is **"expectation."**

You'll see how the meaning of the entire form changes by looking at the table on the slide. I'll give examples for each case.

**ㄹ/을 줄(을) 알다 & 모르다 Future + 줄**

Let's first look at examples where the modifier's tense is **future tense** (~ㄹ/을 줄).

Okay, to make things easy, the only two cases where “줄” means “how” is when 알다 or 모르다 is in present or future tense.

This is one of the only two combinations where **"줄" means "how."**

* **Example 1:** 남자친구분 한국말 할 줄 아세요?
  + (Does your boyfriend know how to speak Korean?)
  + This is a common question you'd hear if your boyfriend is a foreigner. It literally means "Do your boyfriend know the *way/method* to speak Korean?"

**Example 2:** 열심히 배워서 아마 조금 할 줄 알 거예요.

* (He's worked hard, so he must know how to speak a little bit.)
* Here, 알 거예요 (future tense of 알다) expresses a guess or expectation that he *will know how to* speak.

These two examples are the only cases where "줄" means "how." In all other cases, "줄" means **"that."**

Now when “줄” means that, 알다 can be interpreted as "know" or "think," depending on the situation. The keyword for this form is **"expectation."** Just think: "What happened to your expectation?"

* If you expected it to rain, and it *does* rain as you expected, you'd say 비가 올 줄 알았어요. (I knew it would rain.) - Your expectation was met.
* If you expected it to rain, but it *doesn't* rain contrary to your expectation, you'd say 비가 올 줄 알았어요. (I thought it would rain.) - Your expectation was *not* met.

It's quite simple, isn't it?

There's a very common phrase: **그럴 줄 알았어!** (That's what I thought! / I knew it!) Sometimes, it's even used like "I told you so!"

**ㄹ/을 줄(을) 알다 & 모르다 Future + 줄 Examples**

**V.S + 게 / 도록**

let's move on to our next set of connectors: **~게 and ~도록**. Both of these forms have a very similar meaning: **"so that."**

* **Grammar Aspects:**
  + They can both be used after **descriptive verbs** and **action verbs**.
  + They can only come after the **present stem**, not a past stem.
  + Neither of them is **받침-sensitive**.
  + The verb stem is always in the **present tense** when attached to ~게 or ~도록.

Let's look at an example: 아이도 이해할 수 있게/도록 설명을 쉽게 해주세요. (Please explain it simply so that a child can understand.)

As you can see from this example, in terms of meaning and grammatical usage, there's almost no difference between ~게 and ~도록. However, ~도록 is generally **more formal** than ~게. So, I recommend using ~게 when you can, as it's more natural in everyday conversation.

Today, we'll focus more on ~게 than ~도록, because ~게 is also a prerequisite concept for understanding causative verbs, which we'll learn later.

**Descriptive V.S + 게**

Let's first look at what happens when **~게 attaches to a descriptive verb stem**. In this case, it's one of the ways to **make an adverb**.

하지만 그 어떤 descriptive verb에 게를 붙인다고 해서 natural 해지지 않는다.

For example, there’s a lot of adverbs ending in 이, 리, or 히.

However, it's important to know that you can't just attach ~게 to *any* descriptive verb and expect it to sound natural as an adverb. For example, there are many adverbs that end in 이, 리, or 히:

* 많다 (to be many) → 많이 (a lot)
* 빠르다 (to be fast) → 빨리 (quickly)
* 완벽하다 (to be perfect) → 완벽히 (perfectly)

And then there are many words that are just adverbs on their own, like 조금 (a little), 잘 (well), 방금 (just now), 일찍 (early), and so on.

So as you can see, making adverbs in Korean isn't as simple as English where you can just add "~ly" to an adjective. There is no consistent rule. And that’s why I recommend learning adverbs as individual words, just like you learn nouns.

So, now that we've covered descriptive verbs, let's move on to the case where ~게 comes after an action verb.

**Action V.S + 게**

When **~게 is used after an action verb stem** (on its own, without 하다 or 만들다), you can interpret it as **"so that one can."** Even though the word "can" isn't explicitly in the form, the nuance is very similar.

Because of this, it also expresses a certain degree of **intention**. You might compare it to ~려고 which we learned last time. However, there are crucial differences in their grammatical usage:

* **Subject:** With ~려고, the subject of both clauses (the one doing the action and the one intending) must be the same. But with ~게, the **subjects should be different**.
* **Sentence Type:** ~려고 cannot be used in suggestions or commands. But ~게 is **mostly used in suggestions and commands**.

So, the meaning might be similar but their grammatical applications are completely opposite!

* 모두가 **보게** 그림을 크게 그려주세요.

- Please draw the picture big **so that everyone can see it.**

* 제가 **앉을 수 있게** 의자를 좀 당겨주세요.

- Please pull the chair a bit **so that I can sit** down.

* 앰뷸런스가 먼저 **지나가게** 길을 비켜주세요.

- Please clear the way **so that** the ambulance **can pass** first.)

**~게 하다 & 게 만들다**

Because of ~게's "so that" meaning, it's also used to express **"to let" or "to make someone do something." Using** ~게 하다 and ~게 만들다. Now, 만들다 literally means "to make," so ~게 만들다 feels just a tiny bit more **forcible** than ~게 하다, but they are mostly interchangeable in meaning.

* **엄마가 아이에게 야채를 먹게 했어요.**

**- Mom made the child eat vegetables.**

* **바쁜 스케줄이 저를 피곤하게 만들어요.**

**- A busy schedule makes me tired.**

If you want to express "to give permission" (like "to let someone do"), you use **~게 해주다**.

* 1. 선생님이 학생들에게 질문하게 해주셨어요. (The teacher let the students ask questions.)

If you want to express "to force someone to do" something, you can add words like **억지로** (forcibly/against one's will) or **강제로** (forcibly/compulsorily) into the sentence.

* 1. 회사가 직원들에게 강제로 야근하게 만들었어요. (The company forcibly made the employees work overtime.)

**~게 하다** is also considered the last resort for making a causative verb, especially with verbs that don't work with the 이/리/히/기/우/추 suffixes we learned before. So, it's a very important form for understanding causative verbs more broadly.

**Noun을/를 시키다**

Now, let's learn about a verb that has exactly the same meaning to the forms we just learned: **시키다**

시키다 is actually the causative of 하다. If you look up 시키다 in the dictionary, it says “하게 하다”.

To make someone do something

So when do you use 시키다? You use it when you have a verb that can be separated into an object and a verb, like 운동하다 (to exercise) which is 운동을 하다, it's much more natural to use **시키다** instead of ~게 하다 or ~게 만들다.

While 운동하게 하다 isn't grammatically wrong, 운동을 시키다 sounds much more natural and common.

Here are 4 examples for ~을/를 시키다:

1. 엄마가 아이에게 운동을 시켰어요. (Mom made the child exercise.)
2. 사장님이 직원들에게 야근을 시켰어요. (The boss made the employees work overtime.)
3. 선생님이 학생들에게 숙제를 시켰어요. (The teacher made the students do homewor
4. 저는 배달 음식을 자주 시켜 먹어요. (I often order delivery food and eat it.)
   * Here, 시키다 means "to order."

**~게 되다**

Our final form for today is **~게 되다**.

This form means **"to become in such a way that"** or, more naturally, **"to end up"** doing something or being in a certain state. It implies a change in situation or a result that happened naturally, inevitably, or due to circumstances, rather than by direct intention. It's not necessarily a bad outcome.

Here are 4 examples:

1. 한국에 살다 보니 한국말을 잘 하게 됐어요. (As I lived in Korea, I ended up becoming good at Korean.)
   * This implies a natural progression, not a direct intention to "become good."
2. 갑자기 비가 와서 집에 못 가게 됐어요. (It suddenly rained, so I ended up not being able to go home.)
   * The rain caused an unavoidable change in plans.
3. 열심히 공부해서 좋은 대학교에 가게 됐어요. (I studied hard, so I ended up going to a good university.)
   * The result (going to a good university) happened due to the effort.
4. 결국 우리는 헤어지게 됐어요. (In the end, we ended up breaking up.)
   * This implies the breakup was an inevitable outcome of the situation.

There's also another form, **~고 말다**, which also means "to end up." However, ~고 말다 implies that **something bad happened eventually**, or something you *didn't want* to happen, happened despite efforts. You're going to find out the difference for yourself while doing your assignment!
