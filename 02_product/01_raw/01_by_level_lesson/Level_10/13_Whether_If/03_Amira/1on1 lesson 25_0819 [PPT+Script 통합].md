## Lesson Overview
This lesson marks the conclusion of a 24-session journey, focusing on transitioning from foundational grammar to advanced sentence structures and natural conversational patterns. The primary objective is to solidify the student's "language core"—the ability to read, understand, and construct complex sentences independently—while shifting the focus toward "muscle memory" through listening and speaking practice.

The lesson begins with detailed feedback on previous assignments, specifically addressing nuances in direct quotations, vocabulary choice for skills versus personal health, and the logic of answering negative questions in Korean. Following this, the lesson covers four major grammar concepts: the formal connector ~(으)며 (and its common spoken variant), indirect questions using the bound noun 지, the "regardless/choice" marker ~든지, and the time-related construction ㄴ/은 지 되다.

Finally, the lesson provides a strategic framework for self-study during the upcoming break. Ji introduces the "Input-Process-Output" model for sound, recommending a specific three-step method for watching Korean media (English subtitles, then Korean subtitles, then shadowing). To support this, several digital tools like Language Reactor, Miraa, and Lingopie are introduced to facilitate efficient vocabulary acquisition and listening comprehension.

### Slide 14 — ~(으)며 Meaning & Usage
- **On screen:** Two tables comparing ~(으)며 to ~고 and ~면서. D.V (Descriptive Verbs) = "AND" (Listing). A.V (Action Verbs) = "WHILE" (Simultaneous). A note says it is "almost never used in speech" and can sound "poetic." An image of the band 부활 is shown with lyrics using 며.
- **Ji's teaching:** ~(으)며 is almost exclusively used in written or formal contexts, or in song lyrics. This song is by one of my favorite bands, 부활 (Resurrection)—it's a beautiful ballad. You'll almost never hear 며 in speech except for one specific usage. With Descriptive Verbs, it means "and" for listing adjectives, like 이 컴퓨터는 빠르며 용량이 커요. In conversation, you'd just use ~고. With Action Verbs, it means "while," exactly like ~면서. In speech, always use ~면서. For example, 샤워를 하며 TV를 봤어요 sounds like you have a waterproof TV in the shower! Usually, you'd say 샤워를 하고 (and then) TV를 봤어요.

### Slide 16 — 다 냐 자 라 + 며 / 면서(요) Special Usage
- **On screen:** Explanation of the quoting form + 며. Used to confirm/contradict info ("I thought you said..."). 며 is for 반말, 면서요 for 존댓말. A map shows the North 경기 dialect where 며 is pronounced "매." An image of a dog says "놀아준다매.." (You said you'd play with me..).
- **Ji's teaching:** This is the special usage that is very common in speech. It comes from the quoting form (한다[고 하]며). You use it to confirm or contradict something you heard or thought. For example, 스타벅스 싫다며? (I thought you said you didn't like Starbucks?). You use a rising intonation like a question. In this usage, people often pronounce 며 as "매." This comes from the North 경기 dialect but is used everywhere now. Use 면서요 for polite speech and 며 (or 매) for casual speech.

### Slide 18 — Indirect Question Modifier + 지 - 1
- **On screen:** A conjugation table for Present tense. Action verbs/있다/없다 + 는지. Descriptive verbs + ㄴ/은지. 이다 + 인지. A list of "Verbs related to Information": 알다, 모르다, 궁금하다, 가르치다, 묻다, 알려주다.
- **Ji's teaching:** The 지 here is a bound noun indicating unknown or uncertain information. It is NOT the same as the "지?" ending in "하지?". Because it's a bound noun, you can add markers like 지는, 지가, or 지를. It's used for embedded questions. You'll usually see it paired with verbs related to information, like "to know," "to not know," or "to be curious." To conjugate, just find the right modifier for the tense and attach 지.

### Slide 19 — Indirect Question Modifier + 지 - 2
- **On screen:** A list of question words (누구, 어디, 왜, etc.). Explanation of 어쩐지 (retrospective/certain) vs 왠지 (speculative). A cartoon character says "뭔지 알아" (I know what it is).
- **Ji's teaching:** This works with all question words. I also want to explain 어쩐지, which Koreans use all the time. It means "somehow" or "no wonder." It comes from 어쩌다 + 지. You use it when you had a feeling about something and now it's confirmed. For example, 어쩐지 수상하더라 (No wonder/Somehow I knew you were suspicious). 왠지 also means "somehow," but it's more speculative, like a feeling about the future: 왠지 오늘은 좋은 일이 생길 것 같아 (Somehow I feel like something good will happen today). 어쩐지 is retrospective; 왠지 is speculative.

### Slide 20 — Indirect Question Past & Future
- **On screen:** Conjugation tables for Past (았/었는지) and Future (ㄹ/을지 or ㄹ/을 건지). Notes explain that ㄹ/을 건지 is less common and emphasizes intention.
- **Ji's teaching:** Past tense is straightforward: take the past stem and add 는지. For the future, you have two options. ㄹ/을지 is the most common. ㄹ/을 건지 is used when you want to put more emphasis on the "will" or the intention, like "I don't know if I *will* do that." Most of the time, ㄹ/을지 is perfectly fine.

### Slide 24 — ~든(지)
- **On screen:** Usage of 든지 after question words (~ever) and verbs (whether or not/regardless). A comparison: 든지 (choice) vs 던지 (past recollection). A cartoon shows "언제든지 전화해!!" (Call me whenever!!). A note explains 따위 (negative "and stuff").
- **Ji's teaching:** The 지 in 든지 is optional. The core idea is presenting multiple options where any choice is fine. A common mistake even Koreans make is confusing 든지 with 던지. 든지 is for choices; 던지 is for reflecting on a past feeling (e.g., "It was *so* busy that..."). When used with question words, it means "~ever," like 언제든지 (whenever). After a verb, it's often used twice for "whether or not." Look at this meme: the person says they don't care about "reels and stuff" (릴스 따위). 따위 is a negative way to say "and stuff." But the friend keeps sending them regardless (그러든지 말든지).

### Slide 25 — ~든가
- **On screen:** Explanation that 든가 is almost interchangeable with 든지. After a noun, (이)든지 is more natural. Sentence endings: "그럼 먹지 말든가" (Then don't eat it [or do, I don't care]).
- **Ji's teaching:** 든가 and 든지 are essentially the same. You can omit the 지 in 든지, but you cannot omit the 가 in 든가. After a noun, 든지 sounds more natural. You can end a sentence with either to show a "do it or don't, I don't care" attitude. For example, if someone complains about fishbones, you might say 그럼 먹지 말든가 (Then don't eat it [or do, whatever]). It's like a command with an indifferent attitude.

### Slide 27 — ㄴ/은 지 되다
- **On screen:** Header "NOTICE THE SPACE." Formula: [ㄴ/은] 지 [Time] + 되다. Meaning: "It has been [Time] since [Action]." Common verbs: 되다, 넘다 (more than), 지나다 (pass), 흐르다 (flow).
- **Ji's teaching:** This form is entirely different from the indirect question. Notice the space before 지! Here, ㄴ/은 indicates a completed action, and 지 indicates the duration from that action until now. It means "It has been [time] since [doing something]." 되다 is most common, but you can use 넘다 for "more than a year," or 지나다/흐르다 for time passing. For example: 한국에 온 지 벌써 3년이 넘었어요 (It's been more than 3 years since I came to Korea).

## Teaching Points
- **~(으)며:** A formal connector used primarily in writing or lyrics. It functions as "and" (listing) for descriptive verbs and "while" (simultaneous action) for action verbs. In speech, the quoting form + 며 (often pronounced 매) is used to confirm or contradict information ("I thought you said...").
- **Indirect Questions (Modifier + 지):** Uses the bound noun 지 to indicate uncertain information. It requires the appropriate modifier based on tense: 는지 (Present Action/Existence), ㄴ/은지 (Present Descriptive), 았/었는지 (Past), and ㄹ/을지 (Future). It is commonly paired with information-related verbs like 알다 or 궁금하다.
- **어쩐지 vs 왠지:** Both mean "somehow." 어쩐지 is retrospective and used when a prior feeling is confirmed ("No wonder..."). 왠지 is speculative and used for present/future feelings ("For some reason, I feel...").
- **~든지 vs ~던지:** 든지 expresses a choice between options or "regardless" (often paired with question words for "~ever"). 던지 is a past-tense marker used to express how something felt in the past ("It was so [adjective] that...").
- **ㄴ/은 지 되다:** Indicates the time elapsed since a completed action. Crucially, there must be a space before 지 to distinguish it from the indirect question marker.
- **Negative Questions:** In Korean, you answer "Yes" (네) to agree with the negative premise of the question (e.g., "You don't have a pet?" -> "Yes, [I don't]").
