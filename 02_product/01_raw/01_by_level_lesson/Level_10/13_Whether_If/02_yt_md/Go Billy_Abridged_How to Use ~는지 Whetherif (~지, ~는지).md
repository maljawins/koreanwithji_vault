## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the grammar point ~지, specifically focusing on its function as a connector for unknown or uncertain information within a sentence. He clarifies from the outset that this is distinct from the sentence-ending ~지 used for seeking agreement or confirmation. The lesson is structured to take learners from basic usage—asking "how" to do something—to advanced nuances, including tense changes and the distinction between unknown information and established facts.

The core philosophy presented is that while ~는지 is often translated as "whether" or "if," it is more accurate to view it as a marker for "unknown information." By framing the concept this way, learners can more easily integrate various question words like "who," "what," "where," "when," and "why" into complex sentences. The instructor uses a whiteboard to visually map out conjugation rules and sentence structures, emphasizing that the underlying concept remains consistent even as the complexity of the surrounding vocabulary increases.

---

### Basic Form: Knowing "How" to Do Something
The most common introductory use of this form involves the adverb 어떻게 (how). To construct this, one takes an action verb stem and attaches 는지. The instructor explains that this effectively turns the action verb into an adjective form. For example, the verb 하다 (to do) becomes 하는지. This is then followed by a verb of knowing or not knowing, such as 알다 (to know) or 모르다 (to not know). An optional object marker 를 can be attached to the 는지 clause to treat the entire unknown action as the object of the sentence.

The instructor provides a practical example: "Do you know how to ride a bike?" Instead of a simple question like "How do you ride a bike?" (자전거를 어떻게 타요?), this grammar allows for asking about the state of someone's knowledge. The resulting sentence is:
> **자전거를 어떻게 타는지 알아요?** / Do you know how to ride a bike?

He notes that using the honorific 아세요? is often better when speaking to strangers, such as asking for directions:
> **서울역에 어떻게 가는지 아세요?** / Do you know how to go to Seoul Station?

---

### Expanding to "Whether" or "If"
The instructor moves to the second level by removing the word 어떻게. When used without a specific question word, the ~는지 form translates to "whether" or "if." He reiterates that the 지 itself signifies an unknown or uncertain piece of information. This allows for sentences that discuss whether a certain condition is met or an action is performed.

For instance, to express uncertainty about someone's preferences, one might say:
> **찰스가 BTS를 좋아하는지 몰라요.** / I don't know if (whether) Charles likes BTS.

This structure treats the clause "Charles likes BTS" as the unknown information. The instructor encourages learners to practice with other verbs, such as asking if someone is currently performing an action:
> **찰스도 한국어를 공부하고 있는지 알아요?** / Do you know if Charles is also studying Korean?

---

### Intermediate Usage: Tenses and Descriptive Verbs
At the intermediate level, the instructor demonstrates how to apply this grammar across different tenses and with descriptive verbs (adjectives). For the past tense, the verb stem is combined with the past tense marker (았/었/였) followed by 는지. For the future tense, several options exist, including using 겠는지 or the 을/ㄹ 지 ending. If using the future marker 거시다, it becomes 건지.

- **Past Tense:** 공부했는지 (whether one studied)
- **Future Tense:** 할지 (whether one will do), 먹을지 (whether one will eat)

When using descriptive verbs in the present tense, the ending changes based on whether the stem ends in a vowel or a consonant (ㄴ/은지), similar to how adjectives are formed. For the copula 이다 (to be), it becomes 인지. The instructor provides an example using a descriptive verb:
> **A가 친절한 사람인지 몰라요.** / I don't know if A is a nice person.

He also expands the list of verbs that can follow the ~지 clause. Beyond just "knowing" or "not knowing," one can use verbs like 궁금하다 (to be curious), 알려주다 (to let someone know), 가르쳐주다 (to teach), or 말하다 (to tell).
> **내일도 더울지 궁금해요.** / I am curious whether it will be hot tomorrow too.
> **내일도 비올지 알려 주세요.** / Please let me know if it will rain tomorrow too.

---

### Advanced Nuances: "Whether or Not" and Fact vs. Uncertainty
The instructor concludes with advanced applications. To emphasize "whether or not," one can repeat the adjective or verb in its positive and negative forms. This is done by attaching 지 to the first adjective, then attaching 지 to the negative form of that same adjective.
> **한국어가 어려운지 안 어려운지 알아요?** / Do you know if Korean is difficult or not?

Alternatively, for descriptive verbs, one can use 아닌지 (from 아니다, to not be) to represent the "or not" portion:
> **빌리가 똑똑한지 아닌지 몰라요.** / I don't know whether Billy is smart or not.

Finally, the instructor clarifies the difference between ~는지 알다 and ~는 것을 알다. The ~는지 form is strictly for **unknown information** or uncertainty. In contrast, ~는 것을 알다 (or ~다는 것을 알다) is used for **established facts**.
- **Uncertainty:** 찰스가 바보인지 알아요? (Do you know [the unknown info of] whether Charles is a fool?)
- **Fact:** 찰스가 바보라는 것을 알아요? (Are you aware of the [established fact] that Charles is a fool?)

He illustrates this further with money:
> **철수가 돈이 많이 있는지 알아요?** / Do you know if Chul-soo has a lot of money? (Unknown)
> **철수가 돈이 많다는 걸 알아요?** / Do you know [the fact] that Chul-soo has a lot of money? (Known fact)
