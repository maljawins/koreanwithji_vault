**The Quoting Form**

Alright, we’re finally going to learn the quoting form, which is also known as Indirect Quotation.

The quoting form is incredibly versatile in its usage and grammar, specifically with omissions and contractions. So, it's really useful and convenient if you know how to use it. But it’s also a concept that many learners find challenging precisely because of its versatility making it LOOK like a different form sometimes.

So, it looks like today's going to be one of those days with a lot of grammar rules to talk about! But I'll do my best to explain it as clearly as possible.

So, when do you use the quoting form?

Well, we use it for whenever you’d need to use quotation marks - Things like “He said” “He asked” But it’s not only those two.

In Korean, you can quote someone’s suggestions and commands as well.

And these are the four types of quoting forms:

* **~다고** for **statements**
* **~냐고** for **questions**
* **~자고** for **suggestions**
* **~라고** for **commands**

You can optionally drop the 고 at the end for a slightly more casual feel. We're going to talk about how we can shorten the quoting form a bit later. For now, just memorize **"다냐자라"** like a catchy phrase. Say it with a little rhythm to it and try to make it sound catchy enough so that it stays in your head, like "다냐자라, 다냐자라."

And naturally, the verbs that come after are typically related to “messages” and these are some of the commonly used verbs together with the quoting form.

말하다 / 듣다 / 전하다 / 생각하다

And specifically with questions, you'll often see 묻다 (to ask). With commands, you'll see 시키다 (to order someone to do something).

Now, before we learn how to make each one and their examples, we first need to talk about how we can quote thoughts.

**Quoting Thoughts**

Let’s look at these two English sentences.

I thought “I should do it”.

I thought I should do it.

As you can see, the quotation marks clearly make them mean different things.

But in spoken English, the difference is often inferred by context since it's impossible to *hear* the quotation marks, obviously.

Now, the first one is literally quoting "what I thought" And the other one is talking about expectation

The first one in Korean would be “해야겠다고 생각했어요”

Here, I’m literally quoting my own thought “해야겠다 like “I should do it” is what I thought.

And the other one, about your expectation, would be: **해야 할 줄 알았어요.**

So this can be said in response to 왜 그랬어? Why did you do it? implying "I thought I should do it. That’s why I did it.”

You can see how you *need* these quotation marks in English to distinguish them, but in Korean, we can practically and conveniently replace them by just using the quoting form. This is why it's also known as **Indirect Quotation**.

And there's also the **"Direct Quotation,"** which is really simple to make.

**Direct Quotation**

Here, you do need the quotation marks because you’re directly quoting somebody’s exact words.

And whatever comes in here, whether it’s a statement, question, suggestion, or a command, you always add **(이)라고** after the quotes. This (이)라고 comes from 이다 (to be).

And (이)라고 is 받침-sensitive.

Now, you might think this form is used only in writing but you can hear it in speech more often than you think. It’s not too common, but not uncommon either.

In spoken English, nobody ever says something like “said the daughter”. And I’m guessing this might have something to do with the fact that you can’t HEAR the quotation marks.

But in Korean, we CAN use direct quotation in speech as it is audibly distinguishable by the sound of “라” in “라고”

Like in “해야겠다”라고 생각했어요. If you compare it with 해야겠다고, there’s that clear “라” sound, which allows you to tell that they’re directly quoting somebody.

By the way, if you didn’t know, this is why a lot of Koreans struggle with English punctuations. You can see why, now.

엄마가 "밥 먹어!"라고 말했어요.

* Translation: Mom said, "Eat your rice!"
* Explanation: This directly quotes Mom's command. The 라고 clearly indicates it's a direct quote.

친구가 "나 내일 여행 가"라고 했어요.

* Translation: My friend said, "I'm going on a trip tomorrow."
* Explanation: This is a direct quote of a statement.

아이가 "이거 뭐야?"라고 물었어요.

* Translation: The child asked, "What is this?"
* Explanation: This directly quotes the child's question.

**Special case with 주다 - to give**

Now, there's one important exception you need to know about when quoting commands with the verb **주다** (to give).

**주다 changes to 달라고** when the speaker is telling someone to give something *to them*.

But when given to someone else, you use 주라고.

* **Example 1:** 지수한테 나한테 전화 달라고 문자 남겨.
  + Translation: Text Jisoo and tell her to "give ME a call."
  + Explanation: Here, the speaker wants Jisoo to call *them*.

When the speaker is asking for something to be **given to *someone else***, you use **주라고**.

* **Example 2:** 지수한테 너한테 전화 주라고 문자 남길게.
  + Translation: I'll text Jisoo and tell her to "give YOU a call."
  + Explanation: Here, the speaker wants Jisoo to call *the listener*.

Examples

You’ll very often hear couples calling each other 자기 or 당신. But 당신 is typically only for old, married couples, usually over 50. My parents call each other 당신!

So, again, please don't use 당신 casually.

**The Quoting Form - Special usage**

Now, the quoting form has a special usage where, when it's followed by a verb **unrelated to "messages"** (like 말하다, 묻다, 생각하다), it can be interpreted with a different meaning than the usual "someone says that."

In these cases, you can think of it as **~해도** (even if/even though) being dropped, and the meaning becomes **"even though"** or **"even if."**

**Note that you cannot drop 고 in this case. You need to keep 해도 to drop 고, like 힘들다 해도.**

예문: 힘들다고 [해도] 포기하면 안 돼요.

* Literal Translation: Even if someone says it's hard, you shouldn't give up.
* Natural Translation: Just because it's hard, it doesn't mean you can give up.
* Explanation: The nuance is very similar. It implies that the reason (it's hard) is not a sufficient excuse for the action (giving up).

예문 3개

** 일을 오래 한다고 [해도] 꼭 효율이 좋은 것은 아니에요.**

* **Translation: Just because you work longer hours, it doesn't mean you're being efficient.**
* **Explanation: Working long hours (the stated fact) doesn't necessarily lead to efficiency.**

** 화를 낸다고 [해도] 해결될 문제가 아니에요.**

* **Explanation: Expressing anger (the stated action) will not lead to a solution.**

**Short Quoting Form**

Finally, we also have something called the **Short Quoting Form**. This is simply a way to end your sentence by **quoting someone**, which is why it's also known as a **quotation ending**.

It's basically ~아요 or ~어요 for the quoting form in informal speech, and ~ㅂ니다 for formal speech. The short quoting form is slightly informal, meaning it's primarily used in speech, and not as much in writing. We generally don’t use these kinds of contractions in writing.

And unlike the normal quoting form, you **cannot quote yourself** in short quoting form. The person you're quoting could be "they," "he," "she," or even "you" (meaning the listener), depending on the context, but never "I" (the speaker).

For **present tense informal speech**, all you need to do is add an extra stroke to 다냐자라, making it **대냬재래**.

* 이다 (to be) or 아니다 (to not be) also follow this pattern, changing to (이)래요 or 아니래요.

And for **formal speech level**, you add **~ㅂ니다** to 다냐자라, so you get 답니다, 냡니다, 잡니다, and 랍니다.

Now, if you want to make that **past tense informal**, you just add ㅆ (ssang-siot batchim) before the 어요 ending, getting **댔어요**, **넸어요**, **쟀어요**, and **랬어요**.

And for **future tense informal**, you get **할 거래요** for statements and **할 거냬요** for questions.

**뭐래?** comes from 뭐라고 해? Which means “What are you saying? or, more naturally, "What did you say?" It’s a super common phrase we use you should definitely know, and you could use all these short quoting form endings as the answer to this question.
