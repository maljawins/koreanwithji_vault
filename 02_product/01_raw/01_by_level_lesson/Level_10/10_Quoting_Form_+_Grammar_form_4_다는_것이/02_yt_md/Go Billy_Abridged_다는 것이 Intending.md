## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the intermediate-level grammar form 다는 것이, which is used to express that a speaker intended or planned to do one action but ended up doing something else instead. This form is specifically rooted in the quoting form but carries a unique pragmatic function that distinguishes it from simple noun-describing structures.

The lesson follows a structured arc: first, establishing the necessary prerequisites (plain form and noun nominalization); second, detailing the specific conjugation rules for action verbs; third, defining the semantic meaning and first-person restriction; and finally, providing a variety of practical example sentences to illustrate how the form highlights the gap between intention and reality.

---

### Prerequisites and Conjugation
Before using this form, learners must be familiar with the plain form of verbs and the concept of nominalization using 는 것. To conjugate an action verb into the 다는 것이 form, the verb must first be converted into its plain form. For a verb like 하다 (to do), the stem ends in a vowel, so 니은 is added to create 한다. For a verb like 먹다 (to eat), the stem ends in a consonant, so 는다 is added to create 먹는다.

The instructor highlights a specific exception for verbs ending in ㄹ, such as 팔다 (to sell). In this case, the ㄹ is removed from the stem, and because the remaining stem ends in a vowel, 니은 is attached to create 판다. Once the plain form is achieved, the speaker simply attaches 는 것이. This can be shortened to 는 게 in casual speech, where 게 represents the combination of the word 것 (thing) and the subject marker 이.

---

### Meaning and Usage Restrictions
The structure is represented as "A 다는 것이 B," where A is the intended action and B is the actual result. It translates naturally to English as "I was intending to do A, but B," "I was going to do A, but B," or "I meant to do A, but B." The instructor emphasizes that this form is used when the speaker intends to do one thing, but something else happens instead.

There are several strict rules for this form. First, it is only used with action verbs, never descriptive verbs, because one cannot "intend" a state of being in this context. Second, it is restricted to the first person; it is used to talk about the speaker's own intentions, not those of a third party. Third, the form itself is always in the present tense (plain form + 는 것이), while the second half of the sentence (the result B) is almost always in the past tense to describe what actually happened. Using a past tense like 했다는 것이 would change the meaning entirely to a standard quoting form describing a fact, rather than an intention.

---

### Comparison with (으)려고 했는데
The instructor compares 다는 것이 with the more common form (으)려고 했는데. While both can translate to "I was going to... but," (으)려고 했는데 is much looser and more common in everyday speech. It does not have the same tense or verb type restrictions and can even stand alone without a second clause. In contrast, 다는 것이 is less common in daily conversation and places a much stronger emphasis on the specific intention of the speaker. It specifically highlights the mistake or the unexpected turn of events that followed the original plan.

### Practical Examples
The instructor provides several examples to demonstrate the "Intention vs. Result" dynamic:

> **드라마를 한 편만 더 본다는 것이 너무 재미있어서 세 편을 봤어요.** / I only meant to watch one more episode of the drama, but it was so fun I watched three.
> **사탕을 열 개 산다는 것이 초콜릿을 열 개 사버렸네요.** / I was going to buy ten candies, but I bought ten chocolates instead.
> **잠깐만 잔다는 게 여섯 시간을 자버렸네.** / I was only intending to sleep a little bit, but I ended up sleeping for six hours.
> **맛만 본다는 것이 반이나 먹고 말았어요.** / I only intended to taste it, but I ended up eating half.
