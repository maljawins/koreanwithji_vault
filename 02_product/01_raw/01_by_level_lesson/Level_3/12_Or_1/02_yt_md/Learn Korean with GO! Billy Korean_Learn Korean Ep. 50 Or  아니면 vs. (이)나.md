### Overview

The instructor introduces the concept of "or" in Korean, explaining that there are multiple ways to express it depending on the context and the speaker's intent. The lesson focuses on the two most common methods: using the word **아니면** and the particle **(이)나**. While both can be translated as "or" in English, they serve different grammatical functions and carry distinct nuances regarding choice and exclusivity. The video uses a mix of noun-to-noun comparisons and full-sentence examples to illustrate these differences.

---

### Using 아니면 for Choices

The word **아니면** is used to present distinct choices to a listener. It functions similarly to the English "or" when offering a selection where the listener is expected to choose one option over the other. On screen, the instructor highlights three key uses for **아니면**: it can be placed between two nouns, between two sentences, and it specifically signifies "choices."

When used between nouns, it looks like this:
> **저 아니면 철수** / Me or Chul-soo

When connecting sentences, **아니면** typically follows a complete sentence. The instructor notes that while English often condenses options into a single sentence (e.g., "Are you going or not?"), Korean speakers frequently use two separate questions connected by **아니면**.

Example sentences provided:
> **갈 거예요 아니면 안 갈 거예요?** / Are you going to go or not?
> **같이 갈래요? 아니면 집에 있을래요?** / Do you want to go together or stay at home?
> **아들이에요? 아니면 딸이에요?** / Is it a boy or is it a girl?

---

### Using (이)나 for Non-Exclusive Options

The second way to say "or" involves adding a particle directly to a noun. The form depends on whether the noun ends in a vowel or a consonant. If the noun ends in a vowel, add **나**. If the noun ends in a consonant, add **이나**.

Unlike **아니면**, this particle is not used for offering strict choices. Instead, it carries the nuance of "or something" or "either one is fine." It suggests that the speaker is not required to choose only one option, or that the specific choice isn't critically important.

Examples of the construction:
> **저나 철수** / Me or Chul-soo (ends in vowel)
> **빵이나 먹을 거예요** / I'll eat bread or something (ends in consonant)

---

### 아니면 vs. (이)나: The Nuance of Exclusivity

The instructor provides a direct comparison to clarify the difference in meaning between the two forms.

Consider the sentence:
> **과일이나 야채를 먹고 싶어요.** / I want to eat fruits or vegetables.

In this case, using **이나** implies that the speaker would be happy with either fruits, vegetables, or even both. It is a general statement of preference where the options are not mutually exclusive.

Contrast this with:
> **과일 아니면 야채를 먹고 싶어요.** / I want to eat fruits or vegetables.

Using **아니면** indicates a strict choice. The speaker wants one or the other, but specifically not both. If they choose fruits, they definitely do not want vegetables at that moment.

Additional examples of **(이)나** used with the "or something" nuance:
> **옷이나 사세요.** / Buy some clothes or something.
> **햄이나 김치를 넣을까?** / Should we put in ham or kimchi or something?
