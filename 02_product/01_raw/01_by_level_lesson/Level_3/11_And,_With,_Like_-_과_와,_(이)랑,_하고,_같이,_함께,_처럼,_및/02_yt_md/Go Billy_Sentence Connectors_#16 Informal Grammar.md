## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces the concept of "informal grammar," explaining that while many grammar forms can technically be used across various politeness levels, certain expressions carry a specific "feel" that makes them more or less appropriate for formal or informal settings. The lesson focuses on identifying adverbs, particles, and specific verb endings that lean toward an informal or casual register. Understanding these nuances helps learners avoid sounding unintentionally awkward or overly "fancy" in everyday conversation, while also ensuring they don't use slang-like expressions in official or highly polite situations.

---

### The Spectrum of Formality in Grammar

The instructor explains that the "feel" of a grammar form often correlates with its frequency of use and age. Older or less commonly used grammar forms tend to sound more formal or even "fancy." Using these with close friends can sound out of place. Conversely, certain modern or colloquial forms are perfectly acceptable in standard polite speech (the 요 form) but should be avoided in extra-formal situations, such as giving a speech or talking to a high-ranking official. The goal is to match the grammar's register with the overall speech level being used.

---

### Informal Adverbs

Certain adverbs are common in informal speech and the 요 form but should be avoided in formal contexts. The instructor highlights several examples:

- **되게** — extremely, really, or very
- **진짜** — really or very
- **너무** — so much or very
- **막** — like, wildly, or just (often used as a filler word)
- **완전** — completely or very

A specific nuance is noted for **너무 (too much)**: it is only considered informal when used to mean "so much" or "very" in a positive or neutral sense. When used in its original sense of "too much" (excessive), it is not restricted to informal speech.

---

### Informal Particles

Some particles have a more casual feel compared to their formal counterparts. While these are frequently used in everyday polite conversation, they should be replaced with more formal versions in official settings.

- **에게(서)** becomes **한테(서)** in informal speech.
- **와/과** is often replaced by **(이)랑** or **하고**.

The instructor provides a quick conjugation rule for **(이)랑**: use **이랑** after a consonant and **랑** after a vowel. These forms are standard for daily life but are considered less formal than **와/과**.

---

### Formal vs. Informal Grammar Endings

The instructor compares several pairs of grammar forms to illustrate how some feel more formal simply because they are used less frequently in casual talk.

- **에 따르면** vs. **에 의하면**: Both mean "according to," but **에 따르면** is the more formal version.
- **도록** vs. **게**: Both mean "so that," but **도록** carries a more formal weight.
- **때문에** vs. **(으)니까**: Both mean "because," but **때문에** feels slightly more formal than the common **(으)니까**.

---

### The Informal Ending -가지구

A prominent example of an informal grammar form is **-가지구** (or the standard spelling **-가지고**). This form attaches to a conjugated verb and functions identically to the **-서** ending, meaning "because." Because it is highly informal, it can sound almost like slang.

Examples of the transformation:
- **해서** (because [I] do) → **해가지고**
- **가서** (because [I] go) → **가가지고**
- **먹어서** (because [I] eat) → **먹어가지고**

The instructor notes that **가지고** can be further shortened to **갖고** with no change in meaning.

---

### Summary and Practical Application

The lesson concludes with a reminder that using informal grammar within otherwise polite speech is very common among native speakers and will not significantly negatively affect how a learner sounds. However, learners should be mindful of the context. A quick quiz reinforces three points: first, older/rare grammar feels fancy, not like slang; second, informal particles like **한테** should be avoided in formal situations; and third, informal grammar *can* be used with polite speech (요 form), just not in official or "extra polite" contexts.
