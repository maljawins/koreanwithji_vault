### Irregular Conjugations for the 요 Form
The instructor outlines three major irregular patterns for verb stems when attaching the 요 ending. First, for verb stems ending in the consonant ㅎ, the ㅎ is typically removed, and the final syllable combines with the sound 에 before adding 요. For example, 하얗다 (to be white) becomes 하얘요, 그렇다 (to be so) becomes 그래요, and 노랗다 (to be yellow) becomes 노래요.

Second, for verb stems ending in ㄷ, the ㄷ often changes to ㄹ when followed by a vowel. After this change, the standard 아 or 어 is attached based on the stem vowel, followed by 요. Examples include 듣다 (to listen) becoming 들어요, 걷다 (to walk) becoming 걸어요, and 깨닫다 (to realize) becoming 깨달아요.

Third, for verb stems ending in ㅅ, the ㅅ is removed when followed by a vowel, but unlike other irregulars, the remaining stem does not combine with the following vowel. The standard 아 or 어 is added as a separate syllable. For instance, 낫다 (to recover/be better) becomes 나아요, 붓다 (to pour/swell) becomes 부어요, and 짓다 (to build/make) becomes 지어요.

### Adjusting Politeness and Colloquialism
The instructor explains that the 요 form carries a soft and respectful feel, but its intensity can be adjusted. One common way to make speech sound more natural and colloquial in everyday conversation is by removing grammatical markers such as the topic marker (은/는), subject marker (이/가), or object marker (을/를). However, the instructor warns that removing too many markers can make speech sound like slang or cause ambiguity.

Specific examples of ambiguity are shown on screen. The sentence 저 김치 먹어요 could mean "I eat kimchi" (if 저 is a shortened 저는) or "Eat that kimchi" (if 저 means "that"). Similarly, 저 좋아해요 could mean "I like it" or "Like me" depending on whether the missing marker was 는 or 를. To avoid confusion, markers should be kept if the meaning becomes vague. However, the object marker is frequently removed when its inclusion would sound repetitive, such as changing 저는 저녁을 요리를 해요 to 저는 저녁을 요리해요.

### Making the 요 Form More Polite
To increase the level of respect within the 요 form, speakers can incorporate honorific speech. The instructor demonstrates this by comparing 혹시 알아요? (By chance, do you know?) with the more respectful 혹시 아세요?. While both use the 요 form, the latter includes the honorific infix 시, making it more appropriate for addressing superiors or strangers.

Another method to enhance politeness and add a feeling of gentle curiosity is using the endings 나요 and 가요. These endings make questions sound softer. For action verbs, 나요 is attached to the stem (e.g., 좋아하다 becomes 좋아하나요?). For descriptive verbs, 은가요 is used after a consonant and ㄴ가요 after a vowel (e.g., 멀다 becomes 먼가요?). If a descriptive verb stem ends in ㅂ, it changes to 우 before adding ㄴ가요 (e.g., 맵다 becomes 매운가요?). In the past tense, 나요 is used for all verb types, such as 먹었나요? or 더웠나요?. In casual speech, these simply become 나 or 가.

### Using 요 Without a Verb
The instructor explains how to apply politeness to sentences or fragments that do not end in a conjugated verb. This is done by attaching 이요 or 요. If a word ends in a consonant, 이요 is typically added (e.g., 집 becomes 집이요), and if it ends in a vowel or the letter ㄹ, 요 is added (e.g., 학교 becomes 학교요). 

Technically, the instructor notes that 요 is the only "correct" ending even after consonants (e.g., 집요), and it is always used after grammatical markers (e.g., 은요, 는요, 을요). However, in actual speech, adding 이요 after a consonant sounds more natural to native speakers and is widely accepted despite the technical rule.

---

### Pronunciation and Particle Notes
In the final notes, the instructor points out a common pronunciation shift: the 요 ending is often pronounced as 여 in fast, natural speech (e.g., 안녕하세요 pronounced as 안녕하세여). While this sounds slightly less formal, it is extremely common. 

Additionally, a distinction is made between "markers" (which can often be removed) and "particles" like 에 (at/to), 에게 (to a person), 만 (only), and 도 (also). These particles should generally not be removed because doing so almost always makes the sentence vague. The only major exception is the words 여기 (here), 거기 (there), and 저기 (over there), which frequently drop the particle 에 in colloquial speech.
