## Teaching Points

### Overview
This lesson focuses on the three primary Korean nouns used to indicate location: 여기 (here), 거기 (there), and 저기 (over there). The instructor explains that these words are categorized based on their proximity to the speaker and the listener. The lesson also covers the grammatical behavior of these nouns when paired with the location particle 에, noting that the particle is often omitted in natural speech.

The learning arc begins with a detailed breakdown of each location noun, followed by a summary of their spatial relationships. It concludes with a conversation practice that demonstrates how these words function in a real-world context, such as discussing one's location and the weather.

---

### Proximity and the Three Location Nouns
The instructor introduces **여기** as the noun meaning "here." It refers to a location that is next to or close to the speaker. Learners may recognize this word from the common expression **여기요**, which is used to get someone's attention (like a waiter); in that context, the polite ending **요** is attached to the noun. An example of its use in a sentence is:
> **빌리도 여기에 자주 와요.** / Billy also comes here often.

The second noun is **거기**, which translates to "there." This word is specifically used for a location that is close to the listener but not close to the speaker. The instructor provides the following example:
> **저는 거기에 가고 싶어요.** / I want to go there.

The third noun is **저기**, meaning "over there." Similar to 여기, learners may know this from the phrase **저기요**. Spatially, **저기** is used when the location being discussed is far from both the speaker and the listener. A visual diagram on screen illustrates this: **여기** is the immediate circle around the speaker, **거기** is the circle around the listener, and **저기** is the area outside both of those circles. An example sentence is:
> **저기에 영화관이 있어요.** / There is a movie theater over there.

---

### Using the Location Particle 에
When using these location nouns, the particle **에** (at/to/in) is frequently attached to indicate the destination or location of existence. However, the instructor emphasizes that with **여기**, **거기**, and **저기**, the particle **에** is completely optional. In casual and even most standard speech, it is more common to omit it entirely.

The instructor demonstrates this with two equivalent sentences that carry the exact same meaning:
- **제 책이 저기에 있어요.** / My book is over there.
- **제 책이 저기 있어요.** / My book is over there.

Both versions are perfectly correct, but the second version (without **에**) is what a learner will likely hear most often in Korea.

---

### Conversation Practice
The lesson concludes with a dialogue between two characters, 윤소 and 예슬, to show these words in action.

윤소 begins by stating her location:
> **저는 지금 한국에 있어요. 날씨가 정말 좋아요.** / I am now in Korea. The weather is really good.

예슬 responds by referring to 윤소's location (which is far from 예슬):
> **와우! 저도 거기에 가고 싶어요. 여기는 날씨가 더워요.** / Wow! I also want to go there. Here (where I am) the weather is hot.

윤소 then invites 예슬:
> **여기 와 주세요.** / Please come here.

예슬 explains why she cannot:
> **거기가 너무 멀어요. 지금은 제가 갈 수 없어요.** / There (where you are) is too far. I can't go now.

This practice highlights the shifting perspective of "here" and "there" depending on who is speaking. For 윤소, Korea is **여기**, but for 예슬, Korea is **거기**.
