## Teaching Points

### Overview
This lesson focuses on the specific set of Korean verbs and adjectives that must be paired with the place-marking particle 에. While many learners are familiar with 에 as a general location marker, its usage is strictly governed by the verb that follows it. The instructor categorizes these into three distinct groups: verbs of movement or posture, verbs of placement, and descriptive adjectives that indicate a relationship to a specific target or field.

The instructor emphasizes that the most effective way to master these particles is not through abstract grammar rules, but by memorizing "verb-particle chunks" or full sentences. By learning expressions like 차에 타다 (to get on a car) as a single unit, students naturally internalize which particle belongs with which verb. Throughout the session, each verb is practiced through its present, past, and future conjugations to build functional fluency.

---

### The Concept of Presence and Movement
The particle 에 is primarily used with verbs that indicate presence or a change in physical state relative to a location. The instructor reviews the foundational verbs of presence: 있다 (to be/exist), 살다 (to live), and 존재하다 (to exist). A notable exception is mentioned for 살다 (to live), which is unique in that it can be used with either 에 or 에서 without a significant change in meaning.

Beyond simple existence, 에 is used with verbs of posture and movement that result in a state of being "on" or "at" a place. This includes verbs like 앉다 (to sit), 서다 (to stand), and 타다 (to ride/get on). The instructor demonstrates that when you sit on a chair, the chair is the destination of your movement, necessitating the 에 particle.

---

### Group 1: Movement, Posture, and Social Interaction
The first group of verbs includes physical actions and social stances. For physical actions, the instructor covers 타다 (to ride), 앉다 (to sit), 서다 (to stand), and 올라가다 (to go up). For 서다 (to stand), a nuance is explained regarding the difference between 서 있어요 (to be in a state of standing/in line) and 서고 있어요 (to be in the process of forming a line).

This group also includes verbs of "appearing" on media, such as 나오다 and 출연하다. While 나오다 has many meanings, when paired with 에, it specifically means to appear on public media like TV, newspapers, or magazines. 출연하다 is more specific to appearing on a screen (movies or dramas). Finally, this group covers social verbs like 동의하다 (to agree) and 반대하다 (to disagree). Crucially, these use 에 only when agreeing with a "thing" (like an opinion or plan); if agreeing with a person, the particle changes to 와/과.

---

### Group 2: Verbs of Placement
The second group consists of verbs that involve putting or storing an object in a specific location. The primary verbs are 넣다 (to put in) and 놓다 (to place on). The instructor uses a visual demonstration with a cup and a "mouth" (prop) to show the difference: 넣다 implies putting something inside a container, while 놓다 implies placing something on a surface like a desk.

This group also includes 보관하다 (to store or keep). This verb is used for the long-term maintenance of items, such as storing clothes in a closet (옷장에 보관하다) or keeping food in a refrigerator (냉장고에 보관하다). The instructor notes that the "냉" in 냉장고 (refrigerator) comes from a Sino-Korean root meaning "cold," helping students understand the etymology of the word.

---

### Group 3: Adjectives and Target-Oriented States
In Korean, adjectives (descriptive verbs) function similarly to action verbs and follow the same conjugation rules. Group 3 focuses on adjectives that describe a state relative to a target. 좋다 (to be good) becomes "good for [target]" when used with 에, such as 몸에 좋다 (good for the body/health).

Other key expressions in this group include 약하다 (to be weak at/for), 관심이 있다 (to be interested in), and 재능이 있다 (to be talented in). The instructor explains the nuance of 약하다: it can mean being academically weak at a subject like math (수학에 약하다) or having a "soft spot" for someone (여자에 약하다). For 관심이 있다 (to be interested in), the instructor clarifies that 에 is used for objects or fields of study, but if you have a romantic interest in a person, you must use 에게 or 한테 instead.
