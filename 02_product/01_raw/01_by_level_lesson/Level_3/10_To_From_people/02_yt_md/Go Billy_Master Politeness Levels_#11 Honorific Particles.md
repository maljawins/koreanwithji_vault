## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces honorific particles, which are specialized versions of common Korean particles used to show extra respect toward the person being discussed. Just as certain nouns and verbs have honorific equivalents, particles like the topic marker, subject marker, and the "to/from" marker have specific honorific forms. These are typically used in conjunction with other honorific speech elements, such as the `-(으)시다` verb suffix, to create a cohesive and highly respectful tone.

The lesson emphasizes that these particles are exclusively used for people, never for animals or inanimate objects. While they are not strictly mandatory even when using honorific speech, omitting them can sometimes make a sentence feel incomplete or less polished. The instructor explains that the choice to use them depends on the desired level of politeness and the specific social context of the conversation.

---

### What Are Honorific Particles?
The instructor identifies three primary honorific particles that learners should master. First, the standard topic marker `은/는` becomes `께서는`. Second, the subject marker `이/가` becomes `께서`. Third, the particle `에게` (meaning "to" or "from") becomes `께`. These particles are only attached after the name or title of someone else whom the speaker wishes to honor; they are never used for oneself. A critical rule displayed on screen is that these are reserved for people; one would never use `께서` for a pet or a building.

---

### When to Use Honorific Particles
The instructor clarifies the necessity of these markers. While it is not strictly required to use honorific particles every time one uses honorific speech, doing so demonstrates a higher degree of respect. In contexts where the speaker is already using exclusive honorific speech (such as the `-(으)십니다` style), failing to use the corresponding honorific particles can make the speech sound "incomplete." However, when mixing honorifics with the standard polite `요` form—resulting in a polite but not extremely formal tone—these particles are considered optional. The instructor notes that adding these particles will always make the speech sound more refined and polite.

---

### Using 께서는 (Honorific Topic Marker)
The instructor explains that while `은/는` is used for oneself or acquaintances in casual or standard polite settings, `께서는` is used when the topic of the sentence is someone deserving of honorifics. Several examples are provided to illustrate this:

> **우리 할머니께서는 한국에서 살고 계세요.** / My grandmother is living in Korea.
> **회장님께서는 어떻게 생각하세요?** / What do you think, President?
> **사장님께서는 조금 전에 나가셨어요.** / The boss left a little while ago.
> **아버지께서는 식사 중이세요.** / My father is in the middle of a meal.

In these sentences, the instructor highlights how `께서는` pairs naturally with honorific verb forms like `계시다` (honorific of "to be/stay") or `식사` (honorific of "meal/eat").

---

### Using 께서 (Honorific Subject Marker)
Similar to the topic marker, `께서` replaces `이/가` when the subject of the sentence is a person the speaker wants to show extra respect toward. The instructor provides the following examples:

> **사장님께서 사무실에 안 계세요.** / The boss is not in their office.
> **선생님께서 오늘 많이 피곤하신가 봐요.** / I guess the teacher is very tired today.
> **할아버지께서 TV를 보고 계세요.** / Grandfather is watching TV.
> **재림 씨께서 알려주셨어요.** / Jaerim told me.

The instructor notes that even for someone like a colleague (Jaerim), using `께서` adds a layer of professional respect or politeness to the statement.

---

### Using 께 (Honorific To/From Marker)
The particle `께` serves as the honorific version of `에게` or `에게서`. It functions as both "to" and "from" depending on the verb used. It can also replace the more informal `한테` or `한테서`. Examples shown include:

> **이건 선생님께 보내야 돼요.** / I have to send this to the teacher.
> **사장님께 이야기했어요?** / Did you tell the boss?
> **선생님께 물어보고 싶었어요.** / I wanted to ask the teacher.
> **그 차트는 주연 씨께 보여주세요.** / Show that chart to Juyeon.

---

### Listening Practice and Nuance
The instructor presents a listening exercise involving a Teacher's Day announcement. The text uses `은사님`, which is an even more polite term for `선생님` (teacher). 

> **다음은 김만기 선생님께서 은사님께 전하는 편지를 낭독하시겠습니다.** / Next, Teacher Kim Man-gi will recite a letter he wrote to his teacher.
> **김만기 선생님께서는 우리 학교를 졸업하신 후 40년간 국제 변호사로 활동하고 계십니다.** / Teacher Kim Man-gi has been active as an international lawyer for 40 years since graduating from our school.

The instructor concludes with a note on tone and delivery. He explains that even perfect honorific speech can sound rude if delivered with a robotic or sarcastic tone. Conversely, a beginner who makes mistakes but is enthusiastic and polite in demeanor is often better received. Finally, he mentions that `께서` is a distinct particle, meaning it can combine with other particles, such as `께서도` (`께서` + `도` meaning "also").
