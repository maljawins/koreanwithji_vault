**Intro**

Starting from this lesson, we’re gonna be talking about time and place in a detailed fashion.

And we’re going to be starting with what’s called “postpositions”.

Let’s get straight into it.

To tell you what postpositions are, we first need to talk about prepositions - words like “in”, “at”, “on” and so on.

Pre means “before” and Post means “after”. So, in English, we place our **pre**positions **before** the noun like “on the desk”

But Korean uses **post**-positions, meaning they are used **after** the word they relate to. Simply put, Korean postpositions are just words that are used to describe where or when something happens.

And here’s the important thing. Every postposition is a regular noun. And you still need to use particles like normal, just like a regular noun in Korean.

I’ll explain while talking about “In”

**“안”**

Take a look at this sentence.

사과가 상자안에 있어요 – The apple is in the box.

Now, 안 is “inside” and notice “에”.

If 안 already means inside, couldn't we just say “상자 안 있어요”?.

No, because like I said, 안 is just a normal noun. So we don't treat them special, which means you need to have a location marker, namely 에. Without this, it doesn't mean it is located there.

And **because** every postposition is a noun, you technically could do this: “사과가 상자의 안에 있어요”, If you remember, “의” is the possessive marker, like “apostrophe S” or “Of”. So, we’re treating 안 like the noun that it is – THE inside. So, this is like saying “The apple is AT the INSIDE OF the box. Pretty specific.

But Koreans never talk like this, I’m just showing it to you to explain that postpositions are nouns in Korean.

**속**

We have another postposition that means “inside”.

Now, what’s the difference between 안 and 속? You only need to ask yourself ONE question.

“Does it have a door?”

Now, 속 is used with things that ”don’t have a door” such as water – “물 속” or abstract things like dream - “꿈 속” whereas 안 is used with things with physical ”doors” like a house or a box – “집 안”, “상자 안”

Let’s look at some more examples.

“일상 속” - "in daily life." 일상 means daily life. So, this is a no brainer. Your daily life is obviously not a physical place with a door.

“거울 속” – “In the mirror” Now, a mirror does have a physical frame but it doesn’t have a door, or an entrance. And what you see in the mirror is just the reflection of the real world, which is an “abstraction”. Therefore, 속 is used.

“뱃속” – 배 is stomach. Well, this is physical, isn’t it? And your mouth is technically the door to your stomach so shouldn’t you use 안?

Well, while 배 does mean stomach, it’s not the “organ” for digestion. It’s just “Tummy” and “Tummy” doesn’t have a door.

By the way, this ㅅ 받침 under ‘배’ is called 사이시옷 – which translates to “시옷 in between”. Now, the purpose of 사이시옷 is to combine two nouns into a new compound noun. So essentially, 뱃속 the inside of your stomach.

Here’s a couple more examples of the use of 사이시옷

햇빛 – 해: Sun / 빛: Light

고춧가루 – 고추: Chili / 가루: Powder – Chili powder

콧구멍 – 코: Nose / 구멍: Hole - Nostril

The word 속 by itself can also mean "insides." This is how you would say like "your stomach hurts," so you could make this sentence:

속이 안 좋아요 (sok-i an jokayo)

Literally, "my insides are not good" where 속 is referring to the insides. Here, you're using 속 because it metaphorically means your insides.

And for general use, 안 is going to be more common. However, 속 has several extra uses that 안 does not.

**밖 (bakk) - Outside**

All right, next: 밖 (bakk). 밖 means "outside," and again, this is also just a regular noun. So outside of what? Well, let's do an example sentence:

밖에 비가 와요 (bakk-e bi-ga wayo)

So literally, "rain comes." That's how you say "it rains" - 비가 와요. "It's raining." It's raining where? Well, the location marker 에 - outside 밖에. So it's raining outside - literally "outside."

**앞 (ap) - Front**

Next up: 앞 (ap) has a few different translations. The most simple translation for 앞 is "front," as in saying "in front of," but it can also just be used to mean "the front." It can also mean "forward" - anything this way, like this is 앞.

So we can make an example sentence:

카페 앞에서 기다려요 (kape ap-eseo gidaryeoyo)

"I am waiting" - 기다려요 is "to wait." Where am I waiting? Well, here 에서 is being used. We're not using 에 because we're not going to the front of the cafe. We're using 에서 because we're doing something at a location. What location? The front of the cafe.

So 카페 앞 means "the front of a cafe." Again, you could technically have the possessive marker right here - "카페의 앞" - but you don't need this.

So "I am waiting in front of the cafe."

There's one more thing you should know. Now this is not a postposition - this is an adverb: 바로 (baro) is "right." For example, you can have 여기 for "here," and you can have 바로 여기 for "right here."

You can have 앞 for "in front of," or you can have 바로 앞 (baro ap) - "right in front of."

So if you want to emphasize any of these postpositions - now you usually don't need to do this, but if you need to emphasize them - just put 바로 before.

**뒤 (dwi) - Behind**

The next one is 뒤 (dwi), meaning "behind."

개가 문 뒤에 숨었어요 (gae-ga mun dwi-e sumeosseoyo)

So the dog - the door - behind. "The door behind" - "the dog is to hide." So "the dog hid behind the door." 숨었어요 - "so the dog hid behind the door."

**위 (wi) - Top/Above/On top of**

Okay, 위 (wi) means "the top." It can also mean "on top of," and it also means "above."

나무 위에 원숭이가 있어요 (namu wi-e wonsungi-ga isseoyo)

So "on top of the tree, there's a monkey on top of the tree." Where is the monkey? Well, it is 위에. So "the top of the tree" - "it is on the top of the tree." So "there's a monkey on top of the tree."

**옆 (yeop) - Side/Next to**

Next, we have 옆 (yeop), which means "beside," like next to something.

집 옆에 (jip yeop-e) - so "next to the house" or "the house's side," "on the side of the house." This is optional.

차를 주차했어요 (cha-reul juchahaeosseoyo)

"The car is to park" - now you can park anything. You can park a car, you can also park a bike, but in this case it is 차를.

집 옆에 차를 주차했어요 (jip yeop-e cha-reul juchahaeosseoyo)

So "I parked the car next to the house."

**아래 (arae) & 밑 (mit) - Below/Under**

Here’s another set of postpositions that are commonly confused. 아래 and 밑 both mean "below" or "under," But they have different nuances though. And the difference is extremely subtle.

아래 is fully underneath. 밑 has more of the meaning 'base; foundation; root,'. They’re often interchangeable for being physically below a location.

For example, 아래 and 밑 both work for, "The pen is under the chair."

So both “펜이 의자 아래에 있어요.” “펜이 의자 밑에 있어요” work.

But when you’re talking about something that’s NOT physical, like on a web page, you’d use 아래 not 밑.

“아래 버튼을 클릭하세요.” "Click the button below" 밑 doesn't quite work here because a web page is not a physical place.

**사이**

사이 means between. Yes, this is the same 사이 in 사이시옷 – The 시옷 in between.

And here’s how you say “between A & B”

**A 와/과 or (이)랑 B 사이.**

We learned 와/과 and (이)랑 in level 2 and they mean “AND” for connecting two nouns.

Again, 사이 is a noun. So you’d need a particle after 사이.

우체국은 주유소**와** 소방서 **사이**에 있어요. (The post office is in between the gas station and the fire station)

Now, 사이 has a special use where it’s not necessarily a postposition.

제 친구와 저는 사이가 좋아요. – “Between” is good?

For this specific usage, you can translate 사이 as the relationship BETWEEN the two people. And this sentence means “My friend and I get along”.

And “to not get along” means 사이가 안 좋다 / 좋지 않다 – the relationship is NOT good.

And you can take it further and say 사이가 나쁘다 – The relationship is BAD.

So, I recommend you learn the phrase “사이(가) 좋다” as an idiom.

**전 & 후 – Before & After**

Alright, next, we have 전 and 후.

전 means before and 후 means after. They’re also regular **nouns** referring to the moments before and after. So you can use them with **time markers** (에, 부터, 까지):

* **30분 후에 저녁을 먹을 거예요 - We’ll eat dinner after(in) 30 minutes**
* **그 전부터 계속 너를 좋아했어 - I’ve always liked you since before that**
* **일요일 전까지 전화 주세요 – Give me a call before (by) Sunday**

**쪽**

Now, we're going to learn about a lot of useful Direction words.

쪽 is the most common one that you're going to see. What it means is a direction or a way as in like that way or this way this direction. And it’s only used together with other descriptions like 이 쪽, 저 쪽, and 그 쪽. So, you're not going to be using 쪽 by itself.

Here’s some common phrases you’ll hear.

어느 쪽이에요? – Which way is it?

Another crucial set of words you need to know - left and right.0

왼쪽 – Left (way)

오른쪽 – Right (way)

We also use 쪽 for North South East West. But in Korean the order goes “East, West, South, North.”

EWSN – Go billy on youtube came up with this acronym. Eat Warm Spicy Noodles.

So we say “동서남북”

동쪽 – East

서쪽 – West

남쪽 – South

북쪽 – North

“해는 동쪽에서 떠요” – The run rises from the east.

남 and 북 are the words we use to say South Korea and North Korea.

Korea in Korean is 한국. And South Korea is 남한 and North Korea is 북한.

But we Koreans never call our country 남한. We just say 한국 or 대한민국 in its full name, which means REPUBLIC of Korea.

Here’s a little culture lesson for fun.

We do not acknowledge North Korea’s regime and its sovereignty. And according to our constitution, 북한은 별도의 국가가 아닌 대한민국 영토의 일부이고 북한 주민은 대한민국 국민이다.

So, we’re not supposed to call ourselves “남한” because that would imply we acknowledge 김정은’s regime. There is only ONE Korea and 북한의 영토는 김씨 일가가 불법으로 점령하고 있을 뿐이다.

And considering what they’ve done and STILL doing to OUR people, so many Koreans understandably get upset when people ask “Are you from the North or South?”

I personally don’t take any offense. But here’s a little story to help you understand why other Koreans DO get offended, and rightfully so.

My grandfather escaped the North during the wartime and saw his entire family either getting killed or captured. And he’s the only one who made it to the south. And what he said is even more shocking. He said a part of him felt relief for those who got killed because God only knows what happened to those who got caught. Yes, I’m talking about my mother’s uncles and aunts, here.

And it’s not just me. A lot of Koreans share similar tragic history. Hopefully, now you know why Koreans get upset at the whole “North or South” question.

So, if you ever feel the urge to ask a Korean whether they’re from the North or South, don’t. I assure you 99.99% of Koreans you have and WILL see in your ENTIRE lifetime are from the South.

**편**

Back to the lesson. Now, 편 is also a very common word and it means “a side” so it doesn't mean a direction or a way but it means a side of something.

so let me give you a few common words that use 편.

건너편: 건너다 means to cross so this means the other side ACROSS.

반대편: 반대 means opposite. 반대편 means the opposite side.

맞은편: 맞다 means to face. 맞은편 means the facing side.

So, all three words are interchangeable to express the idea of “Across”. But again, they are all regular NOUNS.

약국은 길 건너편에 있어요. – The pharmacy is across the street.

Notice the particle 에 in 건너편에.

There’s one more usage of 편. It can also be used to mean side as in “I’m on your side” like “taking sides”.

So, you can use any noun, any person and attach 편 afterwards.

“걱정 하지마. 난 항상 네 편이야.” – Don’t worry. I’m always on your side.

Let's now talk about a few more direction words that you're going to come across.

The first one I wanted to talk about is 방향. 방향 means direction so it has the same meaning as 쪽 but unlike 쪽, you can use 방향 by itself.

방향 is a Sino-Korean word and more formal.

쪽 is more casual and can be used more widely.

Next, I have a set of three words that you should learn. These are especially important to know for your taxi ride in Korea.

**직진하다 – To go straight**

**좌회전하다 – To make a left turn**

**우회전하다 – To make a right turn**

**좌 and 우 are Sino-Korean words that respectively mean left and right. And 회전 means a “turn”. So, together**
