**Intro**

In this lesson, we’re going to learn about the particle (으)로 known as the direction marker. But this particle has SO many other usages than just indicating direction and we’re going to talk about each and every one of its uses in this lesson.

**Grammar**

Before we get talk about their different uses, you’ve probably noticed that this particle is also 받침-sensitive.

After a 받침, you’d use 으로

After a vowel, you’d use 로

The only exception would be for words ending ㄹ 받침. In tis case, you’d just use 로, not 으로.

**(으)로 - Toward**

Let’s talk about the main meaning of (으)로.

For the first use of (으)로, you could translate it as “Toward” as in “Toward the direction of a location”

Now, you might think what about ‘에’?

‘에’에 대해 잠시 remind 하자면 ‘에’는 크게 3가지 사용법이 있다.

‘에’ can indicate “the time” at which something happens like - 저는 토요일에 친구랑 만났어요.

It can also let you know where someone or something IS - 지수는 집에 있어요

And it also indicates where something or someone is moving TO, like 나는 학교에 가요. This last use is what we’re going to compare with (으)로.

We can also say 나는 학교로 가요.

The difference is very subtle and definitely untranslatable. It’s all about the nuance.

The most crucial difference concerning movement is that '에' refers to a precise location, while '(으)로' refers to direction or general vicinity.

“학교**에** 가요” 라고 말한다면 정확하게 교실안에 자리를 잡고 수업을 들으러 간다는 말이고

When using '(으)로' with a verb like '가다' (to go), you are moving toward that location, but you are not necessarily going ***to*** the place itself

**학교로 가요"** (Goes toward the school) could mean you are going there because maybe a cafe is next to it, or because that is the general path toward your home. So, it feels more like “I’m HEADING toward the school.”

So, this is why it will sound more natural to use (으)로 in a taxi. You’re HEADED to the airport, You’re HEADED to the hotel, etc. The driver’s going to drop you off in the vicinity of your destination.

공항으로 가주세요. – To the airport, please.

Direction words + **(으)로**

We learned about 쪽 and 편 in the last lesson. Anytime that it follows implies “that way” without necessarily pinpointing where, you’d use (으)로 after these words.

So, the common phrase you might hear all the time in a place like a restaurant would be

“이 쪽으로 오세요 – This way, please”

**(으)로 - USING**

The second meaning of (으)로 would be “using”. The keyword to remember about this usage would be “the means”.

So, this one doesn’t attach to locations but nouns.

“카드로 계산할게요” – I’ll pay with the card.

“케첩은 토마토로 만들어요” – Ketchup is made with tomatoes.

In these cases, the word “with” would be the most natural translation. But in this case, it doesn’t mean “Together with”.

“이 전화번호로 문자주세요”- Please text me on this number.

Again, in English, “ON” would be the most suitable preposition but this means “please send me a text USING this phone number.” Therefore 로 is used.

This is why (으)로 is your go-to particle to use when you’re describing how you travel somewhere. Namely, the means of transportation.

If somebody asked you “How did you get here?” and you wanted to say you got here BY a car, you’d just say “I drove here” in English.

But in Korean, “여기로 운전했어요” would be a totally awkward sentence. Instead, you’d say “차로 왔어요”. I got here BY a car.

Another example: “I FLEW to Seoul”. Now, obviously, nobody can actually fly. I don’t know why people say this but I learned to accept it. By the way, 너도 한국어에 대해 이상하게 느끼는 점이 분명히 있을테지만 때로는 “그냥 그런 거구나” 하고 받아들일 줄 알아야 한다. 지금 이 경우처럼 언어라는 게 참 논리적이지만은 않기 때문이다.

So in Korean, we’d say “I came to Seoul by a plane” - “서울에 비행기로 왔어요”

Another very natural way to say these sentences would be using the verb “타다” which means to “get on a VEHICLE.” And in this particular usage, we use “타고”

So you can alternatively say “차(를) 타고 왔어요” or “비행기(를) 타고 왔어요”. The object marker is optional.

I got in the car and then I came

I got in the plane and then I came

Would be the literal translation.

**(으)로 – “As”**

The third use of (으)로 is “as”. By “as”, I mean “Acting as”, or “In the role of”. Or it could even translate to “for” in some cases.

For example, you can say “I got that as a gift” or “I worked there as a manager” or “I had cereal for breakfast”

**So for any noun that you’d treat AS some other noun, you’d also use (으)로.**

**I think this use would be best explained through examples. So here’s a couple**

**저는 저녁으로 치킨을 먹을 거예요**

**너는 크리스마스 선물로 뭐 받았어?**

**식품 회사에서 세일즈 매니저로 일했어요**

**(으)로 – “Choice”**

This one is used when you’re making a choice among several options available.

It kind of feels like you’re LEANING toward your chosen option.

Let’s use steak as an example. You have several options when it comes to your steak. – Rare, Medium and well-done.

You could technically say “미디움 스테이크를 주세요” – Please give me a medium steak.

It makes sense but it sounds awkward.

You kind of want to imply that there are other choices but the one I’M choosing is medium.

**스테이크는 미디움으로 주세요. → Literally, “As for the steak, please give it AS medium.”**

**(으)로 – “Into”**

The next one, it can also mean “into” as in “Changing into”. So it’s only used with verbs that show change, like these ones

바꾸다 – To change (something)

바뀌다 – To change (by itself)

변하다 – To change (by itself) – Sino-Korean.

They all mean “to change” but I’ll get into the specifics about the difference in the next lesson.

For now, all you need to know is that (으)로 is used to say “change into”

**(으)로 – “Due to”, “For”, “From”**

(으)로 can also indicate **the cause** of something. Again, this use is best understood from examples.

이 식당은 삼겹살로 유명해요 – This restaurant is famous FOR pork belly.

저희 할아버지는 암으로 고생하셨어요 – My grandfather suffered FROM cancer.

**Closure**

I know. It’s just one particle with so many uses. It’s like “for” in English.

You can buy a shirt FOR $5.

You can wait FOR a long time.

Or you can do something FOR your boyfriend.

But see how you can almost instantly understand its meaning when you see the word being used in real sentences.
