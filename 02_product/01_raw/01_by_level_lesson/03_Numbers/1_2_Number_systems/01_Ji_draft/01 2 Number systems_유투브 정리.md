**Understanding the Types of Numbers**

Before diving into the Korean systems, it's essential to understand the three types of numbers: **cardinal**, **ordinal**, and **nominal**.

* **Cardinal Numbers** are for counting quantities. For example, in the phrase "there are five apples," the number "five" is a cardinal number.
* **Ordinal Numbers** are for ordering or ranking. Examples include "first," "second," and "third" place in a race.
* **Nominal Numbers** are for identification or names, like phone numbers, zip codes, or jersey numbers. They don't represent a quantity or rank.

In English, you use "one, two, three" for both cardinal and nominal numbers, and "first, second, third" for ordinal numbers. In Korean, however, there are two separate systems: one for nominal numbers and one for cardinal numbers. Ordinal numbers are a variation of the cardinal system.

**The Nominal (Sino-Korean) Number System**

This is the easier of the two systems. You may see these referred to as **Sino-Korean numbers** because they're derived from Chinese characters. The first 10 numbers are:

* 1: **il** (일)
* 2: **i** (이)
* 3: **sam** (삼)
* 4: **sa** (사)
* 5: **o** (오)
* 6: **yuk** (육)
* 7: **chil** (칠)
* 8: **pal** (팔)
* 9: **gu** (구)
* 10: **sip** (십)

This system is additive, meaning you can create any number using just these 10. For example:

* **11** is "ten plus one," or **sip-il** (십일).
* **12** is "ten plus two," or **sip-i** (십이).
* **20** is "two tens," or **i-sip** (이십).
* **99** is "nine tens plus nine," or **gu-sip-gu** (구십구).

This makes learning numbers up to 99 much simpler than in English, where you need to learn 27 different words (eleven, twelve, twenty, thirty, etc.).

**Large Numbers**

Beyond 99, new words are introduced, but the logic remains consistent.

* 100: **baek** (백)
* 1,000: **cheon** (천)
* 10,000: **man** (만)

Unlike English, which groups large numbers by three digits (thousands, millions, billions), Korean groups them by **four digits** because it has a separate word for 10,000 (**man**). This means that a million is not "a thousand thousand," but rather "100 ten-thousands," or **baeng-man** (백만).

The system continues with:

* 100,000: **sim-man** (십만)
* 1,000,000: **baeng-man** (백만)
* 10,000,000: **cheon-man** (천만)
* 100,000,000: **eok** (억)
* 1,000,000,000,000: **jo** (조)

**Negative Numbers and Zero**

* **Negative numbers** are created by adding "minus" or "ma-i-neo-seu" (마이너스) in front of the number. For example, -5 is **ma-i-neo-seu o** (마이너스 오).
* **Zero** can be pronounced as either **yeong** (영) or **gong** (공). **Gong** is more commonly used for nominal numbers like phone numbers and zip codes, while **yeong** is used for math, temperature, and scores.

**The Cardinal (Native Korean) Number System**

This system is used for **cardinal numbers** (counting objects) and only goes up to 99. These are pure Korean words, not derived from Chinese. The first 10 numbers are:

* 1: **hana** (하나)
* 2: **dul** (둘)
* 3: **set** (셋)
* 4: **net** (넷)
* 5: **da-seot** (다섯)
* 6: **yeo-seot** (여섯)
* 7: **il-gop** (일곱)
* 8: **yeo-deol** (여덟)
* 9: **a-hop** (아홉)
* 10: **yeol** (열)

Unlike the nominal system, multiples of 10 have their own unique words.

* 10: **yeol** (열)
* 20: **seu-mul** (스물)
* 30: **seo-reun** (서른)
* 40: **ma-heun** (마흔)
* 50: **swin** (쉰)
* 60: **ye-sun** (예순)
* 70: **il-heun** (일흔)
* 80: **yeo-deun** (여든)
* 90: **a-heun** (아흔)

To form other numbers, you combine these. For example, 22 is **seu-mul dul** (스물 둘). This system doesn't have words for zero or numbers over 99.

**Measure Words Exception**

When counting objects, some numbers change slightly. For example, when using a measure word like **gae** (개) which means "units," the first four numbers are altered:

* 1 object: **han-gae** (한 개) (not *hana-gae*)
* 2 objects: **du-gae** (두 개) (not *dul-gae*)
* 3 objects: **se-gae** (세 개) (not *set-gae*)
* 4 objects: **ne-gae** (네 개) (not *net-gae*)

**Ordinal Numbers**

Ordinal numbers (first, second, third) are a slight variation of cardinal numbers.

* 1st: **cheot-beon-jjae** (첫 번째) or **je-il** (제일)
* 2nd: **du-beon-jjae** (두 번째)
* 3rd: **se-beon-jjae** (세 번째)

The pattern continues from the cardinal numbers with the addition of **-beon-jjae** (번재). The first four numbers (**cheot, du, se, ne**) follow the measure word rule, so it's important to remember those exceptions.

**Practical Applications and Usage**

**Math**

* **Fractions**: Unlike English, which reads the numerator first, Korean reads the **denominator first**. For example, 1/2 is **i bun-ui il** (이 분의 일), which literally translates to "one out of two."
* **Decimals**: The word for "point" is **jeom** (점). You simply read the numbers after the decimal point. For example, 3.14 would be **sam-jeom il-sa** (삼 점 일사).
* **Square Roots**: Use the word **je-gop-geun** (제곱근) followed by the nominal number. The square root of 2 would be **i je-gop-geun** (이 제곱근).
* **Exponents**: Use the ordinal number for the power. For example, 10 to the third power would be **sip-ui se-je-gop** (십의 세제곱).

**Temperature**

* **Degrees**: The word for "degrees" is **do** (도), which uses the nominal number system. 25°C would be **i-sip-o-do** (이십오도) **ssi** (씨).
* **Negative Temperatures**: Use **yeong-ha** (영하), which means "below zero." -40°C would be **yeong-ha sa-sip-do** (영하 사십도).

**Currency**

* Currency always uses the **nominal number system**. For example, one million won is **baeng-man won** (백만 원).

**Dates and Time**

* **Dates** use the **nominal number system** and follow a **year-month-day** format. 2023/09/20 would be **i-cheon i-sip-sam-nyeon gu-wol i-sip-il** (이천 이십삼년 구월 이십일).
  + **Year**: Add **nyeon** (년) after the number.
  + **Month**: Add **wol** (월) after the number.
  + **Day**: Add **il** (일) after the number.
* **Time** is an exception, mixing both systems. The **hour** uses **cardinal numbers** with the measure word exception, while **minutes and seconds** use **nominal numbers**.
  + 1 o'clock: **han-si** (한시) (cardinal)
  + 2 o'clock: **du-si** (두시) (cardinal)
  + 30 minutes: **sam-sip-bun** (삼십분) (nominal)
  + 11:58:38 PM: **bam yeol-han-si o-sip-pal-bun sam-sip-pal-cho** (밤 열한시 오십팔분 삼십팔초)
