### Overview
The instructor introduces the logic behind extremely large Korean numbers, moving far beyond the standard curriculum that usually stops at 10,000. While most learners are familiar with the basic Sino-Korean units, the Korean numbering system operates on a base-10,000 (myriad) system rather than the base-1,000 system used in English. This fundamental difference means that new number words are introduced every four zeros rather than every three.

The lesson is structured to first reinforce the common large numbers used in daily life and finance, such as those used for housing prices or national budgets. It then transitions into the theoretical and historical realm, exploring massive numbers found in science and Buddhist literature. The instructor emphasizes that while many of these terms are not known by the average native speaker, understanding the pattern of adding four zeros allows a learner to navigate any numerical scale in Korean.

### The Base-10,000 System and Common Large Numbers
The foundation of large Korean numbers rests on the first four units: `십 (10)`, `백 (100)`, `천 (1,000)`, and `만 (10,000)`. The instructor notes that these have one, two, three, and four zeros respectively. Once you surpass `만 (10,000)`, the pattern of `십`, `백`, and `천` repeats as prefixes. For example, 100,000 is expressed as `십만 (ten ten-thousands)` and 1,000,000 is `백만 (one hundred ten-thousands)`. This pattern continues until you reach 10,000 of `만`, at which point the system introduces a new primary counter.

The next major unit is `억`, which represents 100,000,000 (one hundred million) and contains eight zeros. In practical terms, `억` is a very common number in Korea when discussing the price of real estate; an apartment in Korea will typically cost several `억`. Following the same logic, after `억`, `십억`, `백억`, and `천억`, the system moves to `조` (trillion), which has twelve zeros. Beyond `조` lies `경`, which has sixteen zeros. The instructor explains that `경` is roughly the limit for financial discussions, potentially appearing in contexts like national budgets or the combined wealth of multiple countries.

---

### Scientific and Buddhist Large Numbers
For contexts involving astronomy, biology, or physics—such as counting atoms in the universe—Korean has specific terms that go far beyond `경`. The instructor lists these in increments of four zeros: `해` (20 zeros), `자` (24 zeros), `양` (28 zeros), `구` (32 zeros), `간` (36 zeros), `정` (40 zeros), `제` (44 zeros), and `극` (48 zeros). Most native speakers are only aware of these up to `해`, and anything beyond that is considered specialized knowledge.

The system then enters a tier of numbers derived from Sanskrit and Buddhist metaphors. `항하사` (52 zeros) literally translates to "sands of the Ganges River," symbolizing an immense quantity. This is followed by `아승기` (56 zeros), meaning "immeasurable number," and `나유타` (60 zeros), which represents a value close to infinity. `불가사의` (64 zeros) means "incomprehensible," and the list culminates in `무량대수` or `무량수` (68 zeros), which signifies an immeasurably big number.

---

### Theoretical Extremes
The instructor concludes with two outliers that exist in the periphery of the language. Some sources list `겁` or `대수` as even higher units, though their exact placement can be inconsistent across different texts. Finally, the instructor mentions `구걸` (Googol), which represents 1 followed by 100 zeros. Despite the existence of these astronomical terms, the instructor advises students that mastering up to `경` is more than sufficient for any practical application of the Korean language.
