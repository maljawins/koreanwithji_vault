## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the two distinct number systems used in the Korean language: Sino-Korean numbers and Pure Native Korean numbers. Sino-Korean numbers are derived from Chinese characters and are generally shorter and easier to memorize for beginners. Pure Native Korean numbers are indigenous to the Korean language and are used for different specific contexts. The lesson covers the formation of numbers from zero up to one million in the Sino-Korean system and from one to ninety-nine in the Native system.

The primary challenge for learners is understanding which system to use in a given situation. Sino-Korean numbers are used for dates, money, floor numbers, minutes, phone numbers, and measurements like length, weight, and height. In contrast, Pure Native Korean numbers are used for counting physical objects, people, and stating one's age. The instructor emphasizes that while the vocabulary differs, the mathematical logic of combining numbers to form larger figures remains consistent across both systems.

---

### Sino-Korean Numbers: 1 to 10
The foundation of the Sino-Korean system lies in the first ten digits. The instructor presents these clearly on screen with their corresponding Hangul and phonetic guides. The numbers are: `일 (one)`, `이 (two)`, `삼 (three)`, `사 (four)`, `오 (five)`, `육 (six)`, `칠 (seven)`, `팔 (eight)`, `구 (nine)`, and `십 (ten)`. Memorizing these ten words is essential because all subsequent numbers are built by combining these basic units.

---

### Building Larger Sino-Korean Numbers
To form numbers between 11 and 19, the logic is "ten + digit." For example, 11 is formed by taking `십 (ten)` and adding `일 (one)` to create `십일`. The instructor visually demonstrates this transformation on screen, showing the "10" and "1" merging into "11." This pattern continues through `십이 (12)`, `십삼 (13)`, and so on, up to `십구 (19)`.

For multiples of ten (20, 30, 40, etc.), the logic reverses to "digit + ten." Thus, 20 is `이십 (two-ten)`, 30 is `삼십 (three-ten)`, and 90 is `구십 (nine-ten)`. To create a specific number like 21, you combine these rules: `이십 (20)` + `일 (1)` = `이십일`. The instructor provides numerous visual examples, such as 89 being `팔십구` and 75 being `칠십오`.

---

### Sino-Korean Numbers: 100 to 1,000,000
As the numbers grow, new unit words are introduced. The word for 100 is `백`. Just like with tens, 200 is `이백 (two-hundred)` and 500 is `오백`. The word for 1,000 is `천`. Therefore, 2,000 is `이천` and 9,000 is `구천`. The instructor shows how to read complex years, such as 2020 being `이천이십`.

A significant point of difference from English occurs at 10,000. While English counts in groups of three zeros (ten thousand), Korean uses a base-four system for large numbers. The word for 10,000 is `만`. It is incorrect to say "ten-thousand" (`십천`); one must use `만`. Consequently, 100,000 is `십만 (ten-ten-thousands)` and 1,000,000 is `백만 (hundred-ten-thousands)`. The instructor uses color-coded text on screen to show how these large numbers are partitioned into these Korean units.

---

### Pure Native Korean Numbers: 1 to 99
The instructor transitions to the Native Korean system, noting that it is only used for numbers up to 99. Beyond 100, Koreans default back to the Sino-Korean system. The first ten native numbers are: `하나 (one)`, `둘 (two)`, `셋 (three)`, `넷 (four)`, `다섯 (five)`, `여섯 (six)`, `일곱 (seven)`, `여덟 (eight)`, `아홉 (nine)`, and `열 (ten)`.

Unlike the Sino-Korean system where multiples of ten are simply "digit + 십," the Native system has unique words for each ten-unit:
- 20: `스물`
- 30: `서른`
- 40: `마흔`
- 50: `쉰`
- 60: `예순`
- 70: `일흔`
- 80: `여든`
- 90: `아흔`

To form a number like 11 in the native system, you combine `열 (10)` and `하나 (1)` to get `열하나`. For 25, you combine `스물 (20)` and `다섯 (5)` to get `스물다섯`. The instructor highlights that while the names are longer, the additive logic is identical to the Sino-Korean system.

---

### Zero and Final Review
The lesson concludes with the words for zero. In Sino-Korean contexts (like phone numbers or math), zero is `영`. In more casual or specific native contexts, it can be referred to as `빵` (which also means bread). The instructor finishes with a quiz, challenging the viewer to provide both the Sino-Korean and Native Korean names for various numbers to ensure they can distinguish between the two systems.
