## Teaching Points

### Overview
Korean utilizes two distinct number systems: Sino-Korean numbers (derived from Chinese) and Pure Korean numbers (native to the Korean language). Choosing the correct system depends entirely on the context of what is being counted or described. While both systems can be used for simple counting in isolation—`일 (one), 이 (two), 삼 (three)` for Sino-Korean and `하나 (one), 둘 (two), 셋 (three)` for Pure Korean—they are generally not interchangeable in practical usage.

The instructor provides a comprehensive guide to navigating these systems, emphasizing that Sino-Korean numbers tend to feel more formal, official, or "fancy," whereas Pure Korean numbers are used for more everyday, physical, or living things. The lesson covers specific categories for each, including money, dates, measurements, time, age, and physical objects, while also addressing common exceptions and the modern tendency to switch systems as numbers get larger.

---

### Sino-Korean Numbers: Formal and Official Contexts
Sino-Korean numbers are the standard for anything considered official or formal. The instructor illustrates this by noting that a high-stakes countdown, such as a space agency launch, would exclusively use Sino-Korean: `십 (10), 구 (9), 팔 (8), 칠 (7)`. This system is also the default for all large numbers. While Pure Korean numbers exist up to 99, it is mandatory to switch to Sino-Korean for any number 100 or higher, starting with `백 (100)`. In modern daily life, most speakers actually make this switch much earlier, often transitioning to Sino-Korean once they reach 40 or 50, and occasionally as low as 20.

---

### Money, Addresses, and Identification
Anything involving currency is strictly Sino-Korean, regardless of how small the amount is. This applies to Korean `원 (Won)`, dollars, and cents. For example, the smallest common bill is `천원 (1,000 won)`. Similarly, when numbers are used as identifiers rather than quantities, Sino-Korean is used. This includes phone numbers, addresses, zip codes, apartment numbers, and floor levels. For instance, bus number 5000 is referred to as `오천 번 버스 (Bus No. 5000)`, and apartment 100 is `백호 (Unit 100)`. A minor exception for phone numbers is the use of `공` for zero, which is a native Korean reading often preferred in that specific context.

---

### Dates and Measurements
Dates are handled entirely through the Sino-Korean system. This includes the year, such as `이천 이십 사년 (2024)`, the months, such as `일월 (January)` and `이월 (February)`, and the days of the month, such as `일일 (1st)` or `십일 (10th)`. Furthermore, any measurement involving a tool—distance, weight, volume, length, or height—requires Sino-Korean numbers. If you are describing the temperature, you use the counter `도 (degrees)` with Sino-Korean, such as `삼십도 (30 degrees)`. Essentially, if a ruler, scale, or thermometer is involved, the Sino-Korean system is the correct choice.

---

### Pure Korean Numbers: Living Things and Small Quantities
Pure Korean numbers are typically reserved for smaller amounts and things that are alive. When counting people, the counter `명` is used: `한 명 (one person), 두 명 (two people)`. For animals, the counter is `마리`: `한 마리 (one animal), 두 마리 (two animals)`. This system is also used for simple, informal counting, such as the "1, 2, 3" used before taking a photo: `하나, 둘, 셋`. Using Sino-Korean in these casual, small-scale counting situations would sound awkward to a native speaker.

---

### Age and the Two Systems
Age is a unique category that can use either system depending on the counter chosen. The most common way to state age is using Pure Korean numbers with the counter `살`. For example, `한 살 (one year old)`, `열 살 (10 years old)`, or `스무 살 (20 years old)`. Unlike other categories where speakers switch to Sino-Korean at 40 or 50, it is perfectly acceptable to use Pure Korean for age even into the 80s or 90s. However, there is a Sino-Korean alternative: the counter `세`. When using `세`, you must use Sino-Korean numbers. This version is considered more formal and polite, often appearing in official documents or very respectful speech.

---

### The Tangibility Rule and Time Exceptions
A helpful, though not absolute, rule of thumb is that Pure Korean numbers are used for physical, tangible objects you can touch, while Sino-Korean numbers are used for intangible concepts like measurements or identification numbers. For example, counting physical rocks would use Pure Korean. However, there are notable exceptions. The most prominent is time: hours use Pure Korean numbers with the counter `시` (e.g., `한 시` for one o'clock), while minutes and seconds use Sino-Korean numbers with `분` and `초` (e.g., `십 분` for 10 minutes). Another exception is `번 (times/instances)`, which uses Pure Korean (e.g., `한 번` for one time) despite being intangible.

The instructor also notes that some items can be counted in two ways depending on the focus. If you are counting physical sheets of paper in a book, you use the Pure Korean counter `장`. If you are referring to the abstract page numbers of a book, you use the Sino-Korean `페이지`.
