### The Three Types of Numbers
Before diving into Korean specifically, the instructor defines three categories of numbers used in general linguistics. Cardinal numbers are for counting quantity, such as saying there are five items in a photo. Ordinal numbers are for ordering or ranking, such as first, second, or third place in a race. Nominal numbers are for identification or names, such as phone numbers, zip codes, or jersey numbers, where the number does not represent a quantity or a rank. While English uses the same set of numbers for cardinal and nominal purposes, Korean uses one system for nominal and another for cardinal.

---

### Sino-Korean Numbers (Nominal System)
The first system taught is the Sino-Korean system, often referred to as the nominal system. These numbers are derived from Chinese characters (Hanja), which is why they sound similar to Mandarin. The instructor lists the first ten: 1 is `일`, 2 is `이`, 3 is `삼`, 4 is `사`, 5 is `오`, 6 is `육`, 7 is `칠`, 8 is `팔`, 9 is `구`, and 10 is `십`. A pronunciation note is provided for 6 (`육`): in certain linguistic contexts or when counting quickly, it may sound like `륙` with an "R" sound, though this is best learned through conversational practice.

The strength of this system is its mathematical logic. To create any number from 11 to 99, you simply combine these ten words. For example, 11 is 10 + 1, or `십일`. 12 is 10 + 2, or `십이`. To say 20, you say "two tens" (`이십`). Therefore, 22 is "two tens plus two" (`이십이`). 99 is "nine tens plus nine" (`구십구`). This structure makes math more intuitive in Korean than in English, where words like "eleven" or "twenty" must be memorized as unique entities.

### Large Numbers and the Four-Digit Chunking Rule
As numbers get larger, the Sino-Korean system continues its logical progression. 100 is `백`, 1,000 is `천`, and 10,000 is `만`. The instructor highlights a major difference between Korean and English: while English groups numbers by three digits (thousands, millions, billions), Korean groups them by four digits. After `천` (1,000), the next major unit is `만` (10,000). Then comes `십만` (100,000), `백만` (1,000,000), and `천만` (10,000,000). The next unique word is `억` for 100,000,000.

This difference often leads to translation errors on social media platforms like Instagram, where follower counts might be literally translated into awkward Korean phrasing. ### Zero and Negatives
Negative numbers are expressed by placing the word `마이너스` (minus) before the number, such as `마이너스 오` (-5). For the number zero, Korean has two words: `영` and `공`. `공` is most common for nominal uses like phone numbers (e.g., 010 is `공일공`) or zip codes. `영` is used in math, temperature, or sports scores (zero points). The instructor shares a "genius wordplay" example from a Big Bang song where the word `공` is used to mean both "zero" and "ball," implying a bank account has so many zeros that it looks like a pool table full of balls. Additionally, the informal word `빵` can be used for "zero points," similar to "zilch" in English.

---

### Native Korean Numbers (Cardinal System)
The second system is the Native Korean or "pure" Korean system, used primarily for counting (cardinal numbers). These sound entirely different from the Sino-Korean set: 1 is `하나`, 2 is `둘`, 3 is `셋`, 4 is `넷`, 5 is `다섯`, 6 is `여섯`, 7 is `일곱`, 8 is `여덟`, 9 is `아홉`, and 10 is `열`. Unlike the Sino-Korean system, the multiples of ten in this system have unique names that must be memorized: 20 is `스물`, 30 is `서른`, 40 is `마흔`, 50 is `신`, 60 is `예순`, 70 is `이른`, 80 is `여든`, and 90 is `아흔`.

This system generally only goes up to 99. For 100 and above, Koreans typically switch to the Sino-Korean system. The most common everyday use for Native Korean numbers is stating one's age. For example, a 20-year-old would say they are `스물` years old.

---

### Measure Words and Ordinal Variations
Korean uses "measure words" (counters) after numbers to denote units, similar to saying "five blades of grass." When using Native Korean numbers with measure words, the first four numbers change their form: `하나` becomes `한`, `둘` becomes `두`, `셋` becomes `세`, and `넷` becomes `네`. For example, "one object" is `한 개`, not `하나 개`.

Ordinal numbers (first, second, etc.) are variations of the Native Korean system. "First" is an exception: `첫 번째`. From there, the pattern uses the modified cardinal form + `번째`: `두 번째` (second), `세 번째` (third), `네 번째` (fourth), and so on. Beyond four, the numbers return to their standard cardinal form, such as `다섯 번째` (fifth).

---

### Mathematics, Fractions, and Decimals
In mathematics, Sino-Korean numbers are the standard. Fractions are read by stating the denominator first, followed by `분의`, then the numerator. For example, 1/2 is `이분의 일` (literally "out of two parts, one"). Decimals use the word `점` (point). For 3.14, you say `삼 점 일사`. A specific nuance for zero in decimals is that it is usually `영` before the decimal point and `공` after it (e.g., 0.102 is `영 점 일공이`).

For advanced math, square roots use the loanword `루트` (e.g., `루트 이` for √2). Exponents use `제국` (power). Interestingly, 10 to the 3rd power (`10의 세제국`) combines a Sino-Korean base with a Native Korean exponent. While the word `승` was used for power in the past, `제국` is now considered more proper.

---

### Temperature and Currency
Temperature uses the word `도` (degrees) with Sino-Korean numbers. Celsius is `섭씨` and Fahrenheit is `화씨`, placed before the number (e.g., `섭씨 이십오도`). Negative temperatures use the word `영하` (below zero), while positive temperatures can be emphasized with `영상` (above zero). Currency also exclusively uses the Sino-Korean system followed by `원` (won), such as `백만원` (one million won).

---

### Dates and Time
Korea uses a descending Year-Month-Day format. Years and days use Sino-Korean numbers followed by `년` (year) and `일` (day). Months are simply the number 1–12 followed by `월`. There are two pronunciation exceptions for months to make them sound more natural: June is `유월` (not `육월`) and October is `시월` (not `십월`).

Telling time is the most complex application because it mixes both systems. Hours use Native Korean numbers with the word `시`, while minutes and seconds use Sino-Korean numbers with `분` and `초`. For example, 1:30:50 is `한시 삼십분 오십초`. The instructor suggests thinking of the hour as an ordinal ("the first hour"), which helps explain why it uses the Native system. If using a 24-hour clock (military time), Koreans often switch entirely to Sino-Korean (e.g., 13:00 is `십삼시`).
