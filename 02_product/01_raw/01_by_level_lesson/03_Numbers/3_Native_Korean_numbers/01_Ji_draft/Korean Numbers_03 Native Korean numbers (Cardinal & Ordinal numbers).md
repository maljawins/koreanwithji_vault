**Cardinal Number System**

All this time we were talking about just one system – the nominal numbers.

You might be thinking “How much more can there possibly be?” Well, don't worry, the second system is much shorter.

In this lesson, we’re going to talk about the cardinal numbers. To remind you, cardinal numbers are for counting.

You might see these being referred to as native Korean numbers or pure Korean numbers. This means that unlike Sino-Korean numbers, these aren't derived from Chinese characters.

And let me just tell you that these numbers sound completely different from the other number system that we just learned, so pay close attention.

One is 하나. Sometimes we say 하나 instead of 일 to avoid confusion with pronunciation because one and two in Sino-Korean - 일 and 이 - sound sort of similar. So if your phone number contains the numbers 2-1-5-4, although you could pronounce this as 이일오사, you could alternatively say 이하나오사 to differentiate the number one.

So in native Korean:

* One is 하나.
* Two is 둘
* Three is 셋
* Four is 넷
* Five is 다섯
* Six is 여섯
* Seven is 일곱
* Eight is 여덟
* Nine is 아홉
* Ten is 열

If you remember, the nominal number system allowed us to make any other numbers with just the first ten. For example, since 십 is 10 and 이 is 2, 십이 would be 12, and 이십 would be 20.

This isn't exactly the case with these cardinal numbers here. 열 is 10 and 둘 is two, so does 열둘 mean twelve? Yes.

But does 둘열 mean 20? No. 20, 30, and the other multiples of 10 have their own special word, just like English. So we need to learn them separately.

Here are the multiples of 10 in cardinal numbers:

* 10 is 열
* 20 is 스물
* 30 is 서른
* 40 is 마흔
* 50 is 쉰
* 60 is 예순
* 70 is 일흔
* 80 is 여든
* 90 is 아흔

Wait but what comes after 99? What's a hundred in native Korean?

Well, a hundred doesn't exist in the native Korean number system. It only goes from 1 to 99 - not even zero exists here.

If you want to count over 100, you’d have to switch to the nominal system and say 백 and go from there, so that 101 would be 백하나 and so on. But Koreans don't commonly do this, and we usually only go up to 99 in cardinal numbers.

So for example, the name of this film, is 백한마리 달마시안. Notice this word “마리”. In Korean, there’s a concept called “Counter words”. And “마리” is used for counting animals specifically.

This concept doesn't exist in English, so it deserves an entire lesson on its own. I'll talk about counter words in detail in the next lesson.

But the key takeaway is that in Korean, we put an additional word after a number to denote how many units of an object there are.

This is similar to how in English, you’d say "two cups of water" Instead of "two waters." Here the word "cups" denotes how many units of water there are. It's the same thing in Korean, except you do this for almost everything you can think of, not just for drinks.

The most notable example of cardinal numbers in everyday life would be for age. If someone asked me how old I was this year, I would say 서른여섯살 with 살 being the counter word for age.

Spoiler alert: We have a different age system in Korea and I’ll talk about this in the lesson “Age” along with the counter words.

**Exceptions with Counter Words**

Now, there are a few exceptions when counting in cardinal numbers, depending on whether you use counter words.

I'll use the most common Korean counter word for this example: 개, which is used for counting objects generally.

Again, the counter word comes after a number to show how many units of the thing there are.

Although one is 하나, one object would be 한개, not 하나개.

Two is 둘, but two objects would be 두개, not 둘개.

Three is 셋, but three objects would be 세개, not 셋개.

Four is 넷, but four objects would be 네개, not 넷개.

This exception includes any other numbers related to these four—like 11 to 14, 21 to 24, and so on.

Other numbers from five to nine stay the same. For example, five is 다섯, so five objects would be 다섯개, and six objects would be 여섯개.

**The 20 Exception**

Now, here's one exception you need to remember: the number 20—스물.

When you attach a counter word to 스물, it changes to 스무.

So "20 objects" would be 스무개, not 스물개.

"20 years old" would be 스무살, not 스물살.

But from 21 onwards, you use the full form 스물 again: 스물한개, 스물두개, and so on.

Only the twenty by itself changes to 스무 when paired with a counter.

**Ordinal Number System**

Alright, so far, we’ve covered nominal and cardinal numbers. And now, we have just one more to go: ordinal numbers, which is for ordering, like first, second, and third.

Ordinal numbers are just a slight variation of cardinal numbers, and they're really similar to the counter words exception that we just talked about.

* One is 하나, first is 첫째 or 첫번째
* Two is 둘, second is 둘째 or 두번째
* Three is 셋, third is 셋째 or 세번째
* Four is 넷, fourth is 넷째 or 네번째

The fact that the word "first" is called 첫(번)째 is an exception to remember.

From the fifth on, it’s always 번째 not just 째 after the number. As you can see, the exceptions are usually related to one, two, three, and four.

* Five is 다섯, fifth is 다섯번째
* Six is 여섯, sixth is 여섯번째
* Seven is 일곱, seventh is 일곱번째
* Eight is 여덟, eighth is 여덟번째
* Nine is 아홉, ninth is 아홉번째
* Ten is 열, tenth is 열번째

**Exceptions for Ordinal Numbers**

Now let me talk about the exceptions for ordinal numbers.

These are actually similar to the exceptions for counter words I mentioned earlier.

For numbers that end in one—like eleventh, twenty-first, and so on—the words end in 한번째.

So eleventh is 열한번째, not 열첫번째.

Twelfth is 열두번째—similar to 22nd, 32nd, and so on.

13th is 열세번째—similar to 23rd, 33rd, and so on.

14th is 열네번째—similar to 24th, 34th, and so on.

**The 20th Exception**

And twentieth is 스무번째, not 스물번째.

Just like with counter words, 스물 changes to 스무 when paired with 번째.

Anything beyond that follows the same pattern.

But I doubt you'll be counting something to the 13th or 14th place, so knowing only up to 10th place is probably enough.
