## Teaching Points

### Overview
This lesson introduces the Native Korean (Pure Korean) number system, which exists alongside the Sino-Korean system covered in previous episodes. The instructor explains that while Sino-Korean numbers are used for things like currency (Won), they cannot be used to count everything. Specifically, counting physical objects and people requires the Native Korean system.

The learning arc begins with the basic numbers 1 through 10, moves into the teens, and then covers the unique names for multiples of ten (20, 30, 40, etc.). The lesson also introduces the concept of "counters," which are specific words required when counting different categories of items. Finally, the instructor provides practical usage tips, noting that Native Korean numbers are rarely used for values above 60, at which point speakers typically revert to the Sino-Korean system.

---

### Why Two Number Systems?
The instructor illustrates the necessity of the Native Korean system by comparing mathematical numbers to counting items. While one can say the number "five" using the Sino-Korean `오 (five)`, this cannot be used to count "five people." To count people, one must use the Native Korean number system combined with a specific counter. A visual on screen clarifies that while `사람` means "person" or "people," it is not the word used as a counter for people. This leads to the introduction of "Korean Counters," which are category-specific words like `원 (Won)` for money. Different counters pair with different number systems; for example, `원` pairs with Sino-Korean numbers, while many other common counters pair with Native Korean numbers.

### Native Korean Numbers 1 to 10
The instructor presents the first ten numbers in the Native Korean system. A visual chart displays the digits alongside their Hangul spellings:

- **1:** 하나
- **2:** 둘
- **3:** 셋
- **4:** 넷
- **5:** 다섯
- **6:** 여섯
- **7:** 일곱
- **8:** 여덟
- **9:** 아홉
- **10:** 열

A specific pronunciation note is provided for the number eight: `여덟` is pronounced as `여덜` due to standard Korean sound change rules where only one consonant in a final double-consonant cluster is voiced.

---

### Teens and Multiples of Ten
To form numbers from 11 to 19, the system follows a logic similar to the Sino-Korean system: take the word for ten (`열`) and attach the single digit. For example, 11 is formed by combining `열 (ten)` and `하나 (one)` to create `열하나`. The instructor lists the full sequence from 11 to 19: `열하나`, `열둘`, `열셋`, `열넷`, `열다섯`, `열여섯`, `열일곱`, `열여덟`, and `열아홉`.

The system diverges significantly from Sino-Korean when reaching 20. Unlike the Sino-Korean system, which reuses the numbers 1-10 to build multiples (e.g., "two-ten" for twenty), the Native Korean system has unique, distinct names for every multiple of ten up to 90:

- **10:** 열
- **20:** 스물
- **30:** 서른
- **40:** 마흔
- **50:** 쉰
- **60:** 예순
- **70:** 일흔
- **80:** 여든
- **90:** 아흔

To form a number like 21, you combine the word for twenty (`스물`) with the word for one (`하나`) to get `스물하나`.

---

### Practical Usage and Limits
The instructor offers a crucial tip regarding the frequency of these numbers. While it is important to be aware of the words for 60 through 90, they are rarely used in modern daily conversation. Most Koreans switch to the Sino-Korean system once a number exceeds 40 or 50. Furthermore, any number above 99 is exclusively expressed using the Sino-Korean system. To practice, the instructor suggests counting steps while walking or climbing stairs, as this builds the muscle memory needed to recall the numbers quickly without hesitation.
