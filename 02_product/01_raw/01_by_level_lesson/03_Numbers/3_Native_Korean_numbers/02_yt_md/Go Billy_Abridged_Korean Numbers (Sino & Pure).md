## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the concept of dual number systems in Korean: Sino-Korean and Pure Korean. To help English speakers understand why a language would need two systems, he uses the analogy of Arabic numerals versus Roman numerals. While English uses Arabic numerals for almost everything, Roman numerals are still used in specific contexts like movie titles (e.g., Rocky II) or copyright dates. Similarly, Korean uses its two systems for very specific, non-overlapping purposes.

The lesson arc begins with the Sino-Korean system, covering the basic digits, the logic for building large numbers up to nearly 100 million, and the specific categories where these numbers are required. The second half of the lesson focuses on Pure Korean numbers, their unique counters, and the grammatical changes that occur when certain numbers are used as adjectives. The instructor emphasizes that while the systems may seem daunting, learners only need to master the logic of how they combine with specific "counters" to describe the world.

### Sino-Korean Numbers: Logic and Large Values
Sino-Korean numbers are based on Chinese characters and are used for mathematical or "general" numbering. The instructor lists the first ten digits: `일 (1)`, `이 (2)`, `삼 (3)`, `사 (4)`, `오 (5)`, `육 (6)`, `칠 (7)`, `팔 (8)`, `구 (9)`, and `십 (10)`. Building larger numbers is a matter of simple multiplication and addition based on place value. For example, to say 12, you say `십이` (10 + 2). To say 56, you identify five tens and six ones: `오십육` (5 x 10 + 6).

As numbers grow, new units are introduced. The instructor explains that `백` is 100, `천` is 1,000, and `만` is 10,000. A crucial difference between Korean and English is that Korean counts in units of 10,000 (`만`) rather than 1,000. Therefore, 100,000 is expressed as "ten ten-thousands" (`십만`), and 1,000,000 is "one hundred ten-thousands" (`백만`). The instructor demonstrates this by constructing the largest possible number using these units: 99,999,999, which is `구천구백구십구만 구천구백구십구`. He notes that once you hit 100,000,000, a new unit called `억` is used, though it is beyond the scope of this basic lesson.

### When to Use Sino-Korean Numbers
Sino-Korean numbers are the default for measurements and abstract data. The instructor outlines several key categories. First, they are used for "general numbers" like phone numbers, addresses, and floor numbers (using the counter `층`). Second, they are used for all forms of currency, including the Korean `원 (won)` and foreign currencies like `달러 (dollar)`. Third, all scientific measurements such as `미터 (meters)` and `도 (degrees)` for temperature require this system.

Dates and specific time units also rely on Sino-Korean. Years, months, and days are counted this way. Months are simply the number plus `월 (month)`, such as `일월 (January)` and `이월 (February)`. The instructor highlights two important phonetic exceptions: June is `유월` (not 육월) and October is `시월` (not 십월). Finally, while hours use Pure Korean, the smaller units of time—`분 (minutes)` and `초 (seconds)`—always use Sino-Korean.

---

### Pure Korean Numbers and the Power of Counters
Pure Korean numbers are native to the language and are primarily used for counting physical objects and people. The digits 1 through 10 are `하나`, `둘`, `셋`, `넷`, `다섯`, `여섯`, `일곱`, `여덟`, `아홉`, and `열`. Unlike the Sino-Korean system, Pure Korean has unique words for the tens places: `스물 (20)`, `서른 (30)`, and `마흔 (40)`. The instructor notes that most modern Koreans switch to Sino-Korean numbers once they get past 40 or 50 because the Pure Korean terms for 60, 70, and so on are rarely used in daily life.

The most critical aspect of using Pure Korean numbers is the "counter" system. In Korean, you cannot just say "three books"; you must say "book three [volume-counter]." The instructor introduces several essential counters: `명` for people, `마리` for animals, and `개` as a general counter for inanimate objects. He demonstrates the sentence order: Noun + Number + Counter. For example, "five mice" is `쥐 다섯 마리`. Other specific counters include `병` for bottles, `잔` for cups/glasses, `장` for flat sheets (paper or t-shirts), and `권` for books.

---

### Grammatical Changes and Ordinal Numbers
The instructor explains that the numbers 1, 2, 3, 4, and 20 undergo a spelling change when they are followed immediately by a counter. `하나` becomes `한`, `둘` becomes `두`, `셋` becomes `세`, `넷` becomes `네`, and `스물` becomes `스무`. This is why "one o'clock" is `한 시` and "twenty people" is `스무 명`. However, if the number is part of a larger compound, like 21, only the final digit changes if applicable (e.g., `스물한 명`).

The lesson concludes with ordinal numbers (first, second, third). To turn a Pure Korean number into an ordinal, you add `번째`. For example, "second" is `두 번째` and "third" is `세 번째`. The word for "first" is an exception: instead of using the number one, you use the word `첫` to create `첫 번째`. Finally, the instructor mentions that Pure Korean is used for counting "times" or iterations using the counter `번`, and it is the system used when counting down to take a photo: `하나, 둘, 셋!`
