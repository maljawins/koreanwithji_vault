## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor addresses a common point of confusion for Korean learners: the existence of two distinct systems for counting days. While most students are familiar with the Sino-Korean system using the counter 일 (day), there is a traditional Pure Korean system that is still widely used in daily life. The lesson focuses on identifying which terms from the Pure Korean system are essential for fluency and which are archaic or rarely used. The instructor emphasizes that while the Sino-Korean system is versatile, the Pure Korean system is restricted to counting durations and cannot be used for calendar dates.

---

### The Sino-Korean System: 일 vs 날

The most common way to count days is by using the counter **일 (day)**. The instructor clarifies that **일** is strictly a counter and cannot stand alone as a noun meaning "day" in a general sense. For example, if you want to say "I want to meet you another day," you must use the word **날 (day)** instead of **일**. To count days using this system, you simply place a Sino-Korean number before the counter. Because the Sino-Korean number for "one" is also **일**, the term for "one day" becomes **일일 (one day)**. This continues logically with **이일 (two days)** and **삼일 (three days)**.

A key feature of the Sino-Korean system is its dual purpose. These terms can represent both a duration of time and a specific date on the calendar. Therefore, **일일** can mean "one day" or "the 1st of the month." Similarly, **십일** can mean "ten days" or "the 10th," and **삼십일** can mean "thirty days" or "the 30th."

---

### The Pure Korean System: Essential Terms

The second system for counting days uses Pure Korean vocabulary. Unlike the Sino-Korean system, these terms are used exclusively for counting the number of days (duration) and never for calendar dates. The instructor identifies the first four terms as extremely common and essential for learners to memorize:

- **하루** / one day
- **이틀** / two days
- **사흘** / three days
- **나흘** / four days

The instructor highlights a frequent point of confusion for learners regarding **사흘 (three days)**. Because it starts with the sound "sa," many mistakenly assume it refers to four days (since **사** is the Sino-Korean number for four). However, **사흘** is three days, and **나흘** is four days. These terms are interchangeable with their Sino-Korean counterparts (**일일/하루**, **이일/이틀**, **삼일/사흘**, **사일/나흘**) when counting duration, but you must never use **사흘** to mean "the 3rd of the month."

---

### Advanced and Archaic Pure Korean Days

Beyond the first four days, the Pure Korean system continues, though these terms are much less common in modern speech. The instructor provides the list from five to nine days for reference:

- **닷새** / five days
- **엿새** / six days
- **이레** / seven days
- **여드레** / eight days
- **아흐레** / nine days

While these terms are derived from the Pure Korean numbers (**다섯, 여섯, 일곱, 여덟, 아홉**), the instructor notes that even native speakers rarely use them in daily conversation, typically opting for the Sino-Korean system instead. However, the term for ten days, **열흘 (ten days)**, remains very common and is worth learning. It is easy to remember because it incorporates the Pure Korean word for ten, **열**.

---

### Special Terms and Longer Durations

The instructor mentions that while one could theoretically continue counting by adding numbers after ten (e.g., **열하루** for 11 days, **열이틀** for 12 days), it is practically never done in modern Korean. There are, however, a few specific terms for longer durations that still appear in conversation or specific contexts:

- **보름** / fifteen days
- **스무날** / twenty days
- **그믐** / thirty days

**보름** is quite common and is often used to refer to a fortnight or the half-month mark. **그믐** has a specific nuance related to the lunar calendar, where it signifies the very last day of the month. On a standard solar calendar, it simply refers to a thirty-day period. The instructor concludes by reiterating that for practical purposes, learners should focus on mastering 1 through 4 (**하루, 이틀, 사흘, 나흘**), 10 (**열흘**), 15 (**보름**), and 30 (**그믐**).
