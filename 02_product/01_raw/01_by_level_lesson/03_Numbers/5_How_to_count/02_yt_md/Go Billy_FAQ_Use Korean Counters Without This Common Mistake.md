## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor addresses a common hurdle for Korean learners: the natural use of counters. While many students learn the basic mechanics of counting—pairing a noun with a specific counter and the correct number system—they often fall into patterns of speech that sound repetitive or unnatural to native speakers. The lesson focuses on two primary areas of improvement: avoiding the redundant use of grammatical markers and eliminating unnecessary nouns when the counter or context already provides sufficient information. By mastering these nuances, learners can move beyond technically correct sentences to more fluid, native-like Korean.

---

### Common Number and Pronunciation Pitfalls

Before diving into counters, the instructor briefly highlights other frequent mistakes related to the Korean number system. One such error involves pronunciation, specifically the word for sixteen, **십육**, which is actually pronounced as `심뉵`. Another common mistake occurs with specific months of the year. While most months simply use the number plus **월 (month)**, June and October are exceptions. Instead of using the full number names, they are shortened: June is **유월** (not 육월) and October is **시월** (not 십월).

---

### Avoiding Redundant Particles

A major mistake learners make when using counters is overusing subject or object markers. In a standard counting sentence, a marker is required, but it should only appear once—either after the noun being counted or after the counter itself. The instructor demonstrates this using the example of counting three monkeys:

- **원숭이 세 마리가 있어요.** / There are three monkeys. (Correct: Marker after counter)
- **원숭이가 세 마리 있어요.** / There are three monkeys. (Correct: Marker after noun)
- **원숭이가 세 마리가 있어요.** (Incorrect: Redundant markers)

Using two subject markers in this context sounds repetitive and awkward. The same rule applies to the object marker **을/를**. When saying "I bought five books," one should choose one position for the marker:

- **책 다섯 권을 샀어요.** / I bought five books.
- **책을 다섯 권 샀어요.** / I bought five books.
- **책을 다섯 권을 샀어요.** (Incorrect: Redundant markers)

The instructor notes that while you can add other particles for different meanings—such as adding **도 (also)** to say **원숭이도 세 마리가 있어요** (There are also three monkeys)—you must avoid doubling up on the same functional marker.

---

### Eliminating Redundant Nouns

The most significant tip for sounding natural is knowing when to drop the noun entirely. Many Korean counters are specific enough that the noun becomes redundant. The instructor highlights the counter **명 (people)** as the prime example. Because **명** is only used for humans, including the word **사람 (person/people)** is often unnecessary.

> **다섯 명을 만났어요.** / I met five people.

While **사람 다섯 명을 만났어요** is grammatically correct, it sounds repetitive because the counter already tells the listener you are talking about people.

This principle extends to other counters based on context. For instance, **마리** is a general animal counter. If you are looking at a group of monkeys and someone asks how many there are, you should simply say **세 마리가 있어요** (There are three) rather than repeating the word for monkey. Similarly, **권** is the specific counter for books. If the context of a bookstore is established, saying **다섯 권을 샀어요** is more natural than repeating **책 (book)**.

---

### When to Keep the Noun

The instructor clarifies that nouns should be kept when they provide necessary specificity that the counter alone cannot provide. Since **마리** can refer to any animal, you need the word **원숭이 (monkey)** if you need to specify which animal you are counting out of a group of different animals. Likewise, if you want to specify a sub-type of an object, such as **역사책 (history book)** instead of just a general book, you would include it:

> **역사책 다섯 권을 샀어요.** / I bought five history books.

However, even in these cases, if the specific noun was just mentioned in the previous sentence or question, it should be dropped in the response to avoid sounding like a "repetitive robot." The ultimate goal is to use the counter as the primary carrier of information whenever the context allows.
