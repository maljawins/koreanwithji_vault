## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces the fundamental concept that Korean utilizes two distinct number systems: Sino-Korean and Pure Korean. The choice between these systems depends entirely on what is being counted. This lesson serves as the first part of a series on counting, specifically focusing on the Sino-Korean system, its formation, and its primary applications in daily life, such as counting money and using scientific measurements.

---

### The Two Number Systems

Korean learners must navigate two sets of numbers for values between 1 and 99. The instructor explains that Sino-Korean numbers are derived from Chinese, while Pure Korean numbers are indigenous to the Korean language. A key distinction is that Pure Korean numbers only exist up to 99; for any value 100 or higher, only the Sino-Korean system is used. To illustrate the difference, the instructor shows how "one" can be either `일 (Sino)` or `하나 (Pure)`, "ten" can be `십 (Sino)` or `열 (Pure)`, and "ninety-three" can be `구십삼 (Sino)` or `아흔셋 (Pure)`.

The usage of these systems is strictly divided by category. Sino-Korean numbers are the most common and are used for counting money, days, and time (minutes/seconds), as well as for all types of measurements. Pure Korean numbers are reserved for counting people, animals, most physical objects, and expressing one's age in years.

### Forming Numbers 11 to 99

Creating larger numbers in Sino-Korean follows a logical mathematical structure based on addition and multiplication. For numbers between 11 and 19, the word for ten `십` is placed before the single digit. For example, 11 is `십일 (10 + 1)` and 12 is `십이 (10 + 2)`. For multiples of ten, the single digit is placed before `십`. Thus, 20 is `이십 (2 * 10)` and 90 is `구십 (9 * 10)`.

Complex numbers like 52 are formed by combining these rules: `오십이 (5 * 10 + 2)`. The instructor highlights one specific pronunciation exception: while 16 is written as `십육`, it is actually pronounced as `심뉵`.

---

### Large Numbers: 100 to 9,999

As numbers increase, new base units are introduced: `백` for 100 and `천` for 1,000. The formation logic remains consistent. 101 is `백일 (100 + 1)`, while 300 is `삼백 (3 * 100)`. A four-digit number like 6,572 is constructed as `육천오백칠십이 (6 * 1,000 + 5 * 100 + 7 * 10 + 2)`. The instructor notes that it is unnecessary to say `일` (one) before these large units; for example, 110 is simply `백십`, not `일백일십`.

---

### Numbers 10,000 and Beyond

In Korean, the counting system shifts to a base of 10,000, represented by the word `만`. Unlike English, which counts in thousands (ten thousand, hundred thousand), Korean uses `만` as a primary unit.

- **10,000:** 만 (not 십천)
- **100,000:** 십만 (ten ten-thousands)
- **1,000,000:** 백만 (one hundred ten-thousands)
- **10,000,000:** 천만 (one thousand ten-thousands)
- **100,000,000:** 억 (ten thousand ten-thousands)

By combining `십`, `백`, `천`, `만`, and `억`, one can count up to the hundreds of billions.

---

### Practical Applications: Money and Measurements

The most frequent use of Sino-Korean numbers for beginners is counting the Korean currency, `원 (Won)`. To state a price, one simply says the Sino-Korean number followed by `원`. Examples provided include `천 원 (1,000 Won)` and `오만 원 (50,000 Won)`.

Additionally, Sino-Korean numbers are used with various loanword counters for measurements. The instructor lists several common units:
- **리터:** liter
- **그램:** gram
- **미터:** meter
- **킬로:** kilo
- **마일:** mile
- **도:** degree (Note: Korea uses Celsius)

---

### Conversation Practice: Shopping

The lesson concludes with a dialogue demonstrating price inquiries. A shopper asks `이거 얼마예요? (How much is this?)`. The clerk responds with prices like `이만 원이에요 (It is 20,000 won)` or `이만 삼천 원이에요 (It is 23,000 won)`. The shopper uses the expression `너무 비싸요 (It is too expensive)` to react to the prices, showing how these numbers function in a real-world retail context.
