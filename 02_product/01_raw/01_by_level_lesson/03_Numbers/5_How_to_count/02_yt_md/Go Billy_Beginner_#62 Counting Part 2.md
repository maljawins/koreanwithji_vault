## Teaching Points

### Overview
This lesson focuses on Pure Korean numbers, which are native to the Korean language rather than being derived from Chinese like Sino-Korean numbers. The instructor explains that while Sino-Korean numbers are used for dates, money, and measurements, Pure Korean numbers are primarily used for counting physical items, people, animals, and expressing one's age. The learning arc moves from basic counting (1–10) to larger numbers (up to 99), then covers the specific grammar rules for using these numbers with counters, specifically focusing on the age counter 살.

The instructor highlights a practical linguistic shift in modern Korea: while Pure Korean numbers exist up to 99, most speakers switch to Sino-Korean numbers for values higher than 49 or 60 in daily conversation. Consequently, the lesson emphasizes mastering the numbers 1 through 40 as the most essential for functional fluency. The session concludes with a demonstration of how to integrate age into a self-introduction and introduces a formal alternative for counting age.

---

### Pure Korean Numbers 1 to 10
The instructor introduces the first ten numbers, which serve as the foundation for all Pure Korean counting. These are: 하나 (one), 둘 (two), 셋 (three), 넷 (four), 다섯 (five), 여섯 (six), 일곱 (seven), 여덟 (eight), 아홉 (nine), and 열 (ten). A specific pronunciation note is provided for the number eight, 여덟, where the final consonant cluster is simplified in speech. Learners are encouraged to practice these ten numbers thoroughly before moving on to higher values.

---

### Counting from 11 to 99
Counting from 11 to 19 follows a simple additive logic: the word for ten (열) is followed by the single-digit number. For example, 11 is 열 하나 (ten + one) and 15 is 열 다섯 (ten + five). However, unlike Sino-Korean numbers where multiples of ten are formed by multiplying (e.g., "two-ten" for 20), Pure Korean has unique words for every tens place. These include 스물 (20), 서른 (30), 마흔 (40), 쉰 (50), 예순 (60), 일흔 (70), 여든 (80), and 아흔 (90). 

To form a number like 42, you combine the word for 40 with the word for 2, resulting in 마흔 둘. The instructor notes that there are no Pure Korean numbers beyond 99; for 100 and above, speakers must use Sino-Korean numbers like 백 (100). In modern usage, it is very common for Koreans to use Sino-Korean numbers for anything over 49, so while numbers like 여든 (80) are useful to recognize, they are less frequently used in speech than the lower numbers.

---

### Counting Age with 살
The most immediate application for Pure Korean numbers is stating one's age using the counter 살 (years old). The instructor clarifies that 살 is a bound noun (counter) and cannot be used alone to mean "age"; it must follow a number. To say "I am 17 years old," one says 저는 열일곱 살이에요. Other examples include 저는 스물아홉 살이에요 (I am 29 years old) and 저는 서른다섯 살이에요 (I am 35 years old). This structure allows learners to expand their self-introduction capabilities.

---

### Important Exceptions Before Counters
A critical grammatical rule is introduced regarding five specific numbers: 하나 (1), 둘 (2), 셋 (3), 넷 (4), and 스물 (20). When these numbers are placed directly before a counter or a noun, their forms change to act as modifiers. 하나 becomes 한, 둘 becomes 두, 셋 becomes 세, 넷 becomes 네, and 스물 becomes 스무. 

The instructor demonstrates this with the age counter:
- **한 살** / 1 year old (not 하나 살)
- **두 살** / 2 years old (not 둘 살)
- **세 살** / 3 years old (not 셋 살)
- **네 살** / 4 years old (not 넷 살)
- **스무 살** / 20 years old (not 스물 살)

It is emphasized that this change only occurs when the number is *directly* before the counter. For a number like 25, the 스물 remains unchanged because it is followed by 다섯, resulting in 스물다섯 살.

---

### Formal Age Counting with 세
For situations requiring extra respect, such as talking about elders or in very formal contexts, the counter 세 is used instead of 살. A major distinction is that 세 is used with Sino-Korean numbers, not Pure Korean numbers. This counter is most common when referring to people over the age of 50. For example, to say "My grandmother is 85 years old," one would say 우리 할머니께서는 팔십오 세이세요. The instructor notes that 이세요 is the honorific form of the verb 이다 (to be). While 세 is more formal, it is still acceptable to use when talking about oneself in a formal setting.

---

### Writing Numbers in Korean
A final practical note explains that while these lessons write out numbers in Hangul to help students practice reading, in real-world Korean writing, Arabic numerals are almost always used. For example, "I am 34 years old" would typically be written as "저는 34 살이에요" rather than writing out the full word 서른 네.
