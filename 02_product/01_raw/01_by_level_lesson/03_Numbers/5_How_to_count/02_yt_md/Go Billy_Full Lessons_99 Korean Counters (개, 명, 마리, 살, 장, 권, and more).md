## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the essential concept of Korean counters, explaining that simply placing a number before a noun is grammatically incorrect in Korean. To count items, people, or animals, one must use a specific "counter" word that corresponds to the category of the object being counted. The lesson focuses on identifying which number system—Pure Korean or Sino-Korean—is required for different counters and how certain numbers change their form when paired with these counters.

The learning arc begins with the most common counters for people and animals, moves into a generic counter for miscellaneous objects, and then covers specific categories like age, flat items, and books. The instructor also explains the transition between number systems for larger quantities and provides practice opportunities throughout the video to reinforce the patterns.

---

### Counting People and the Rule of Five
To count people, the counter **명** is used in conjunction with Pure Korean numbers. A critical grammatical rule is introduced: the Pure Korean numbers for 1, 2, 3, 4, and 20 change their form when followed by any counter. Specifically, **하나 (one)** becomes **한**, **둘 (two)** becomes **두**, **셋 (three)** becomes **세**, **넷 (four)** becomes **네**, and **스물 (twenty)** becomes **스무**. For example, one person is **한 명**, not **하나 명**. This change for 20 only occurs when the number is exactly 20; for 21, the number reverts to the standard form for the tens place while the ones place follows the change rule, resulting in **스물한 명**.

While Pure Korean numbers are standard for counting, the instructor notes that for quantities above 40 or 50, speakers may switch to Sino-Korean numbers for convenience. For any amount above 99, switching to Sino-Korean is mandatory. Therefore, 50 people can be expressed as **오십 명** and 100 people as **백 명**.

---

### Animals, Things, and Age
Animals of all types are counted using Pure Korean numbers and the counter **마리**. When the context is clear, such as responding to a question about how many cows are present, one can simply say the number and the counter, like **세 마리 (three [animals])**. If the noun is included, it typically comes first: **소 세 마리 (three cows)**.

For general objects, especially those that do not have a specific counter or when the specific counter is unknown, the generic counter **개** is used with Pure Korean numbers. An example provided is **의자 다섯 개 (five chairs)**. To count a person's age, the counter **살** is used with Pure Korean numbers. For instance, "I am 31 years old" is translated as **저는 서른 한 살이에요**.

---

### Measuring Units and Specific Categories
Counters used for measuring—such as distance, volume, or temperature—typically utilize the Sino-Korean number system. Examples shown on screen include **오 미터 (five meters)**, **팔 리터 (eight liters)**, and **이십 도 (twenty degrees)**. The instructor notes that Korea uses the Celsius scale for temperature.

Specific counters are used for certain shapes or types of items. The counter **장** is used for flat items like paper or even T-shirts: **종이 다섯 장 (five sheets of paper)** or **셔츠 천 장 (one thousand shirts)**. For books, the counter **권** is employed with Pure Korean numbers, such as **책 한 권 (one book)** or **책 스물 네 권 (twenty-four books)**.
