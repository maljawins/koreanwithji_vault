## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces the question word 몇 (how many) and the essential system of Korean counter words (or measure words). In English, counters are used for specific items like "three bottles of water" or "two plates of lasagna," but in Korean, almost every noun requires a specific counter word when being quantified. The lesson covers the most common counters for objects, people, and animals, as well as specialized counters for age, time, and duration. A major focus of the video is the complex interplay between Pure Korean numbers and Sino-Korean numbers, as different counters require different number systems.

---

### Basic Counter Words and Number Rules

The three most fundamental counter words are 개 (for non-living things), 명 (for people), and 마리 (for animals). When counting items, the standard sentence structure is Noun + Number + Counter. For example, "five pens" is expressed as 펜 다섯 개 (pen five [object counter]). The instructor notes that while you can include the noun, it is often omitted if the context is clear, such as saying 다섯 개 (five [objects]) or 여섯 명 (six [people]).

A critical rule is that these general counters always use Pure Korean numbers (하나, 둘, 셋...). Using Sino-Korean numbers (오 개, 육 명) is incorrect. Furthermore, the numbers 1, 2, 3, 4, and 20 change their form when followed by a counter:
- 하나 (1) becomes 한
- 둘 (2) becomes 두
- 셋 (3) becomes 세
- 넷 (4) becomes 네
- 스물 (20) becomes 스무

The instructor demonstrates this with the sentence:
> **방에 스물 두 명이 있어요.** / There are 22 people in the room.

Here, 스물 둘 (22) changes to 스물 두 because it is followed by the counter 명. Similarly, "13 candies" is 사탕 열세 개, where 열셋 (13) becomes 열세.

---

### Specific Counters for Objects

While 개 is the most general counter for inanimate objects, native speakers use more specific counters to sound natural. The instructor highlights several:
- 잔: for cups or glasses (커피 한 잔 / a cup of coffee)
- 권: for books (책 세 권 / three books)
- 대: for machines or vehicles (컴퓨터 한 대 / one computer)

The instructor mentions that while using 개 for these items is often understood and acceptable for beginners, learning the specific counters is recommended for reaching a native-like level.

---

### Asking Questions with 몇

To ask "how many," the word 몇 is placed immediately before the counter word. This can be paired with the 이다 (to be) verb or the 있다 (to exist/have) verb.

Common question forms include:
- **사과가 몇 개예요?** / How many apples are there?
- **아이들이 몇 명이에요?** / How many children are there?
- **가족이 몇 명 있어요?** / How many family members do you have?
- **애완동물 몇 마리 키워요?** / How many pets do you raise?

The instructor introduces the verb 키우다 (to raise/grow) in the final example, noting that it is used for both pets and children.

---

### Age and Time

Counting age and telling time involve specific counters and a mix of number systems. For age, the counter is 살, which uses Pure Korean numbers.
- **몇 살이에요?** / How old are you?
- **스물 일곱 살이에요.** / I am 27 years old.

For telling the time of day, the counter for hours is 시 (Pure Korean numbers) and the counter for minutes is 분 (Sino-Korean numbers). This is one of the most confusing aspects for learners.
- **지금 몇 시예요?** / What time is it now?
- **열한 시 이십 분이에요.** / It is 11:20.

The word 반 (half) can be used to replace 삼십 분 (30 minutes), such as 세 시 반 (3:30).

---

### Duration of Time

When expressing how long something takes or how long one has stayed somewhere, the counters change again:
- 시간: hours (Pure Korean numbers). 몇 시간 걸려요? (How many hours does it take?)
- 달 / 개월: months. 달 uses Pure Korean numbers (한 달), while 개월 uses Sino-Korean numbers (일 개월).
- 년: years (Sino-Korean numbers). 한국에 몇 년 있었어요? (How many years were you in Korea?)

The instructor introduces the particle 동안 (for/during) to specify the length of time, such as 열 달 동안 (for ten months) or 일 년 동안 (for one year).
