## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces essential Korean time counters, explaining how to count hours, minutes, seconds, days, weeks, months, and years. The lesson focuses on the critical distinction between when to use Pure Korean numbers versus Sino-Korean numbers, as using the wrong system is a common mistake for learners. 

The learning arc begins with the most common time units (hours and minutes) and progresses through calendar units (days, months, years) before concluding with frequency counters. Throughout the video, the instructor provides visual demonstrations on a whiteboard, highlighting irregular number transformations and specific exceptions in month names.

---

### Counting Hours: 시 vs 시간
The instructor defines a counter as a noun used specifically for counting items. For telling time and counting hours, Pure Korean numbers (하나, 둘, 셋, etc.) are used. However, when a Pure Korean number acts as an adjective before a counter, the first four numbers and the number twenty undergo a transformation: 하나 becomes 한, 둘 becomes 두, 셋 becomes 세, 넷 becomes 네, and 스물 becomes 스무.

The counter **시** is used specifically for "o'clock" to indicate a specific point in time. For example, "three o'clock" is expressed as 세 시. The instructor provides a full drill from one to twelve o'clock and demonstrates the usage in a sentence:
> **일곱시에 일어났어요.** / I woke up at seven o'clock.

In contrast, the counter **시간** is used for counting a duration of hours. While it also uses Pure Korean numbers, it refers to "how many hours" rather than "what time." For example, 두 시간 means "two hours." The instructor illustrates this with a request:
> **두 시간 뒤에 오세요.** / Please come after two hours.

---

### Minutes and Seconds: 분 and 초
Unlike hours, minutes and seconds utilize the Sino-Korean number system (일, 이, 삼, etc.). The counter for minutes is **분** and the counter for seconds is **초**. These are attached directly to the Sino-Korean number. The instructor provides an example for waiting a brief duration:
> **십초만 기다려주세요.** / Please wait just ten seconds.

---

### Years and Days: 년 and 일
Both years and days use Sino-Korean numbers. The counter **년** is used for both counting a number of years and naming a specific calendar year. For instance, 1999년 can mean "1,999 years" or "the year 1999." The instructor demonstrates counting years in a future-tense sentence:
> **오 년 뒤에 세계여행을 할 거예요.** / I will travel around the world in five years.

The counter **일** is used for counting days and naming the day of the month. For example, 삼일 can mean "three days" or "the third day of the month." An example of duration is shown:
> **이십오일 기다렸어요.** / I waited twenty-five days.

---

### Months: 월 vs 개월 and 달
Naming the months of the year requires the counter **월** combined with Sino-Korean numbers 1 through 12. The instructor highlights two critical phonetic exceptions: June is **유월** (not 육월) and October is **시월** (not 십월). An example of a month as a point in time is given:
> **5월에 눈이 왔어요.** / It snowed in May.

For counting the duration of months, there are two options. The first is **개월**, which uses Sino-Korean numbers and sounds slightly more formal. The second is **달**, which uses Pure Korean numbers (한 달, 두 달, etc.). Both have the same meaning. The instructor shows the usage of 개월:
> **한국어를 8개월 동안 공부했어요.** / I studied Korean for eight months.

---

### Weeks: 주 and 주일
Weeks are counted using Sino-Korean numbers followed by either **주** or **주일**. Both are interchangeable when counting duration. The instructor provides an example of a past action:
> **2주 전에 샀어요.** / I bought it two weeks ago.

Additionally, **주** is used specifically for relative time markers: **이번 주** (this week), **지난 주** (last week), and **다음 주** (next week). Note that 주일 is typically not used in these specific phrases.

---

### Frequency: 번
The counter **번** is used to count the number of times an action occurs and requires Pure Korean numbers (한 번, 두 번, etc.). The instructor provides a sentence example:
> **오늘 철수를 두 번 봤어요.** / I saw Chul-soo twice today.

A special orthographic note is provided regarding **한 번**. When written with a space (**한 번**), it literally means "one time." When written without a space (**한번**), it is often used with the `~보다` (to try) verb ending to mean "if you have the chance" or "give it a try." For example, **한번 먹어봐** suggests trying the food if the opportunity arises, rather than strictly limiting the action to a single bite.
