**Dates**

In this lesson, I'll be covering dates and time.

Korea uses the year-month-day format for dates.

So a date like September 17th, 2025 would be written as 2025-09-17.

By the way, if you were to write this in the month-day-year format like the U.S. does, it would be 09-17-2025.

In my opinion, the year-month-day format is the most logical because it follows the same descending order we use for time—like hours, minutes, and seconds.

**Years**

Moving on: 년 means "year," so 2025 would be 이천이십오년.

You just add 년 after the number to make it the year.

**Months**

월 means "month," and you would also put this after the number to say a certain month.

Unlike English, where every month has a special name, Korean just uses 월 after each number. For example, January with one, February with two, and so on.

월 means "month," and you would also put this behind the numbers to say a certain month. Unlike English, where every month has a special name, Korea just associates each month with a number: January with one, February with two, and so on.

So January is 일월

February is 이월

March is 삼월

April is 사월

May is 오월

June is 유월. I know six is 육, but Koreans don't say 육월 - it doesn't sound natural, so instead we say 유월.

July is 칠월

August is 팔월

September is 구월

October is 시월. Similar to June, there's a slightly different pronunciation. Ten is 십, but we say 시월, not 십월.

November is 십일월

and December is 십이월.

Let's move on to the days. 일 means "day," and you would also put this behind the number to say a certain day of the month.

As you've probably noticed, it sounds exactly the same as the Korean word for "one," which is also 일.

Because of this, even Koreans get confused sometimes.

For example, if someone said 이십일, it could be interpreted two ways:

**1.** The 20th—because 이십 means 20 and 일 means "day"

**2.** The 21st—because 이십일 means 21, and the speaker just omitted the word "day"

If you ask for clarification, they might make it super obvious by replying 그냥 이십일, with 그냥 meaning "just." So it would refer to "JUST the 20th."

Or they might say 이십일일 with that extra 일 at the end, which would refer to the 21st.

So let's put this all together. September 17th, 2025 would be 이천이십오년 구월 십칠일.

January 21st, 2024 would be 이천이십사년 일월 이십일일.

Hopefully you get the idea and it's not that confusing - they're just nominal numbers.

**Time**

The real confusion might set in when we're talking about time. 시 means "hour," so if you put this behind the number, it means "o'clock." However, the number we use for the hour has to be cardinal numbers, not nominal numbers.

For example, if I want to say one o'clock, it would be 한시, not 일시. Actually, do you remember the whole measure words exception from the last lesson?

I've mentioned that putting unit words or measure words behind a cardinal number slightly alters how you pronounce them. Here, you can assume 시 is a counter word, so it follows the same rule.

* One o'clock is 한시
* Two o'clock is 두시
* Three o'clock is 세시
* Four o'clock is 네시
* Five o'clock is 다섯시
* Six o'clock is 여섯시
* Seven o'clock is 일곱시
* Eight o'clock is 여덟시
* Nine o'clock is 아홉시
* Ten o'clock is 열시
* Eleven o'clock is 열한시
* Twelve o'clock is 열두시

If you use the 24-hour clock and had to go beyond 12 o'clock, from the 13th hour, normally you'd start using nominal numbers. So 13 o'clock would typically be 십삼시, not 열세시. 14 o'clock would be 십사시, and this pattern continues all the way to 이십사시.

By the way, the military has their own way of counting time where they just pronounce each digit in nominal numbers.

For example, one o'clock would be 공일시. But this is probably irrelevant to you, so let's just skip it.

Moving on to minutes and seconds? 분 is minutes, 초 is seconds. These use purely nominal numbers, so the 30th minute would be 삼십분, and the 50th second would be 오십초.

As a result, a time like 11:58:38 would be 열한시오십팔분삼십팔초.

It might sound weird to you that Koreans use cardinal numbers only for the hour and nominal numbers for the rest. You're right, it's a bit confusing. I suggest you think of it this way: 한시, which is one o'clock, could literally be translated to "the first hour." 두시 would be "the second hour," and so on. It's just a small exception to our rule of time.
