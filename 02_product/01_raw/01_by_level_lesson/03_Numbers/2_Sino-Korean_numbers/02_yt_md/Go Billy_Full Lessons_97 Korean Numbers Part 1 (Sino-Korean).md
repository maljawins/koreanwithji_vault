## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the fundamental concept that Korean utilizes two distinct number systems. This lesson focuses exclusively on the Sino-Korean system, which consists of words originated from the Chinese language. The other system, Pure Korean, is reserved for a future lesson. The instructor emphasizes that while having two systems might sound daunting, the logic behind constructing larger numbers in the Sino-Korean system is actually quite straightforward and follows a consistent mathematical pattern.

The learning arc begins with mastering the basic digits from one to ten. From there, the lesson demonstrates how these ten building blocks are combined to form any number up to 99, followed by the introduction of specific counters for hundreds, thousands, and ten-thousands. A significant portion of the video is dedicated to the unique way Korean groups large numbers by ten-thousands rather than thousands, and the lesson concludes with a practical application: counting Korean currency.

---

### Basic Digits One through Ten
The foundation of the Sino-Korean system is the set of numbers from one to ten. These are presented on screen in two columns:
- **일 (1)**
- **이 (2)**
- **삼 (3)**
- **사 (4)**
- **오 (5)**
- **육 (6)**
- **칠 (7)**
- **팔 (8)**
- **구 (9)**
- **십 (10)**

The instructor notes that once these ten words are memorized, they serve as the components for all larger numbers.

---

### Constructing Numbers 11 to 99
To create numbers between 11 and 99, the system uses a simple additive and multiplicative logic. For numbers 11 through 19, you simply say the word for ten followed by the single digit. For example, eleven is **십일 (10 + 1)** and twelve is **십이 (10 + 2)**. The instructor highlights a specific rule: you do not say "one-ten-one" for eleven; it is simply **십일**, not **일십일**.

For multiples of ten, such as fifty, you place the multiplier before the word for ten. Thus, fifty is **오십 (5 x 10)**. To form a complex number like fifty-nine, you combine these steps: **오십** (fifty) plus **구** (nine) results in **오십구**.

---

### Pronunciation Nuances and Cautions
The instructor points out two specific pronunciation cases to ensure accuracy. First, the number sixteen, written as **십육**, is actually pronounced as `심뉵` due to Korean sound change rules inherited from its Chinese roots. This pronunciation remains consistent even when sixteen is part of a larger number, such as **육십육 (66)**.

Second, a cultural caution is given regarding the number eighteen, **십팔**. Because its pronunciation is very similar to a common Korean swear word, the instructor advises learners to be careful and ensure they pronounce the **팔** clearly to avoid accidental offense.

---

### Moving into Hundreds, Thousands, and Beyond
As numbers grow larger, new counters are introduced for each decimal place. The counter for one hundred is **백**, for one thousand is **천**, and for ten thousand is **만**.
- **백 (100)**: 120 is **백이십**, 404 is **사백사** (skipping the empty tens place).
- **천 (1,000)**: 1,234 is **천 이백 삼십 사**.
- **만 (10,000)**: 10,400 is **만 사백**.

The instructor explains that Korean numerical logic differs from English at the 10,000 mark. While English continues to count in thousands (e.g., one hundred thousand), Korean shifts to using **만** as the base. Therefore, 100,000 is expressed as **십만 (10 x 10,000)**, one million is **백만 (100 x 10,000)**, and ten million is **천만 (1,000 x 10,000)**. For even larger figures, the counter **억** is used for 100,000,000.

---

### Practical Application: Counting Money
The primary use case for Sino-Korean numbers is counting money. The official currency of South Korea is the **원 (Won)**. To state an amount, you simply place the Sino-Korean number before the word **원**.
- **천원**: 1,000 won (approximately $1 USD)
- **만원**: 10,000 won (approximately $10 USD)

The instructor concludes by encouraging regular practice, noting that while the grouping by ten-thousands is the most confusing part for English speakers, it becomes intuitive with repetition.
