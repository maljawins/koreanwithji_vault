## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor explains the unique "Korean Age" system, a cultural method of counting age that differs significantly from the international standard. While the system originated in China, South Korea is currently the only country that continues to use it in daily life. The core of the lesson focuses on the two primary rules that define this system: being born as a one-year-old and aging collectively on New Year's Day rather than on one's individual birthday.

---

### The Two Rules of Korean Age

The instructor uses a sketchbook to visually demonstrate the math behind the system. The first rule is that a baby is considered one year old the moment they are born. This is because the time spent in the mother's womb is counted as the first year of life. On screen, the instructor draws a baby born on December 23, 1992. In the international system, this baby is 0 years old, but in the Korean system, the baby is immediately 1 year old.

The second rule is that everyone in Korea turns a year older on January 1st, regardless of their actual birth date. Using the same example, just nine days after being born (on January 1, 1993), the baby turns 2 years old in Korean age. This creates a situation where a baby born on December 31st becomes 2 years old the very next day. The instructor emphasizes that Korean age counts the number of different calendar years a person has lived in, not the number of full 365-day cycles completed.

---

### Calculating the Age Gap

Because of these rules, a person's Korean age is always one or two years older than their international (actual) age. If your birthday has already passed in the current calendar year, your Korean age is your international age plus one. If your birthday has not yet passed, your Korean age is your international age plus two. 

The instructor provides a real-world example using J-Hope from BTS. Born on February 18, 1994, his international age at the time of the video (February 15, 2020) was 25. However, because he had lived through 27 different calendar years (1994 through 2020), his Korean age was 27. The instructor notes that while most Koreans use this system socially, international age is still used for official legal documents.

---

### Cultural Context and Language Preview

The instructor shares a personal sentiment, writing in her sketchbook that she "secretly hates" the Korean age system because it makes her feel older than she is. She writes the Korean sentence **저는 한국 나이가 싫어요** (I hate Korean age) and explains that it can feel unfair to be "older" by two years. 

Finally, the video previews the linguistic aspect of age. When stating age in Korean, one must use Native Korean numbers rather than Sino-Korean numbers. The instructor writes an example sentence:
> **저는 27살 이에요.** / I am 27 years old.

She clarifies that for the number 27, one must use the Native Korean word **스물 일곱** (twenty-seven) followed by the counter **살** (years old), rather than the Sino-Korean equivalent.
