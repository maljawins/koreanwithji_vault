**Korean Age**

Finally, let’s learn about age. I promised to talk about the Korean age system earlier so I’ll get to that. But before we do, the counter word for age is 살.

I’m thirty-five years old in international age. And in Korean that would be 서른 다섯 살.

Yes, we use native Korean number to count the age.

Now that we got that out of the way, let’s talk about the Korean age.

**Two Age Systems**

In Korea, we use not one, but two different ways to keep track of our age.

"Why is that necessary?" you might ask.

We'll get to that in a moment.

**International Age (만 나이)**

This is probably the way you count your age, which is: You are 0 when you're born, and you age every year on your birthday. In Korean we call this 만 나이, which roughly translates to “Full age”, implying that you have to live a “full” year to turn the next age.

And this is the international age that I’m referring to.

**Korean Age (한국 나이)**

The second method is Korean Age. In Korean, we call this method “세는 나이” which literally translates to “Counting age” or “Age that’s for counting.”

In this system, you are 1 when you're born.

Some people say we count our age this way because you live almost a full year in your mother's belly—from day one of the pregnancy.

And you gain a year not on your birthday, but every new year—basically every January 1st. It doesn't matter which month you were born in.

So yes, in Korea, everyone turns the next age on the same day. Pretty interesting, right?

Here's the most common question I always get:

"Then if you were born on December 31st, do you turn 2 the very next day?"

And the answer is yes because the next day happens to be the new year.

Then what if you were born on Jan. 1? Then tough luck—you’d have to wait another 365 days to turn 2. In Korea, those born on Jan. 1 and those born on Dec. 31 of the same year are the same age despite the fact there’s almost a whole year difference.

**How to Calculate Korean Age**

For example, those born in 1990 have lived through 35 different years—from 1990 all the way to 2025.

Their Korean Age would be 36 because they were one year old plus 35 years. That would be me.

So, this is how you can calculate your Korean age

Current Year – Birth Year + 1.

**Saying Your Age: 년생**

If this looks too complicated, there's another way to say your age: "the year you were born" plus 년생.

년 means year and 생 means “born” So I am 천구백구십년생, or just 구십년생, or 구공년생. It's just more natural to say the year without the 1900 part.

What if you were born after the year 2000? Let’s say you were born in 2004. You could say 이천사년생 or just 공사년생. Remember, zero in Sino-Korean is 공 or 영? In this case, you'd say 공 instead of 영.

**Why Two Systems?**

Now, thanks to Korean Age, it becomes a lot easier to group people.

For example, if you were talking about 36-year-olds in 2025, you would be referring to anybody all born in the same year of 1990. This wouldn’t work for International Age because your age would depend on if your birthday has passed already or not.

**When to Use Each System**

Here are some differences between Korean and International Age:

Koreans use International Age or 만 나이 for official purposes. For instance, if you were filling out an official document and it asked you to write down your age, you would put your International Age.

Korean Age, on the other hand, is pretty unofficial and is used for more social circumstances in everyday situations, like when someone asks you how old you are.

As a result, there’s always a one- or two-year gap between Korean and International Age. Some people think Korean Age is always International Age plus one, but that’s not true. Taking me as an example, I’m 36 in Korean age this year (2025). And but my birthday has passed, so I’m 35 in international age. But if it hadn’t passed, I would be 34 internationally.

**Legal Drinking Age**

Now, here's another common question I get: What is the legal age for drinking?

So, according to the Korean law, you have to be 20 in Korean age to be able to legally purchase alcohol. So, in 2025, people who were born in 2006 or earlier can legally buy alcohol. This should also concern you because this law applies to foreigners in Korea as well. So make sure to take notes if you want to have some soju or 막걸리 while you’re in Korea.

**Cultural importance of Age**

Age is a very important subject in Korea. If someone’s older than you, you are expected to use the right speech level when talking to them to show respect. If you’re a K-drama or a K-pop fan or just a fan of Korean media in general, you’ve probably heard of titles like 오빠, 언니, 형, and 누나.

We're going to talk about how to address people in detail when we discuss Korean pronouns.

But simply put:

* A guy would say 형 to an older guy and 누나 to an older girl
* A girl would say 오빠 to an older guy and 언니 to an older girl

Now imagine if we used the international age system in Korea to keep track of everyone's age.

Say someone was born 3 months before you. They’d be older than you for those 3 months of the year, but for the remaining 9 months, you'd be the same again.

See where I'm going with this? This would cause a massive cultural confusion. And this is why we have 2 different age systems in Korea.

**빠른 년생 (Early Age)**

Now, what if you were born on January 1st, 1990 and you're talking to someone who was born on December 31st 1989?

They're literally ONE day older than you, but in Korean age, they're one YEAR older than you.

But does that mean you still have to call them 형/누나 or 오빠/언니? Well, technically, you're "supposed to." But it depends.

There’s a concept called 빠른 년생, literally meaning "early age," And it refers to people who were born between January and early March and started school a year earlier than their Korean age.

This has always been the case with me personally because I was born in January 1990. So, most of my classmates were born in 1989.

If you were in the same grade, you could just become "equals." So, all my "friends" are technically a year older than me. But I never called them 형 or 누나, and I still don't.

Obviously, this is a controversial topic in Korea, and it has created cultural confusion.

Some people think you should still respect age hierarchy—even if it's just a one-day difference. But I personally refuse to call people born in '89 형 or 누나.

**The 2009 Change**

To resolve these issues, the government changed the official cutoff date for school entry to January 1st in 2009, which effectively eliminated the concept of 빠른 년생.

While officially gone, people born in the 1980s and 1990s still refer to themselves using this term.

But if you're not Korean, this shouldn't concern you too much. Koreans are aware of the fact that you guys are not familiar with this whole age hierarchy culture. So we don’t expect you to follow the rules as strictly as Koreans do.
