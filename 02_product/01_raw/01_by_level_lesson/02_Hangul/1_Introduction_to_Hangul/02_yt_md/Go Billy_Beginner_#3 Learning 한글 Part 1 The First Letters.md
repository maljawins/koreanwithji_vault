## Teaching Points

### Overview

In this lesson, the instructor introduces several more consonants in the Korean alphabet, 한글. The focus is on correct stroke order, mouth and tongue positioning, and the specific nuances of Korean pronunciation that distinguish these letters from their nearest English equivalents. The instructor emphasizes that many Korean consonants are "softer" than English sounds and do not map perfectly to English letters like P, B, or J. The lesson concludes with a cumulative practice session combining these new consonants with the previously learned vowel 아.

---

### The Consonant ㄹ

The instructor begins with ㄹ, noting it is often one of the most difficult letters for English speakers to pronounce because it is neither a true 'L' nor a true 'R' sound. The stroke order for ㄹ involves three distinct movements: first, a horizontal line to the right that turns down; second, a horizontal line from left to right in the middle; and third, a line starting from the left of the second stroke that goes down and then turns to the right.

When combined with the vowel 아 to form the syllable 라, the sound is unique. To achieve the correct pronunciation, the instructor advises starting with the tongue in the position used to say the English letter 'D' (as in "dog"). From that 'D' position, one should attempt to say "log" very softly. This results in the characteristic Korean ㄹ sound, which is a flap or a light tap rather than the liquid 'L' or retroflex 'R' found in English.

---

### The Consonant ㅁ

The next letter is ㅁ, which is similar to the English 'M' but slightly softer. The stroke order is critical to avoid confusing it with other letters later: first, a vertical stroke down on the left; second, a stroke from the top of the first going right and then down; and third, a horizontal stroke at the bottom connecting the two. The instructor warns against drawing it as a simple one-stroke box. When paired with 아, it forms the syllable 마.

---

### The Consonant ㅂ

The consonant ㅂ represents a sound between a 'B' and a 'P'. It is not as aspirated as an English 'P' nor as voiced as a hard English 'B'. The stroke order consists of four steps: two vertical lines from top to bottom, followed by two horizontal lines connecting them from left to right (one in the middle and one at the bottom). Combined with 아, it produces the syllable 바. The instructor demonstrates that the sound should be soft and neutral, avoiding the heavy "pop" of an English 'P'.

---

### The Consonant ㅅ

The letter ㅅ is the Korean equivalent of an 'S' sound, though it is generally less "hissy" or sharp than the English 'S'. It is written in two strokes: a curved line sliding down to the left, and a second line branching off to the right. When combined with 아, it forms 사. The instructor notes that the breathiness is more subtle than in English.

---

### The Consonant ㅈ

The consonant ㅈ is described as being between a 'J' and a 'CH' sound. Its stroke order is similar to ㅅ but begins with a horizontal line across the top. The first stroke goes left to right, and then the two legs (similar to ㅅ) are drawn underneath. When combined with 아, it forms 자. The instructor emphasizes that it should not be pronounced with the heavy vibration of an English 'J' or the sharp aspiration of a 'CH'.

---

### The Consonant ㅎ

The final consonant for this lesson is ㅎ, which corresponds to the English 'H' sound. The stroke order starts with a small vertical tick at the top, followed by a horizontal line, and finally a circle (drawn counterclockwise) underneath. When combined with 아, it forms the syllable 하.

---

### Practice and Review

The lesson concludes with a review of all the syllables learned by combining the new consonants with the vowel 아. The instructor leads a repetition exercise for the following sequence:

- **라** (ㄹ + 아)
- **마** (ㅁ + 아)
- **바** (ㅂ + 아)
- **사** (ㅅ + 아)
- **자** (ㅈ + 아)
- **하** (ㅎ + 아)

The instructor encourages students to practice these sounds repeatedly to master the soft, non-aspirated nature of the Korean consonants.
