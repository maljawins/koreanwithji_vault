### Understanding Nasal Consonants
To understand why ㅁ sounds like B and ㄴ sounds like D, one must first understand nasal consonants. In linguistics, M and N are nasal sounds because their pronunciation requires air to flow through the nasal cavity. The instructor demonstrates this by having the viewer place a finger under their nose while humming an "mm" sound to feel the airflow. If you pinch your nose while trying to say "mm," the sound stops immediately because the air path is blocked.

Physiologically, this happens because of the velum, which is the back part of the roof of the mouth. When we pronounce nasal consonants, the velum is lowered, allowing air to escape through the nose. If the velum is raised, it obstructs that airflow, changing the quality of the resulting sound.

---

### Denasalization: The "Stuffy Nose" Effect
The instructors use the analogy of having a bad cold to explain denasalization. When someone has a stuffy nose, the sentence "My mommy makes me muffins on Mondays" naturally sounds like "My bubby bakes me buffins on Bundays." In Korean, native speakers often do not completely open the nasal cavity at the beginning of a word. This loss of nasal airflow is called denasalization.

In the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA), this is represented by a specific symbol (a tilde with a slash) above the letter. The less air that escapes through the nose, the more the Korean ㅁ sounds like an English B, and the more the Korean ㄴ sounds like an English D. This is particularly common in word-initial positions.

---

### Examples of Denasalization in Korean
The video provides several examples where this phenomenon is clearly audible. For the letter ㅁ, words like **모자 (hat)**, **미국 (the United States)**, and **미안해요 (I'm sorry)** often sound like they start with a B to English speakers. For the letter ㄴ, the word **네 (yes)** frequently sounds like "de," while **나무 (tree)** sounds like "damu," **노래 (song)** sounds like "dorae," and **누구 (who)** sounds like "dugu."

The instructor cites a dissertation by Dr. Young Shin Kim titled "An acoustic, aerodynamic and perceptual investigation of word-initial denasalization in Korean." The research found that native Korean speakers denasalized ㅁ roughly 76% of the time and ㄴ a staggering 94% of the time at the start of words. This means that the "B-like" and "D-like" pronunciations are actually the statistical norm, even if textbooks focus on the pure M and N sounds.

---

### Critical Rules and Misconceptions
A vital rule to remember is that denasalization only occurs at the beginning of words. In the middle of a word, Koreans always pronounce these consonants with full nasal airflow, meaning they will sound like a standard M or N without exception. Furthermore, native Korean speakers are generally unaware that they are making two different sounds. In their minds, the denasalized version and the pure nasal version are the same single entity.

The instructor also debunks a common misconception regarding tongue position. Some claim that ㄴ sounds like D because Koreans bite their tongue or place it differently than English speakers. However, the instructor demonstrates that even if you bite your tongue while saying "nice," it still sounds like an N. The only factor that makes it sound like "dice" is blocking the nasal airflow. While Koreans do tend to use the blade of the tongue against the alveolar ridge rather than just the tip (as English speakers do), this is a separate articulatory detail that does not cause the D-sound shift.

---

### Practical Advice for Learners
Learners often wonder if they must mimic this "stuffy nose" pronunciation to be understood. The instructors clarify that it is not necessary. Because native speakers perceive both versions as the same letter, they will understand you perfectly whether you use a pure M/N or a denasalized B/D at the start of a word. Choosing to use the denasalized version might make your Korean sound more "native," but it is entirely optional for clear communication.
