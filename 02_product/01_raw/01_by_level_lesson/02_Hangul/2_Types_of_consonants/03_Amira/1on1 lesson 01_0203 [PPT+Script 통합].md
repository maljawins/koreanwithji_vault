## Lesson Overview

This lesson serves as a comprehensive review and reorganization of the fundamentals of 한글, specifically designed to fill in knowledge gaps and address tricky areas of pronunciation. The primary objective is to build a solid foundation in the Korean writing system, moving beyond simple recognition to understanding the mechanics of how sounds are produced and how they interact within syllables.

Ji emphasizes a practical approach to learning: the goal is effective communication rather than immediate perfection. Using the Input-Process-Output model, the lesson focuses heavily on "Input"—ensuring the student knows the correct sounds and rules—which will eventually lead to better "Output" through practice. By understanding the logic behind vowel placeholders, consonant types, and the foundational rules of 받침, students can begin to recognize and pronounce thousands of Korean words, including the vast library of English loanwords.

The lesson is structured to move from basic vowels and the unique role of the vowel ㅡ to the complexities of compound vowels and consonant categorization (basic, aspirated, and double). It concludes with an introduction to 받침 (final consonants), explaining why they are essential for mastering Korean grammar and conjugation, and providing the first set of rules for how these sounds change in multi-syllabic words.

### Slide 43~45 — Why is 받침 Important?
- **On screen:** "ESSENTIAL FOR LEARNING GRAMMAR & CONJUGATION." Example: 어떡해 (eo tteok hae) vs 어떻게 해 (eo tteo keh hae).

You might see it written as 어떻게 해? or shortened to 어떡해? The ㅎ 받침 in "떻" plays a key role in how these sounds are formed and used.

### Slide 46 — Types of Consonants Table
- **On screen:** A table categorizing consonants. Basic (가, 나, 다...), Aspirated (카, 타, 파, 하, 차), and Double (까, 따, 빠, 싸, 짜).
- **Ji's teaching:** Before we dive deep into 받침, we must understand the three types of consonants. Basic consonants are your standard sounds. Aspirated consonants are spoken with a puff of air. Double consonants are strengthened, emphasized sounds with no air coming out.

### Slide 47~52 — Basic Consonant ㅅ (siot)
- **On screen:** ㅅ 시옷 (siot). Labeled "Smooth vs Sound." Slide 49-50 shows "Smooth" (스무스) with the 'S' and 'ㅅ' highlighted in yellow. Slide 51-52 shows "TRY READING: 사랑해 (Sa rang hae)."
- **Ji's teaching:** Most basic consonants are straightforward, but ㅅ is special. It is romanized as 'S', but it's closer to the soft, "smooth" 's' rather than a sharp, hissing 's'. Think of the word "Smooth"—in Korean, we spell it 스무스. Try reading this word: 사랑해. It's a soft start.

### Slide 53 — Romanization vs 한글
- **On screen:** "ROM vs KOR." Icons for Microsoft Edge and Safari are under "Romanization," while the Google Chrome icon is under "한글."
- **Ji's teaching:** Here is a tip: ditch romanization as soon as possible. Focus on the actual 한글 characters. Think of Romanization like Safari or Edge—they work, but 한글 is like Chrome; it's the optimized way to browse the language.

### Slide 54~55 — Aspirated Consonants
- **On screen:** ASPIRATED; WITH A PUFF OF AIR; ㅋ ㅌ ㅍ ㅎ ㅊ.
- **Ji's teaching:** Aspirated consonants (ㅋ, ㅌ, ㅍ, ㅎ, ㅊ) are all about that puff of air. Compare 가 (ga) to 카 (ka), or 다 (da) to 타 (ta). You should feel the air hit your hand if you hold it in front of your mouth.

### Slide 56~59 — The "Sh" Sound Exception for ㅅ
- **On screen:** Slide 57: "Sh sound Depending on the vowel." Slide 58-59 show tables. ㅅ paired with ㅏ, ㅓ, ㅗ, ㅜ, ㅡ stays "Smooth" (사, 서, 소, 수, 스). ㅅ paired with ㅑ, ㅕ, ㅛ, ㅠ, ㅣ becomes "Aspirated (Sh)" (샤, 셔, 쇼, 슈, 시).
- **Ji's teaching:** Even though ㅅ doesn't have a separate aspirated letter, it *does* have an aspirated sound: "Sh." This happens depending on which vowel it is paired with. When ㅅ is paired with vowels that have two strokes (ㅑ, ㅕ, ㅛ, ㅠ) or the vowel ㅣ, it changes from an "s" sound to an "sh" sound. So 사 is "sa," but 시 is "shi." Note that 싀 doesn't actually exist in the language.

### Slide 60~61 — ㅎ (The Aspirator)
- **On screen:** ㅎ 히읗 (hieut). Labeled "Aspirated ㅇ" and "Aspirates other consonants." Example: 좋다 (jo h + da) becomes 조타 (jo ta).
- **Ji's teaching:** Think of ㅎ as the "Aspirator Consonant." Its role is to aspirate other consonants when they are paired together. For example, in the word 좋다 (to be good), the ㅎ at the bottom of the first syllable combines with the ㄷ at the start of the next syllable. ㅎ + ㄷ = ㅌ. So you pronounce it as 조타, not "joh-da." ㅎ aspirates every letter except ㅅ, because ㅅ is the only sound that doesn't have its own dedicated aspirated letter.

### Slide 62~65 — Double Consonants
- **On screen:** ㄲ ㄸ ㅃ ㅆ ㅉ. Labeled "STRENGTHENED" and "WITH NO PUFF OF AIR." Slide 65 shows "Sound 싸운드 VS Smooth 스무스."
- **Ji's teaching:** Double consonants are the opposite of aspirated ones. They are strong and emphasized, but no air should come out. Let's focus on ㅆ. Remember my pun: ㅅ is for "Smooth," but ㅆ is for "Sound." ㅆ is a much more "sound" sound—it's sharper and more tense.

### Slide 66~67 — 받침 Affecting Next Syllables
- **On screen:** 좋다 = 조타; 잃다 (to lose); 읽다 (to read). Text: "the 받침 in the first syllable can affect the pronunciation of the second syllable."
- **Ji's teaching:** This is a key concept: the 받침 at the end of one syllable often changes how the *next* syllable is pronounced. We saw this with 좋다 becoming 조타. It also happens in words like 잃다 and 읽다.

### Slide 68~72 — Single Syllable 받침 Rules
- **On screen:** ㄱ ㄴ ㄷ ㄹ ㅁ ㅂ ㅇ. Slide 69 explains that many letters collapse into 7 base sounds. ㄱ, ㅋ, ㄲ all sound like ㄱ (g/k). ㄷ, ㅌ, ㅅ, ㅆ, ㅈ, ㅊ all sound like ㄷ (d). ㅂ, ㅍ all sound like ㅂ (b).
- **Ji's teaching:** When a consonant is in the 받침 position at the end of a single syllable, there are only 7 possible sounds. ㄴ, ㄹ, ㅁ, and ㅇ are self-explanatory—they sound like themselves. However, many other consonants simplify. For example, whether you see ㄱ, ㅋ, or ㄲ at the bottom, they all just make a "k" sound (like in 낙, 낰, 낚). Similarly, a whole group of letters (ㄷ, ㅌ, ㅅ, ㅆ, ㅈ, ㅊ) all simplify to a "d" sound when they are at the bottom.

### Slide 73~74 — Tension Practice
- **On screen:** TRY READING: 악 닫 압 숫 낮. Labeled with their simplified sounds: ak, dad, ab, sud, nad. Slide 74: "Key to pronouncing double consonants: Feel the tension in your mouth / throat."
- **Ji's teaching:** Let's practice reading these: 악, 닫, 압, 숫, 낮. To get these right, you need to feel the tension forming in your mouth and throat. You aren't fully releasing the sound; you are catching it. This tension is the key to mastering double consonants later.

### Slide 75~80 — Double Consonant Practice with Real Words
- **On screen:** Words with double consonant sounds: 악기 (Musical Instrument), 닫다 (To close), 압박 (Pressure), 숫소 (Male Cow), 낮잠 (Nap). Slide 80: "REMEMBER THE FEELING OF THE TENSION IN YOUR MOUTH AND THROAT."
- **Ji's teaching:** We can use that 받침 tension to help us pronounce double consonants in the middle of words. In 악기, the tension from the ㄱ in 악 makes the next syllable sound like 끼. So it's like saying "ak-(tension)-gi" which results in 아끼. The same happens for 닫다 (pronounced 다따), 압박 (아빡), 숫소 (수쏘), and 낮잠 (나짬).

### Slide 81~85 — Imaginary 받침 for Double Consonants
- **On screen:** 꼬리 (Tail), 따로 (Separately), 빠르다 (Fast), 싸다 (Cheap/Wrap), 짜다 (Salty/Squeeze). Each slide shows an "imaginary 받침" in parentheses to help with the tension (e.g., (옥) 꼬리).
- **Ji's teaching:** If a word *starts* with a double consonant, you can use an "imaginary 받침" to create that same throat tension. For 꼬리, imagine there is an 옥 sound before it to get your throat ready for the ㄲ. For 따로, imagine 앋. For 빠르다, imagine 압. This helps you produce that strong, airless sound correctly.

### Slide 86 — Introduction to 받침 Rules
- **On screen:** Three bullet points: 1. To pronounce multi-syllable words correctly, you need to understand 받침. 2. The sound changes depending on the next syllable's initial consonant. 3. We will focus on essential rules only.
- **Ji's teaching:** Now we get into the actual rules of how sounds change between syllables. There are hundreds of pages of rules in official guides, but most are pointless to memorize. We are going to focus only on the most essential ones that you will actually use.

### Slide 87 — 받침 Rule 1: Liaison with ㅇ
- **On screen:** 날아 = 나라. "When it's followed by ㅇ, 받침 sound moves over." Labeled "To fly."
- **Ji's teaching:** The first and most common rule is the liaison rule. When a syllable with a 받침 is followed by a syllable starting with the placeholder ㅇ, the 받침 sound simply moves over to take the place of the ㅇ. So, 날아 is pronounced exactly like 나라 (na-ra). Other examples include 먹어 sounding like 머거, or 받아 sounding like 바다.

### Slide 88 — Exceptions to Rule 1
- **On screen:** 맛없다 = 마덥다 (Not tasty); 멋없다 = 머덥다 (Not cool).
- **Ji's teaching:** There are always exceptions. For the words 맛없다 (not tasty) and 멋없다 (not cool/no swag), the ㅅ doesn't move over as an "s" sound. Instead, it follows the simplification rule first (ㅅ becomes ㄷ) and then moves over. So they are pronounced 마덥다 and 머덥다.

## Teaching Points

- **The Placeholder ㅡ (eu):** In Korean, every consonant must be paired with a vowel to form a syllable. When translating English words with consonant clusters (like "str" in "stress"), the vowel ㅡ is used as a "placeholder" to allow those consonants to exist in the Korean syllabic structure (스트레스).
- **Compound Vowel Groups:** Many compound vowels share identical pronunciations in modern Korean.
    - **Group 1 ("eh"):** ㅐ and ㅔ
    - **Group 2 ("yeh"):** ㅒ and ㅖ
    - **Group 3 ("weh"):** ㅚ, ㅙ, and ㅞ
- **Consonant Categorization:**
    - **Basic:** Standard sounds (ㄱ, ㄴ, ㄷ...). Note: ㅅ is a "smooth" soft 's'.
    - **Aspirated:** Produced with a strong burst of air (ㅋ, ㅌ, ㅍ, ㅊ, ㅎ).
    - **Double:** Produced with high vocal tension and no air (ㄲ, ㄸ, ㅃ, ㅆ, ㅉ).
- **The Role of ㅎ:** ㅎ acts as an "aspirator." When it appears as a 받침 and is followed by a basic consonant, it combines with that consonant to create its aspirated version (e.g., ㅎ + ㄷ = ㅌ).
- **ㅅ Pronunciation Shift:** The sound of ㅅ changes from "s" to "sh" when paired with the vowel ㅣ or any y-vowel (ㅑ, ㅕ, ㅛ, ㅠ).
- **Single Syllable 받침 Simplification:** At the end of a syllable, most consonants collapse into one of seven sounds:
    - ㄱ, ㅋ, ㄲ → **ㄱ**
    - ㄷ, ㅌ, ㅅ, ㅆ, ㅈ, ㅊ, ㅎ → **ㄷ**
    - ㅂ, ㅍ → **ㅂ**
    - ㄴ, ㄹ, ㅁ, ㅇ stay as they are.
- **받침 Rule 1 (Liaison):** When a syllable ending in a consonant is followed by a syllable starting with the vowel placeholder ㅇ, the final consonant sound "slides" into the next syllable (e.g., 날아 → 나라).
