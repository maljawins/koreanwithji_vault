### The Nature of Korean Vowels

The instructor identifies three primary traits of Korean vowel sounds: they are definite, unflexible (clear-cut), and soft/lazy. Unlike English, where vowel sounds can be subtle or omitted, every Korean syllable must have a clearly defined vowel. This requirement often leads to English words gaining extra syllables when transliterated into Korean. For example, the one-syllable name "Jeff" becomes the two-syllable **제프**, and the one-syllable word "great" expands to the four-syllable **그레이트**. The instructor demonstrates this by writing the words and circling the added **ㅡ (eu)** vowel sounds that "bring the vague sounds to life."

Korean vowels are "unflexible" because they do not flow into the next syllable like English vowels do. The instructor compares the pronunciation of **오이 (cucumber)**. In an English style, the sounds might glide together as "oy," but in Korean, **오** and **이** are distinct, clear-cut sounds. However, this clarity should not result in a harsh or overly enunciated delivery. Instead, the sounds should be connected "softly and lazily," without excessive mouth pressure. This "lazy" quality allows for a natural flow while maintaining the integrity of each vowel.

### Pronunciation Tips for Specific Vowels

For vowels involving a "y" sound (like **야, 여, 요, 유**), the instructor notes that English speakers often add a slight "e" sound at the start. In Korean, one should remove that initial glide and go directly to the vowel for a more clear-cut sound. Similarly, for "w" sounds (like **와, 워, 위**), learners should avoid the English tendency to start with a "u" shape and instead produce the sound directly.

The vowel **ㅡ (eu)** often poses a challenge because it lacks an English equivalent. The instructor demonstrates the mouth shape: bring the teeth close together (without clenching) and widen the lips horizontally. For the complex vowel **ㅢ (ui)**, the instructor explains it is a combination of **ㅡ** and **ㅣ**. While it can be enunciated fully, in many common words or names like **희**, it is often simplified to a plain **ㅣ (i)** sound in natural speech.

### Distinguishing Consonants: The ㄱ, ㅋ, ㄲ Group

The instructor explains that Korean consonants often come in groups of three, distinguished by aspiration (air flow) and tension. Using the **ㄱ (g/k)** group as the first example, she compares them to English sounds. English "G" (as in "go") is unaspirated, while "K" (as in "kite") is aspirated. The Korean **ㄱ** sits in the middle; it produces some air but less than an English "K." Interestingly, **ㄱ** is more aspirated when it starts a word (like **가방**) but becomes naturally unaspirated when it follows another syllable (like the second **ㄱ** in **구경**).

The aspirated **ㅋ** is very similar to the English "K," producing a significant burst of air. The double consonant **ㄲ** is a "tensed" sound. To produce it, one must tense the throat and use a higher pitch with no air escaping the mouth. The instructor demonstrates the contrast between **가 (plain)**, **카 (aspirated)**, and **까 (tensed)**, noting that the tensed version sounds louder and more emphasized.

---

### The ㄴ Sound and the ㄷ, ㅌ, ㄸ Group

The instructor addresses why the Korean **ㄴ (n)** can sometimes sound like a **ㄷ (d)** to English ears. In English, the tongue touches further back on the roof of the mouth for "N." In Korean, the tongue touches much closer to the back of the front teeth, creating a sound that borders on a "D."

For the **ㄷ (d/t)** group, the logic follows the previous set. The plain **ㄷ** is slightly aspirated (between English "D" and "T"). The aspirated **ㅌ** matches the English "T" (as in "tail"). The tensed **ㄸ** requires throat tension and a high pitch. For Spanish speakers, the instructor notes that **ㄲ** and **ㄸ** are very similar to the Spanish "G" and "D" sounds.

---

### The ㅂ, ㅍ, ㅃ and ㅈ, ㅊ, ㅉ Groups

The **ㅂ (b/p)** group introduces a new element: vibration. English "B" (as in "bread") produces deep vibration in the throat. Korean **ㅂ** has much less vibration; the lips "pop" open with slight aspiration. The aspirated **ㅍ** is like the English "P" (as in "part"). The tensed **ㅃ** is a high-powered, tensed version where the lips pop open with force but no air. The instructor likens the **ㅃ** sound to the "B" in the Chinese word "baba" or the "B" in the English phrase "I've **b**een to Korea."

For the **ㅈ (j)** group, the plain **ㅈ** is more aspirated than the English "J" (as in "jet"). The aspirated **ㅊ** is similar to the English "CH" (as in "cheese"). The tensed **ㅉ** is the high-pitched, tensed version found in words like **진짜 (really)**. The instructor emphasizes that double consonants (**ㄲ, ㄸ, ㅃ, ㅉ, ㅆ**) are generally louder and more high-toned than their plain counterparts.

---

### The ㄹ and ㅅ, ㅆ Sounds

The **ㄹ** sound is described as being between the English "L" and "R." The instructor specifies that it is closer to "L" because the tongue does not curve at all. To pronounce it correctly, place the tongue slightly further back than you would for an English "L," but do not let it roll or touch the roof of the mouth like an "R."

Finally, the instructor distinguishes **ㅅ** and **ㅆ**. The plain **ㅅ** is a soft hissing sound similar to the "S" in "slight." The double **ㅆ** is a much stronger, more tensed hissing sound, similar to the "S" in "sail." She demonstrates the difference using the word **사다 (to buy)** versus **싸다 (to be cheap)**.
