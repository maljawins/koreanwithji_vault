### Overview
This lesson focuses on the mechanics of Korean syllables beyond basic letter recognition. The instructor takes a break from introducing new characters to explain how syllables interact during speech and how more complex syllable blocks are constructed. The primary goal is to help learners transition from reading individual blocks in isolation to pronouncing words and sentences with natural flow.

The learning arc covers three main areas: the "blending" or "linking" rule where final consonants move into empty initial positions, the structural layout of syllables containing four letters (compound final consonants), and the specific pronunciation rules for compound final consonants involving the letter ㄹ. By understanding these phonetic shifts, students can achieve a more native-like speed and rhythm in their spoken Korean.

### Blending Syllables (Linking Rule)
The instructor introduces a fundamental pronunciation rule: when a syllable ending in a consonant is followed by a syllable starting with the "empty" consonant ㅇ, the final consonant of the first syllable "flows through" or "blends" into the beginning of the next. This is because the ㅇ at the start of a syllable has no sound value of its own, leaving a vacuum that the preceding consonant naturally fills during speech.

A visual example is shown with the word **한국인 (Korean person)**. While written as **한-국-인**, the final ㄱ of the second syllable moves to the empty spot of the third syllable when spoken at a normal speed. The instructor represents this phonetically on screen as **'한구긴'**. He emphasizes that this rule only affects pronunciation, not spelling; the word must always be written as **한국인**. This blending allows for smoother, faster, and more natural speech.

The instructor provides a list of practice words to demonstrate this linking effect:
- **힘들어 (It's hard)** sounds like **힘드러**
- **미국은 (As for America)** sounds like **미구근**
- **닫아 (Close it)** sounds like **다다**
- **한국어 (Korean language)** sounds like **한구거**
- **사람이 (A person [subject])** sounds like **사라미**
- **영어 (English)** sounds like **영어** (Note: the NG sound of the final ㅇ flows through)
- **발음 (Pronunciation)** sounds like **바름**
- **달이 (The moon [subject])** sounds like **다리**
- **많이 (Many/A lot)** sounds like **마니** (Note: the ㅎ is often silent or blends)

### Constructing Syllables with Four Letters
Korean syllables can contain up to four letters: one initial consonant, one vowel, and two final consonants (known as a compound 받침). The instructor explains that the layout of these blocks depends entirely on whether the vowel is vertical or horizontal.

For vertical vowels like **아, 야, 어, 여**, the initial consonant is placed on the left, the vowel on the right, and the two final consonants are placed side-by-side at the very bottom. This creates a square-like arrangement. An example shown is **앉 (from 앉다, to sit)**, where **ㄴ** and **ㅈ** sit together at the base.

For horizontal vowels like **오, 요, 우, 유, 으**, the initial consonant sits on top, the vowel is in the middle, and the two final consonants are placed side-by-side at the bottom. The instructor demonstrates this with the block **긁 (from 긁다, to scratch)**, showing the **ㄹ** and **ㄱ** next to each other under the vowel **으**.

### Pronouncing Compound Final Consonants
When a syllable with two final consonants is followed by a syllable starting with a consonant (not the empty ㅇ), usually only one of the two consonants is pronounced. The instructor focuses on a common pattern involving the letter **ㄹ (L/R)**. In many compound combinations like **ㄹㅁ, ㄹㄱ, ㄹㅂ, ㄹㅍ**, the **ㄹ** is silenced if it is not followed by an empty ㅇ.

The instructor provides specific examples of this "disappearing ㄹ" rule:
- **삶 (Life)** is pronounced as **삼**
- **흙 (Soil/Dirt)** is pronounced as **흑**
- **읽 (Read)** is pronounced as **익**
- **밟 (Step on)** is pronounced as **밥**
- **옮겨 (Move)** is pronounced as **옴겨**

However, the instructor notes a very common exception to this rule: the word **여덟 (Eight)**. In this specific case, the **ㄹ** is actually pronounced, resulting in **여덜**. He concludes by reminding students that if these words were followed by an empty ㅇ (like **삶이**), both consonants would be heard because the second one would link forward (sounding like **살미**).
