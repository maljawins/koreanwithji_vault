## Lesson Overview
This lesson marks a significant transition in the course, moving from the foundational study of 한글 characters to the practical application of building a Korean language core. The primary objective is to finalize the study of 한글 by mastering compound 받침 and then introducing a strategic framework for sentence construction. Ji emphasizes that from this point forward, romanization will no longer be used, encouraging a full immersion into reading and writing in 한글.

The lesson is divided into three main parts. First, students learn the rules for the 11 compound 받침, identifying which letter is dominant in pronunciation and how they change when followed by vowels or other consonants. Second, Ji introduces the "Right Approach" to learning Korean, which focuses on holistic sentence understanding rather than word-for-word translation from English. Finally, the lesson introduces the STLOHV framework (Subject, Time, Location, Object, How, Verb), which serves as the structural roadmap for all future Korean sentence building.

A major theme of this session is understanding the unique nature of Korean as a highly contextual and expressive language. Ji explains that Korean verbs are the most complex but also the most expressive part of the language, carrying information about tense, context, sentence type, and social hierarchy. By the end of the lesson, students are equipped with a 7-step study method to deconstruct and reconstruct Korean sentences effectively.

### Slide 17~18 — Consonant Assimilation Rules
- **On screen:** Rules for compound 받침 assimilation. 
    - Rule 1: When followed by ㅇ (silent), the second letter moves over.
    - Rule 2: Same consonant assimilation rules apply with the dominant letter.
    - Rule 3: When followed by non-silent consonants, every compound 받침 is either an amplifier or an aspirator (if it contains ㅎ).
- **Ji's teaching:** When these 받침 are followed by other consonants, the same consonant assimilation rules apply. The only difference is that, no matter which letter is dominant, all compound 받침 act as either amplifiers or aspirators.

### Slide 19~28 — Followed by ㅇ (Silent Consonant)
- **On screen:** Slides showing the second letter moving to the next syllable.

    - Slide 21: 않아 → 아나 (ㅎ is crossed out with a red 'X' because it is silent when followed by ㅇ).
    - Slide 22: 넓어 → 널버 (ㅂ moves).

    - Slide 24: 잃어 → 이러 (ㅎ is crossed out; ㄹ moves).

    - Slide 27: 삶은 → 살믄 (ㅁ moves).
    - Slide 28: 읊어 → 을퍼 (ㅍ moves).
- **Ji's teaching:** Let’s first look at the easiest rule. If the next syllable starts with ㅇ, the second letter in the compound 받침 moves to the next syllable. So 넋이 becomes 넉시 and 앉아 becomes 안자. Remember, ㅎ goes silent when followed by ㅇ, so 않아 is pronounced 아나 and 잃어 is pronounced 이러. For the others, 넓어 becomes 널버, 핥아 becomes 할타, 값이 becomes 갑시, 읽어 becomes 일거, 삶은 becomes 살믄, and 읊어 becomes 을퍼.

- **Ji's teaching:** When followed by other consonants, just apply the usual rules to the dominant letter. However, compound 받침 always act as amplifiers or aspirators. In 넋만, the dominant letter is ㄱ, which meets a nasal consonant and becomes an ㅇ sound: 넝만. In 앉다, the dominant letter is ㄴ, but the compound 받침 ㄵ amplifies the 다 sound to 따, so it's 앉따. In 값만, the ㅂ followed by ㅁ naturally turns into ㅁ: 감만. For 읽는, the dominant ㄱ followed by ㄴ becomes 잉는. Finally, for the second-letter dominant ones: 읽다 becomes 익따, 삶다 becomes 삼따, and 읊다 becomes 읍따. On a side note, 읊다 is the only word that uses the ㄿ 받침.

### Slide 46~48 — How to Learn Korean: Wrong Approach
- **On screen:** Slide 47 shows the "Wrong Approach": Input (English) → Process (Translating) → Output (Awkward Korean). Slide 48 explains why: different word order, words that don't exist in the other language, and high context.
- **Ji's teaching:** Now you’ve learned everything about 한글 and pronunciation. It’s time to move beyond the alphabet and start learning the language. Most beginner courses start with phrases like "hello" and "goodbye," and we will learn them, but we’re doing things differently. Simply memorizing phrases won’t help you in the long run; it’s no different than using a translator. One of the most common mistakes is thinking in English and then trying to translate into Korean. This is problematic because Korean is completely different—not just in word order but in how the language works. If you develop a translating habit, you'll never speak naturally because you'll be solving math equations in your head. Also, word-for-word translations often make no sense because many words don't exist in both languages. Finally, Korean is highly contextual.

### Slide 50 — The Right Approach
- **On screen:** Input (Korean Full Sentence) → Process (Deciphering/Logic) → Output (Form own sentences/Read aloud).
- **Ji's teaching:** The key difference here is that your input is a Korean sentence right from the start. You do need translation, but it’s merely part of the process. The focus is on understanding the full sentence logically. I cannot emphasize enough how important it is to use full sentences as input. In Korean, we say, "See the forest, not just the trees." The most important step is output. If you don’t use what you learn, you’ll forget it. I live in Korea and don't have anyone to speak English with. I can understand movies, but when I speak, I sometimes freeze up. That’s why you have to use it by making your own sentences and reading them out loud.

### Slide 51~53 — How Korean Works: STLOHV & Particles
- **On screen:** Slide 52 shows the acronym STLOHV: Sub, Time, Location, Obj, How, Verb. Example sentence: 나는 아침에 집에서 한국어를 열심히 공부해요. (I morning home Korean hard study). Slide 53 highlights the particles: 는, 에, 에서, 를.
- **Ji's teaching:** Korean sentences follow a framework I call STLOHV. Every sentence has at least one of these elements. Let's look at this example: 나는 아침에 집에서 한국어를 열심히 공부해요. 나 means I, 아침 means morning, 집 means home, 한국어 means Korean, 열심히 means hard, and 공부해요 means study. Each section separated by spaces aligns with S, T, L, O, H, and V. Grammar happens at the end of each section. These words attached to the nouns (는, 에, 에서, 를) are called particles. They connect nouns and indicate their roles. Some act like prepositions (in, at, on), but others like 는 and 를 have no direct English equivalent.

### Slide 54~57 — How to Study Sentences
- **On screen:** A step-by-step guide. 1. Separate by spaces. 2. Look up words. 3. Identify STLOHV. 4. Connect dots. 5. Understand logic. 6. Write your own. 7. Read aloud. 8. Repeat.
- **Ji's teaching:** Let’s say you looked up each word on your own. You have enough clues to guess the meaning. Next, you figure out which word fits into which element of STLOHV. Think of each section like a puzzle piece. Then you connect the dots. This helps you recognize patterns. The output part—writing your own sentence and reading it aloud—is the most important. This is the formula you’ll use from now on instead of running to a translator.

### Slide 58~62 — Word Order and Nuance
- **On screen:** Slide 58 shows four variations of the same sentence with different word orders, all marked "ALL correct." Slide 61 shows how particles (집은, 집을, 집에서) distinguish the role of the word "house."
- **Ji's teaching:** Word order in Korean is flexible. All these variations on the screen are grammatically correct. Why do they change? It happens in English too, like "I did it already" vs "I already did it." In Korean, we change word order to express nuance and emphasis. I wish I could explain this more clearly, but it's something you just have to learn from "feels and vibes" through listening. How do you distinguish the role of a word like 집? Particles! 에서 indicates location, 을 indicates an object. The only hard rule is that verbs typically come at the end. What comes right before the verb is usually the most important information.

### Slide 63~64 — Verbs: Adjectives are Verbs
- **On screen:** 공부하다 (To study - V.) vs 조용하다 (Quiet - Adj). Note: Adjectives at the end of a sentence are called "descriptive verbs" and follow the same conjugation rules.
- **Ji's teaching:** In Korean, adjectives at the end of a sentence, like "the room is quiet," are treated exactly like verbs. 공부하다 literally means "to do study" and 조용하다 means "to be quiet." We treat the act of being quiet as a verb. We call these "descriptive verbs."

### Slide 65~66 — Verb Stem and Ending
- **On screen:** 먹다 (To eat). 먹 is the Stem (highlighted yellow), 다 is the ending. Slide 66 shows 먹었어요 (Ate). 먹 is the stem, 었어요 is the ending.
- **Ji's teaching:** Verbs are divided into two parts: Stem and Ending. Conjugation happens in the ending part. The dictionary form always ends in ~다. If you remove 다, you have the stem. The stem stays the same, and we attach different endings to change the meaning.

### Slide 67~68 — The 5 Types of Endings (Plant Analogy)
- **On screen:** A diagram of a plant. Stem = 먹. Branch = Tense (었). Leaf = Context (어) or Sentence Type. Fruit = Respect/Speech Level (요). Slide 68 lists the 5 types: Tense, Context marker, Sentence Type, Speech Level, Honorifics.
- **Ji's teaching:** The ending is where the magic happens. This is the most difficult part of Korean because our language is so expressive. Nuance, tone, and subtle expressions all depend on the ending. I use this plant analogy: the stem is the trunk, the tense is a branch, the context or sentence type is a leaf, and the respect level is the fruit. You don't need to learn them all now; I'm just showing you how the language works.

### Slide 69~71 — Tense and Context Markers
- **On screen:** Examples with 먹다. 
    - Tense: 먹어 (Present), 먹었어 (Past - ㅆ branch), 먹을게 (Future - ㄹ branch).
    - Context: 먹거든 (I eat, you know), 먹잖아 (You know I ate), 먹을래 (I'm eating it - affirmative), 먹지 (You see, I eat), 먹었구나 (Oh, you ate - realization).
- **Ji's teaching:** Tenses come right after the stem. The branch with ㅆ indicates past, and ㄹ indicates future. Context markers, which I call "leaves," mark the context. For example, 먹었구나 implies you just realized someone else ate. You can express so much emotion with fewer words than in English. This is what I love about my language.

### Slide 72~74 — Sentence Types
- **On screen:** 1. Declarative (먹어 - statement). 2. Inquisitive (먹어? 먹니? 먹냐? - questions). 3. Propositive (먹자 - suggestion). 4. Imperative (먹어라 - command).
- **Ji's teaching:** We have four sentence types. For questions, 먹니 has a kinder tone, while 먹냐 is playful or sometimes rude. For suggestions, we use 먹자 (Let's eat). For commands, adding 라 to 먹어 makes it sound stronger.

### Slide 75~76 — Speech Type (Respect Level)
- **On screen:** Casual (반말) vs Polite (존댓말). Polite is split into Formal and Informal. Slide 76 shows "I eat" in three types: 먹어 (Casual), 먹습니다 (Polite Formal), 먹어요 (Polite Informal - most common).
- **Ji's teaching:** Korean culture is hierarchical, which is reflected in the language. Speech is divided into 존댓말 (polite) and 반말 (casual). 존댓말 has formal types used in news or business, and informal types used in stores or with strangers. This is the "fruit" of the sentence. 먹어요 is the most common speech type you will use.

### Slide 77~81 — Honorifics
- **On screen:** Rules for honorifics: used for older people or higher status. Add ~시 or ~셔 after the stem. Slide 78-80 show word changes: 먹다 → 드시다, 나 → 저 (Humble I), 우리 → 저희 (Humble We). Slide 81 shows 읽다 (To read) variations: 읽으셔, 읽으셔요, 읽으십니다.
- **Ji's teaching:** We use honorifics to humble ourselves while showing respect to others. Sometimes we use entirely different words, like 드시다 instead of 먹다. We also have humble versions of pronouns like 저 instead of 나. If there isn't a separate word, we add 시 or 셔 to the stem.

### Slide 82 — Good News
- **On screen:** Unlike English, Korean verbs do not change based on the subject (I dance / He dances).
- **Ji's teaching:** Here is some good news! In English, you have to change the verb for "he dances" vs "I dance." In Korean, the verb stays the same regardless of the subject.

### Slide 84~86 — Hello & Bye
- **On screen:** Analysis of 안녕하세요. Derived from descriptive verb 안녕하다 (To be well). Slide 86 shows the breakdown: 안녕하 (Stem) + 세요 (Inquisitive ending in informal polite speech).
- **Ji's teaching:** Let's look at our greetings. 안녕하세요 comes from the descriptive verb 안녕하다. If you remove 다, the stem is 안녕하. We add the honorific polite ending to get 안녕하세요. ## Teaching Points
- **Compound 받침 Rules:** There are 11 compound 받침, but ㄽ is obsolete. For most, the first letter is dominant (ㄳ ㄵ ㄶ ㄼ ㄾ ㅀ ㅄ). For three, the second letter is dominant (ㄺ ㄻ ㄿ). When followed by ㅇ, the second letter moves to the next syllable (except ㅎ, which becomes silent). When followed by consonants, they act as amplifiers or aspirators.
- **The STLOHV Framework:** Korean sentences are structured by Subject, Time, Location, Object, How, and Verb. Grammar particles are attached to the end of each noun section to define its role.
- **Verb Structure:** Korean verbs consist of a Stem (trunk) and an Ending. The ending is subdivided into Tense (branch), Context/Sentence Type (leaf), and Speech Level (fruit). Adjectives are treated as "descriptive verbs" and follow the same conjugation rules.
- **Language Philosophy:** Korean is a high-context language where subjects and objects are often omitted. Nuance is expressed through word order and specific verb endings rather than just word choice.
