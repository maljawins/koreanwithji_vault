**What Is Consonant Assimilation?**

Consonant assimilation refers to the sound change where a 받침 sound is affected by the next consonant, resulting in a change in pronunciation.

This happens in English too.

**Here’s some more examples.**

**Kindness → Kain nis**

**I don’t know → I don no**

**Did you → D’ Jew**

It’s pretty much **the same concept.**

Now, the idea that the pronunciation changes might sound intimidating at first but you should know that it’s there to help you pronounce things easier and more naturally.

You’ll see what I mean by this as we go.

**1. When followed by ‘ㅇ’**

Let’s start with the **easiest rule**: When the next syllable starts with **ㅇ**, the silent letter, the 받침 sound *moves* over to the beginning of the next syllable.

Here are some examples.

받아 날아 음악 잡아 앗아가다

Of course, there are a few exceptions, but there are only a handful of words like this. You'll pick them up naturally over time.

So, these are just for your reference:

맛없다, 멋없다, 겉옷

**2. Types of 받침 sounds.**

Reminder. In the previous lesson, you learned that there can only be 7 sounds in the 받침 position which are ㄱ ㄷ ㅂ ㄴ ㄹ ㅁ ㅇ.

And now we have to add ㅎ on the list because this guy needs a bit of extra attention, which we’ll talk about at the end.

Consonant assimilation is basically about how these 8 sounds interact with each other to make pronunciation EASIER on your mouth.

To understand this better, we first need to divide these 8 letters into three categories:

**1. Amplifiers – ㄱ ㄷ ㅂ**
These create tension in your mouth. We covered this when we were learning double consonants. So, when they meet certain sounds, they amplify the next consonant.

**2. Nasal 받침 – ㄴ ㄹ ㅁ ㅇ**
These all form a nasal sound. And what these guys do is that when these guys are met with certain consonants, the sound often changes to flow smoother and more natural.

**3. ‘ㅎ’ - a Wild card**

And finally we have ‘ㅎ’. Remember how we focused on ㅅ last time?

Yeah, in this lesson, we're going to pay special attention to ㅎ in the end

So, these are the **three ways** sounds change:

**1. When the next syllable becomes a double consonant**

* This happens with these 3 amplifier 받침 (ㄱ ㄷ ㅂ) forming that **tension** in the mouth remember?

**2. When pronunciation naturally shifts to an easier sound**
This isn’t really a "rule" per se and it’s just something that happens naturally and in most cases, nasal consonants (ㄴ, ㄹ, ㅁ, ㅇ) trigger this change.

**3. When ‘ㅎ’ causes a sound change.**Like I said, we’ll take a closer look at ㅎ in detail at the end.

**Type 1: Double Consonant Formation**

Alright, let's first look at amplifiers.

Amplifier consonant 받침 + ㄱㄷㅂㅅㅈ = ㄲㄸㅃㅆㅉ

When an **Amplifier consonant** (ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ) is followed by one of these five consonants—**ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ, ㅅ, ㅈ**—the sound turns into a **double consonant (ㄲ, ㄸ, ㅃ, ㅆ, ㅉ)**.

Notice they all have their **double consonant counterpart.**

So when they come after ㄱ, ㄷ, or ㅂ 받침, the tension built up in the mouth naturally amplifies them into stronger sounds.
악기 > 아끼
각도 > 각또
악보 > 악뽀
맞고 > 맏꼬
낮다 > 낟따
낮밤 > 낟빰
앞길 > 압낄
입구 > 입꾸

**2. When pronunciation naturally shifts to an easier sound**

Now, let's look at the second case—when sounds shift naturally to make pronunciation smoother.

When an **Amplifier consonant** (ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ) meets a **Nasal consonant** (ㄴ, ㄹ, ㅁ, ㅇ), the tension created by the Amplifier consonant weakens due to the nasal sound. This makes the pronunciation **softer and more fluid**.

**When ㄱ Meets ㄴ, ㄹ, or ㅁ → It Becomes ㅇ (ng sound)**

If you try to enunciate the final ㄱ sound before pronouncing ㄴ, ㄹ, or ㅁ, it feels unnatural and difficult to say smoothly.

윽느 윽르 윽므 소리 보여주기

So, the sound naturally shifts to **ㅇ (ng)** for easier pronunciation.

**Examples:**

* 학년 → 항년
* 대학로 → 대항로
* 악마 → 앙마

**When ㄷ Meets ㄴ or ㅁ → It Becomes ㄴ**

This is also a **natural shift in tongue position**. The tension created by the ㄷ 받침 gets absorbed by the nasal sounds making pronunciation easier. So, **ㄷ naturally changes to ㄴ**.

같네 빛나 맞니

Again, enunciating each syllable separately is a lot harder.

**When ㅂ Meets ㄴ or ㅁ → It Becomes ㅁ**

Again, the nasal sound makes it harder to enunciate the ㅂ clearly.

Ex: 감사합니다 고맙습니다 앞만

Now, let’s look at how nasal **받침 naturally changes** in pronunciation.

**ㄴ 받침 + ㄱ → Makes an ㅇ (ng) sound**

This follows the same logic we discussed earlier.

The ㄴ sound and ㄱ sound together make an ㅇ sound—like "mango."

So it goes like 앙기다 instead of 안 기 다

And here, there’s actually 2 sets of assimilations happening
So, it goes like 항궁말 / not 한 국 말

Try read this 한기

And there are exceptions to this rule. And again, for reference only.

인기 (인끼) – popularity

윤기 (윤끼) – Shine (n.)

**ㄴ 받침 + ㄹ → ㄴ becomes ㄹ**

When ㄴ meets ㄹ, the ㄴ naturally transforms into ㄹ for smoother pronunciation.

If you try to fully enunciate each syllable separately—like 한 / 류—it feels unnatural and harder on your tongue.

So we say 할류 and it means Korean wave

Same with this word too. 연루 instead of 연 / 루

**ㄴ 받침 + ㅁ → ㄴ becomes ㅁ**

When ㄴ 받침 meets ㅁ, the ㄴ sound naturally turns into ㅁ.

You can enunciate if you want to, but most people don't. And honestly, you can't really tell the difference.

눈물 → 눔물 (tears)

선물 → 선물 (Gift)

When you say it quickly, you can barely hear the difference.

**ㄹ 받침 + ㄴ → ㄴ becomes ㄹ**

When ㄹ meets ㄴ, the same thing happens in reverse—the ㄴ sound becomes ㄹ.

Again, much harder to enunciate separately.

**Examples:**

* 실내 → 실래
* 별난 → 별란
* 설날 → 설랄

**ㅁ 받침 + ㄹ → ㄹ becomes ㄴ**

When ㅁ 받침 meets ㄹ, the ㄹ naturally becomes ㄴ.

Again, you can enunciate if you want to—no problem at all. I personally enunciate the ㄹ sound, but some Koreans will say 금니 instead of 금리.
- 금리 > 금니 / 심리 > 심니 / 음료 > 음뇨

**ㅇ 받침 + ㄹ → ㄹ becomes ㄴ**

When ㅇ meets ㄹ, the same thing happens.

You can enunciate if you want to, but when you're talking naturally, there's not much difference.

승리

정리

**Type 3: ㅎ Sound Changes**

Okay, now it's time to look at how ㅎ changes things.

There are three main ways:

**Way 1: ㅎ Goes Silent**

First, ㅎ goes silent when it's followed by ㅇ:

나아 > 나아 / 닿아 > 다아 / 좋아 > 조아

Also, when it’s followed by ㄴ ㄹ ㅁ ㅇ, Nasal 받침, ㅎ goes silent.

안 해 > 아내 암호 > 아모 실험 > 시럼 방해 > 방애

Some people do pronounce ㅎ, but you can barely hear the difference.

**Way 2: ㅎ Aspirates Other Letters**

Let me remind you of these 4 consonants:

* ㄱ becomes ㅋ
* ㄷ becomes ㅌ
* ㅂ becomes ㅍ
* ㅈ becomes ㅊ

One thing to note: ㅅ doesn't follow this rule because it doesn't have its own aspirated counterpart.

놓고 (노코) 좋다 (조타) 낳지 (나치)

**Amplifier 받침 (ㄱ/ㄷ/ㅂ) + ㅎ**

And the same thing happens when it's the other way around:

So it’s 박하 (바카) 맏형 (마텽) 입학 (이팍) 젖혀 (저쳐)

**Way 3: ㅎ Becomes ㄷ**

ㅎ becomes ㄷ only when it's followed by ㄴ:

닿는 > 닫는 > 단는

쌓네 > 싿네 > 싼네

좋네 > 졷네 > 존네

And remember how ㄷ, when it meets ㄴ or ㅁ, becomes ㄴ?

That's what's happening in the second step here.

**Bonus: Palatalization (구개음화)**

Before we move on to compound 받침, there's one final quirky rule. We call this palatalization, or 구개음화 in Korean.

You can think of it like this:

In English, D is often pronounced like J—like in "Did you" (D'jew).

And there are times when T is pronounced like CH, especially in British English—like "Tuesday" (Chewsday).

Here’s how it works.

**1. ㄷ + 이/여 → ㄷ becomes ㅈ**

If a syllable ends with ㄷ and the next one starts with 이 or 여, ㄷ becomes ㅈ.

굳이 > 구디 X 굳이 > 구지 O

**2. ㅌ + 이/여 → ㅌ becomes ㅊ**

If it's ㅌ (the aspirated version of ㄷ) followed by 이 or 여, it becomes ㅊ.

같이 > 가티 X 같이 > 가치 O
붙여 > 부텨 X 붙여 > 부쳐 O

**3. ㄷ + 히/혀 → becomes ㅊ (with ㅎ aspiration)**

When ㄷ meets 히 or 혀, where ㅎ acts as an aspirator, it becomes ㅊ.

묻히다 > 뭊히다 > 무치다

**Wrap-Up**

The truth is, Korean consonant assimilation rules are extensive.

The National Institute of Korean Language—an official government institution—has a book that's nearly 400 pages long just on these rules.

But learning every single rule is pointless and a waste of time. So we've covered only the most common ones.

Think of it like English spelling and pronunciation.

Words like "comb," "tomb," and "bomb" all sound different, right?

I recommend learning pronunciation as you learn actual words.

And here's some good news: Unlike English, Korean pronunciation is relatively consistent. So don't worry too much.

So that's it. We've covered the basic concepts of consonant assimilation.
