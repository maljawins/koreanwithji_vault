## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the concept of 두음법칙, which literally translates to the "rule of initial sounds" (두 meaning head or initial, 음 meaning sound, and 법칙 meaning law or rule). This linguistic rule describes how certain words derived from Chinese characters (한자) are written and pronounced in Korean when specific consonants appear at the very beginning of a word. Specifically, the rule dictates changes for words that would originally start with the letters ㄹ or ㄴ.

The instructor emphasizes that while the complete academic rules for 두음법칙 are complex, they can be condensed into a few manageable patterns. Crucially, this rule only applies to 한자어 (Sino-Korean words) and only when the target sound is the "initial sound" of the entire word. It is presented as a natural phenomenon of language—a "path of least resistance"—where sounds were historically removed or altered to make them easier for Korean speakers to pronounce.

---

### The First Rule: ㄹ or ㄴ Changing to ㅇ
The first major rule states that whenever a Sino-Korean word originally starts with the letter ㄹ or ㄴ, and that letter is followed by one of six specific vowels, the initial consonant changes into ㅇ (which has no sound in the initial position). The six vowels that trigger this change are 이, 야, 여, 요, 유, and 예.

The instructor provides several common examples to illustrate this transformation. The word for woman, 여자, originally comes from characters that would be read as 녀자; however, because it starts with ㄴ followed by 여, the ㄴ is dropped to become 여자. Similarly, the word for age, 연세, comes from the character 연 (year), and the word for element, 요소, is written with ㅇ instead of its original initial sound. The word for dragon, 용, originally stems from the sound 룡. 

A significant point of confusion for learners is the surname Lee. While often romanized as "Lee" or "Rhee," it is written in South Korea as 이. The instructor notes that the famous actress 이다혜 (Lee Da-hae) writes her name with 이 even though the original 한자 reading is 리. Another example is the word 양심 (conscience), which follows this same pattern of dropping the initial consonant, as does 예의 (etiquette/manners).

---

### The Second Rule: ㄹ Changing to ㄴ
The second rule applies specifically to the letter ㄹ. When ㄹ appears at the beginning of a Sino-Korean word before vowels other than the six mentioned in the first rule, it does not disappear but instead changes into a ㄴ. The vowels that typically trigger this change include 아, 오, 우, 어, 에, and 외.

Examples of this rule include the word for tomorrow, 내일, which originates from the sound 네일. The word for senior citizen, 노인, would originally be read as 로인. Paradise is written as 낙원 instead of the original 락원, and the word for thunder, 뇌성, comes from 뢰성. The instructor also mentions 루각 (a pavilion) coming from 루각 and 능 (royal tombs) coming from 릉. In all these cases, the "harder" ㄹ sound at the start of the word is softened to a ㄴ for easier pronunciation.

---

### The "Initial Sound" Constraint and Exceptions
The instructor stresses that 두음법칙 is strictly an "initial sound" rule. If the same 한자 character appears anywhere else in a word besides the very first syllable, the rule does not apply, and the original sound is preserved. For instance, while 녀자 becomes 여자 at the start of a word, the ㄴ remains in the word 소녀 (young girl) because it is the second syllable. Similarly, while 연세 starts with ㅇ, the word 학년 (school year) keeps the ㄴ because it is not at the beginning.

There is a very specific exception regarding the sounds 열 and 율. When these sounds follow either a vowel or the consonant ㄴ, they change to 열 and 율 (dropping the initial ㄹ). For example, 비율 (ratio) is written this way because it follows a vowel, and 진열 (display) is written with ㅇ because it follows ㄴ. However, if they follow any other consonant, they remain as is, such as in the word 법률 (law), which keeps the 률 sound.

---

### Non-Applicability and Regional Differences
It is important to remember that 두음법칙 only applies to Sino-Korean words. It does not apply to native Korean words, such as the names of the letters themselves (리을, 니은) or the word 녀석 (fellow/guy). It also does not apply to loanwords from other languages, which is why words like 라면 (ramen), 뉴스 (news), and 레몬 (lemon) retain their initial ㄹ or ㄴ sounds. Furthermore, the rule only affects the start of a word; a word like 진리 (truth) is written as is and does not become "진이."

Finally, the instructor points out a major geopolitical distinction: these rules only apply to the standard language of South Korea. In North Korea, 두음법칙 is not followed. Therefore, words like 룡 (dragon) and 녀자 (woman/girl) are written and pronounced with their original initial consonants in the North, whereas they are 용 and 여자 in the South.
