## Lesson Overview

This lesson focuses on the complex mechanics of Korean pronunciation, specifically moving beyond basic letter recognition to understand how sounds interact and change in context. The primary objective is to master the rules of 받침 (final consonants) and the various forms of consonant assimilation. Ji emphasizes that these rules are not meant to be "crammed" or forced into memory through rote learning; instead, they should serve as a logical reference to help the student understand why certain words sound different from their written form.

The lesson is structured around a three-step learning model: Input, Process, and Output. Ji explains that while listening (Input) and understanding the logic (Process) are necessary, the most critical stage is Output—specifically typing and reading aloud. This physical practice builds muscle memory, allowing the fingers and tongue to remember the patterns of 한글 naturally, much like how English speakers "just know" the different pronunciations in words like "bomb" and "tomb."

The core content covers the general rule of consonants moving to silent syllables, various types of consonant assimilation (Amplifiers, Nasals, and the "wild card" ㅎ), and the specific phenomenon of palatalization. By the end of the lesson, the student should understand how Korean speakers prioritize ease of pronunciation and fluid sound transitions over literal enunciation of every written letter.

### Slide 20~21 — Three Types of Assimilation
- **On screen:** A table categorizing consonants:

    2. **Nasals (ㄴㄹㅁㅇ):** Produce more natural, fluid sounds.
    3. **ㅎ:** The wild card with 3 different ways (Silent, Aspirator, or turns into ㄷ).
- **Ji's teaching:** To understand this better, we divide these sounds into three categories. Amplifiers create tension in the mouth. We covered this briefly before—ㄱ, ㄷ, and ㅂ sounds form tension that makes the next sound a double consonant. Nasals (ㄴ, ㄹ, ㅁ, ㅇ) make the sound flow smoother. And ㅎ is a wild card; it can be silent, it can aspirate other letters, or it can even change into a ㄷ sound.

- **On screen:** "ㄱ ㄷ ㅂ 받침 + ㄱ ㄷ ㅂ ㅅ ㅈ makes ㄲ ㄸ ㅃ ㅆ ㅉ".
- **Ji's teaching:** Let’s look at amplifiers first. When an amplifier consonant (ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ) is followed by one of these five consonants—ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ, ㅅ, ㅈ—the sound turns into a double consonant. Notice they all have their double consonant counterpart. The tension built up in the mouth naturally amplifies them into stronger sounds.

## Teaching Points

- **General Rule:** When a syllable ending in a 받침 is followed by the silent consonant ㅇ, the 받침 sound moves to the next syllable (e.g., 음악 → [으막]).
- **Consonant Assimilation:**
    - **Amplifiers (ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ):** When followed by ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ, ㅅ, or ㅈ, they create tension that turns the following sound into a double consonant (ㄲ, ㄸ, ㅃ, ㅆ, ㅉ).
    - **Nasalization:** When amplifiers (ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ) meet nasals (ㄴ, ㅁ, ㄹ), they soften:
        - ㄱ → ㅇ (학년 → [항년])
        - ㄷ → ㄴ (받는 → [반는])
        - ㅂ → ㅁ (감사합니다 → [감사함니다])
    - **Liquid/Nasal Interactions:**
        - ㄴ + ㄹ or ㄹ + ㄴ both result in a double ㄹ sound (한류 → [할류], 실내 → [실래]).
        - ㄴ + ㅁ often sounds like double ㅁ (눈물 → [눔물]).
- **The ㅎ Wild Card:**
    - **Silent:** When followed by ㅇ or preceded by a nasal (좋아 → [조아], 실험 → [시럼]).
    - **Aspiration:** When ㅎ meets ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ, or ㅈ (in either order), it creates the aspirated sounds ㅋ, ㅌ, ㅍ, or ㅊ (좋다 → [조타], 입학 → [이팍]).
    - **ㄷ-Shift:** Before ㄴ, ㅎ acts as ㄷ, which then assimilates to ㄴ (좋네 → [존네]).
- **Palatalization:** When ㄷ or ㅌ are followed by 이 or 여, they shift to ㅈ or ㅊ sounds (같이 → [가치], 굳이 → [구지]).
