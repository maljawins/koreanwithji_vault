## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the grammar construction `~기는 하다` and its various derivatives, focusing on how the addition of the topic marker `는` to a nominalized verb creates a specific nuance of emphasis. The lesson moves from the basic structure of admitting a fact with reservations to more emphatic forms used for direct disagreement or expressing surprise.

The core of the lesson revolves around the pragmatic function of the topic marker `는` when attached to the `~기` nominalization form. By understanding the "rising and falling" intonation associated with the topic marker in English (e.g., "The *pineapple* pizza is okay..."), learners can grasp how Korean speakers use `~기는` to highlight a specific action or state while implying a contrast or a "but" that follows.

---

### The Basic Form: Verb Stem + 기는 하다
The most fundamental version of this form involves taking a verb stem, attaching the nominalizing suffix `기`, adding the topic marker `는`, and finishing with the verb `하다`. This transforms a verb like `먹다 (to eat)` into `먹기는 하다`. For descriptive verbs like `춥다 (to be cold)`, it becomes `춥기는 하다`. 

The instructor explains that this form is used to admit something is true but not completely. It carries the nuance of "I do [verb], but..." or "It is [adjective], but...". For example, if someone asks if you study, answering `공부하기는 해요` suggests "I *do* study (but maybe not enough or not effectively)" compared to the simple statement `공부해요 (I study)`. The instructor notes that `기는` is frequently shortened to `긴` in speech, with `기는` being slightly more formal.

> **철수가 한국 사람이긴 하지만 한국어를 못해요.** / Chul-soo is Korean (admittedly), but he can't speak Korean.

---

### Adding Emphasis: Repeating the Verb
A more emphatic variation of the previous form involves repeating the original verb at the end of the sentence instead of using `하다`. The structure is `Verb Stem + 기는/긴 + Verb`. For example, instead of `많기는 해요`, one can say `많기는 많아요`.

This repetition reinforces the admission. The instructor provides an example regarding the weather:
> **한국 여름이 덥다더니 덥긴 정말 덥네요.** / I heard Korean summers are hot, and they really are hot (as for being hot, they are truly hot).

---

### Disagreeing with ~기는(요)
The "main form" of the lesson is using `~기는` or `~긴` to end a sentence directly, often followed by the polite ending `요`. When the confirming verb (`하다` or the repeated verb) is removed, the nuance shifts from "admitting with reservation" to "disagreeing."

In this context, the form functions as a way to say "No" or "Come on" in response to a compliment or a statement. It uses the emphasis of the topic marker to single out the other person's word and reject it. If someone compliments your Korean skills, you can respond with `잘하기는요` to humbly disagree.

> **에이, 대단하기는요. 운이 좋았어요.** / Come on, amazing? (No way). I was just lucky.

---

### Emphatic Disagreement: ~긴 뭐가 ~
To further strengthen a disagreement, speakers can use the construction `Verb Stem + 긴 뭐가 + Verb`. This literally asks, "As for [verb], *what* is [verb]?" It is equivalent to English expressions like "Cold? What's cold about this?" or "You call this salty?"

The instructor demonstrates this with weather and food:
> **춥긴 뭐가 추워?** / Cold? How is this cold? (What about this is cold?)
> **이게 짜긴 뭐가 짜요?** / This is salty? What's salty about this?

---

### The Adverbial Use of 하긴
Finally, the instructor introduces `하긴`, which functions differently from the previous forms. It is used at the beginning of a sentence to signal agreement with the other person, often after a moment of reflection. It can be translated as "Now that I think about it," "After all," or "True."

> **하긴, 여름이 많이 덥긴 하지.** / True, now that I think about it, it is very hot in the summer.
