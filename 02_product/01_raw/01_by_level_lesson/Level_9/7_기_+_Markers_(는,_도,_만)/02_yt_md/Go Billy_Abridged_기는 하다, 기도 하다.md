### Emphasizing with ~기는 하다 and ~긴 하다
The first structure introduced is `~기는 하다`, which can be shortened to `~긴 하다`. To form this, you take a verb stem and attach `~기는 하다`. The instructor notes that while the particle `는` is technically optional, it is used most of the time. A crucial conjugation rule is that the first part of the expression (`~기는` or `~긴`) never changes; only the final `하다` is conjugated for tense or politeness. For example, even in the past tense, the stem remains `하긴`, and only the ending changes to `했어요`.

The primary function of this form is to emphasize the meaning of "do" or "is" within a verb. It is similar to the English distinction between "I like pizza" and "I *do* like pizza." While the core meaning remains the same, the emphasis shifts. Pragmatically, this is often used when admitting something but with reservations. It suggests that while the statement is true, there is more to the story or a contrasting point coming next.

The instructor provides several examples to illustrate this "admission with reservation" nuance:
- **피자를 좋아해요.** / I like pizza.
- **피자를 좋아하긴 해요.** / I *do* like pizza (but maybe I shouldn't eat it today).
- **날씨가 덥긴 해요.** / The weather *is* hot (but I don't mind it).

This form is frequently followed by contrasting connectors like `~는데` or `~지만`. For instance, `저는 친구가 있긴 한데 다 한국에 있어요` means "I do have friends, but they are all in Korea." Similarly, `맛있긴 하지만 너무 비싸요` translates to "It does taste good, but it's too expensive."

---

### Reusing the Verb for Extra Emphasis
An advanced variation of this structure involves repeating the original verb instead of using `하다` at the end. For example, instead of `가기는 하다`, one can say `가기는 가다`. The instructor explains that using the verb again adds even more emphasis to the action. This is shown in the sentence `철수도 가기는 갈 것 같은데 잘 모르겠어요`, which emphasizes that while the speaker thinks Chul-soo *will* go, they aren't entirely certain.

Other examples of this emphasis include:
- **낮잠을 자긴 했지만 아직 피곤해요.** / I did take a nap, but I'm still tired.
- **제가 미국 사람이긴 하지만 영어를 못해요.** / I am an American, but I can't speak English.
- **알긴 아는데 왜?** / I *do* know, but why? (Why are you asking?)
- **그 영화를 보긴 봤는데 별로 재밌지 않았어요.** / I did see that movie, but it wasn't that good.

---

### Adding "Also" or "Even" with ~기도 하다
The second major point is `~기도 하다`. This structure follows the same construction method as the previous one but uses the particle `도` (also/too/even). This indicates that a person or thing "even" does an action or "also" possesses a quality. Like the previous form, the main verb can be repeated for emphasis (e.g., `크기도 크다` to mean "it was even big").

The instructor highlights that `~기도 하다` is a common way to express "both A and B" in Korean. For example, `영희는 정말 똑똑해요. 그리고 예쁘기도 해요` means "Yeong-hui is really smart, and she is even pretty," which can be translated as "Yeong-hui is both smart and pretty."

Further examples of "both A and B" usage include:
- **그 케이크는 비싸기도 하고 너무 크기도 컸어요.** / That cake was both expensive and too big.
- **시간이 있을 때 드라마를 보기도 하고 음악을 듣기도 해요.** / When I have time, I both watch dramas and listen to music.
- **이 요리는 준비하기 쉽기도 하고 맛있기도 해요.** / This dish is both easy to make and tastes good.

The instructor emphasizes that using `~기도 하다` in these paired sentences makes the speech sound more natural than simply listing actions, as it shows that both points are equally important and emphasized.
