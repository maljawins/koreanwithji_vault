## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces two similar grammar forms: ~기는 하다 and ~기도 하다. Both forms utilize the nominalizing verb ending ~기, which allows verbs to function as nouns within larger grammatical structures. While they share a structural foundation, their pragmatic functions differ significantly: one is used for emphasis and contrast, while the other is used to indicate addition or multiple states.

The lesson is framed as being for intermediate learners, as these forms deal with nuanced emphasis rather than basic sentence construction. The instructor uses a "Difficulty Meter" visual, placing this topic at approximately a 40/100 level. Throughout the video, the instructor demonstrates how these forms apply to both action verbs and descriptive verbs.

---

### The ~기 Ending and Emphasis
Before diving into the specific forms, the instructor explains the role of the ~기 ending. It transforms a verb stem into a noun form. For example, the instructor shows the sentence 김치를 좋아해요 (I like kimchi). By adding the ~기 ending to the verb stem 좋아하- and attaching the topic marker 는, the sentence becomes 김치를 좋아하기는 해요 (I *do* like kimchi). 

Visually, the instructor highlights how the topic marker 는 adds a layer of emphasis. While the basic meaning remains "liking kimchi," the ~기는 하다 version specifically emphasizes the act of liking. This structural change sets the stage for the two primary grammar points of the lesson.

---

### Using ~기는 하다 for Contrast and Admission
The form ~기는 하다 is constructed by taking a verb stem, attaching ~기, adding the topic marker 는, and finishing with the verb 하다. In casual conversation, this is frequently shortened to ~긴 하다. The instructor notes that no other tenses are used within the ~기 portion itself; the tense is expressed by conjugating the final 하다.

The primary function of this form is to emphasize the verb while simultaneously adding contrast. It often carries the feeling of "admitting" something while holding back other information or preparing to offer a "but." The instructor provides several examples to illustrate this "admitting" nuance:

- **맛있기는 하지만 비싸요.** / It does taste good (I'll admit), but it's expensive.
- **한국 사람이긴 하지만 매운 음식을 잘 못 먹어요.** / Although they are Korean, they can't eat spicy food well.
- **한국에 관심이 있긴 한데 아직 한국에 안 가봤어요.** / I am interested in Korea, but I haven't been to Korea yet.

The instructor breaks down the literal meaning: 맛있기 (being delicious) + 는 (as for) + 하다 (it is). Thus, 맛있기는 하다 literally translates to "As for being delicious, it is," which naturally implies a contrastive follow-up.

---

### Using ~기도 하다 for Addition
The second form, ~기도 하다, is constructed similarly: verb stem + ~기 + the particle 도 (meaning "also" or "even") + 하다. Unlike the previous form, this does not focus on contrast. Instead, it shows that a verb *also* happens or a state *also* exists in addition to other things.

The instructor emphasizes a critical distinction between using the particle 도 on a subject versus using the ~기도 하다 form on a verb. If you say 저도 한국어 선생님이에요, it means "I too (among other people) am a Korean teacher." However, using the new form changes the scope:

- **제가 한국어 선생님이기도 해요.** / I also am a Korean teacher (in addition to being other things, like a student or a writer).
- **저는 요리를 잘하기도 해요.** / I also cook well (in addition to my other skills).

The instructor provides a comparison using the character Chul-soo:
- **철수 씨도 똑똑해요.** / Chul-soo is also smart (like other people).
- **철수 씨는 똑똑하기도 해요.** / Chul-soo is also smart (in addition to his other talents).

Finally, the instructor shows how this applies to routines or habits:
> **저는 심심할 때 드라마를 보기도 해요.** / When I'm bored, I also watch dramas (in addition to doing other things).
