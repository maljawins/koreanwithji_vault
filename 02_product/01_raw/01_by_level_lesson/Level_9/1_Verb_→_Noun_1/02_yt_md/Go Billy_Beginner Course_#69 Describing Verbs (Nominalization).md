## Teaching Points

### Overview

This lesson introduces the concept of nominalization, which is the process of turning action verbs into nouns. While learners have previously studied how to describe nouns using descriptive verbs (adjectives), they must learn to transform action verbs into a noun form before those actions can be described or used as the subject of a sentence. The instructor explains that you cannot simply attach a descriptive verb directly to an action verb stem; for example, saying "to swim is difficult" requires changing "to swim" into "swimming." The video covers two primary methods for nominalization—using the suffixes 기 and 는 것—and briefly introduces a third form using 음/ㅁ for creating established nouns.

---

### What is Nominalization?

Nominalization is a linguistic term that simply means "changing something into a noun." In Korean, action verbs cannot be described on their own. The instructor provides a visual example of an incorrect sentence: `수영하다가 어려워요`. This is wrong because `수영하다 (to swim)` is a verb, and only nouns can be described by descriptive verbs like `어렵다 (to be difficult)`. To fix this, the verb must be changed into a noun form that means "swimming." Once a verb is nominalized, it can function as a subject or object in a sentence and can be modified by descriptive verbs to express ideas like "Learning Korean is difficult" or "I like to swim."

---

### The 기 Nominalization Form

The first common way to change a verb into a noun is by using the 기 form. The construction is simple: take the verb stem of an action verb and attach the suffix 기. This works for any action verb regardless of whether the stem ends in a vowel or a consonant. The instructor demonstrates this with two basic examples: `하다 (to do)` becomes `하기 (doing)`, and `먹다 (to eat)` becomes `먹기 (eating)`. Once transformed, these words function exactly like any other noun in a sentence.

The instructor provides several example sentences to show how this form is used in context:
- **저는 수영하기를 좋아해요.** / I like swimming.
- **여기서 한국어를 배우기가 어려워요.** / Learning Korean is difficult here.
- **서울에서 운전하기가 무서워요.** / Driving in Seoul is scary.
- **연습하기가 중요해요.** / Practicing is important.
- **영화를 보기** / Watching movies.

---

### The 는 것 Nominalization Form

The second common method for nominalization involves attaching 는 것 to the action verb stem. The word 것 means "thing," so this form literally translates to "the thing of doing [verb]." To create this form, take an action verb stem and attach 는 것. For example, `하다` becomes `하는 것 (doing)`, and `먹다` becomes `먹는 것 (eating)`. There is one specific rule for verbs with a 릴 (ㄹ) consonant at the end of the stem: you must remove the ㄹ before attaching 는 것. Therefore, `살다 (to live)` becomes `사는 것 (living)`.

In spoken Korean, the combination `것이` is frequently shortened to `게` for ease of pronunciation. The instructor provides examples using both the full and shortened forms:
- **저는 수영하는 것을 좋아해요.** / I like swimming.
- **여기서 한국어를 배우는 것이 어려워요.** / Learning Korean is difficult here.
- **서울에서 운전하는 게 무서워요.** / Driving in Seoul is scary.
- **연습하는 게 중요해요.** / Practicing is important.
- **영화를 보는 것** / Watching movies.

---

### Choosing Between 기 and 는 것

While both forms are used to change verbs into nouns and often carry the same meaning, there are subtle differences in usage and nuance. Generally, the 는 것 form sounds slightly more formal than the 기 form, yet it often sounds more natural in everyday sentences. For instance, while both `저는 한국어를 배우는 게 쉬워요` and `저는 한국어를 배우기가 쉬워요` mean "Learning Korean is easy," the 는 것 version is typically preferred in speech.

However, the 기 form is mandatory when creating a list of items or a "to-do list." The instructor shows a visual list of tasks:
- **책 읽기** / Reading a book
- **밥 먹기** / Eating a meal
- **한국어 연습하기** / Practicing Korean
- **일하기** / Working

In these list formats, using 는 것 would be incorrect. Additionally, the instructor notes that certain advanced grammar patterns specifically require one form over the other, which will be covered in future lessons.

---

### The 음/ㅁ Nominalization Form

The instructor introduces a third, more advanced form of nominalization that uses the suffix 음 (after a consonant) or ㅁ (after a vowel). Unlike the previous two forms, which create a "gerund" (the act of doing something), the 음/ㅁ form often creates a distinct noun that has its own entry in the dictionary. This form is used for established concepts rather than just describing an ongoing action.

Examples of nouns formed this way include:
- **가르치다 (to teach)** → **가르침 (teachings)**
- **자다 (to sleep)** → **잠 (sleep)**
- **싸우다 (to fight)** → **싸움 (a fight)**
- **슬프다 (to be sad)** → **슬픔 (sadness)**
- **살다 (to live)** → **삶 (life)**
- **죽다 (to die)** → **죽음 (death)**

The instructor advises students not to worry about mastering the construction of this form yet, but rather to be able to recognize it when they see these specific nouns in the dictionary.

---

### Conversation Practice: Action Verb vs. Descriptive Verb

The lesson concludes with a dialogue that highlights a common point of confusion: the difference between `싫어하다` and `싫다`. While both mean "to dislike," `싫어하다` is an action verb and `싫다` is a descriptive verb. This means they require different particles. In the sentence `저도 이 숙제 하기가 정말 싫어요`, the speaker uses the descriptive verb `싫다`, which takes the subject marker `가` on the nominalized verb `하기`.

The full practice dialogue:
> **오늘의 숙제가 정말 재미없어요.** / Today's homework is really not fun.
> **저도 이 숙제 하기가 정말 싫어요. 같이 친구들을 만나러 갈까요?** / I also really don't want to do this homework. Shall we go meet friends together?
> **하지만 숙제를 하는 것도 중요해요.** / But doing homework is also important.
> **네, 중요하지만 노는 것도 중요하지 않아요?** / Yes, it's important, but isn't hanging out also important?
> **저는 지금 나가기가 조금 힘들어요. 다음 시험이 오늘 밤이에요.** / For me, going out right now is a little difficult. The next test is tonight.
> **아, 맞아요.** / Ah, that's right.
