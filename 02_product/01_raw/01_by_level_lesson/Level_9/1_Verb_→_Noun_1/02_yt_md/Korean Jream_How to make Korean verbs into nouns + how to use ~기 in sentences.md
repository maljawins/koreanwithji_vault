## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces the three primary ways to transform Korean verbs into nouns: using the suffixes **~기**, **~(으)ㅁ**, and the modifying structure **~는 것 (거)**. While all three serve the purpose of nominalization, they carry distinct grammatical nuances and temporal orientations. The lesson focuses on the conceptual differences between these forms, particularly the prospective nature of **~기** versus the retrospective nature of **~(으)ㅁ**, and provides specific idiomatic patterns where **~기** is the required choice.

---

### Nominalization Methods: 기, 음, and 는 것

The instructor categorizes the three methods based on how they function. Both **~기** and **~(으)ㅁ** are ways to change the verb itself into a standalone noun. In contrast, **~는 것** (often shortened to **~는 거** in speech) is technically a noun-modifying form that translates to "the act of ~ing" or "the fact that [Subject] + [Verb]."

While **~는 거** is the most general and versatile way to express the "act of doing something" in daily conversation—such as in **책 읽는 거 좋아해요** (I like reading books)—the other two forms have specific temporal nuances. The instructor explains that **~기** is prospective, meaning it refers to things that haven't happened yet, things that should happen, or future intentions. Conversely, **~(으)ㅁ** is retrospective, referring to things that are already completed or established facts.

---

### Practical Applications of 기 and 음

Because of its prospective nature, **~기** is the standard form used for to-do lists (**할 일**), bucket lists, resolutions, and promises. On-screen examples include **청소하기** (cleaning), **숙제하기** (doing homework), and the resolution **일찍 자기** (going to bed early). These are all actions intended for the future.

The retrospective **~(으)ㅁ** is frequently used in formal reports or PowerPoint presentations to summarize completed actions or established states. However, the instructor notes that many words using these endings have become "fixed nouns" where the original prospective or retrospective meaning is no longer the primary focus. Students are encouraged to memorize these as independent vocabulary items.

**Fixed Nouns with ~기:**
- **달리기** (running)
- **걷기** (walking)
- **말하기** (speaking)
- **듣기** (listening)
- **쓰기** (writing)
- **읽기** (reading)

**Fixed Nouns with ~(으)ㅁ:**
- **춤** (dance) — from **추다** (to dance)
- **그림** (painting/drawing) — from **그리다** (to paint/draw)
- **잠** (sleep) — from **자다** (to sleep)

### Making Decisions and Plans with ~기로 하다

A major idiomatic use of **~기** involves making decisions or plans. The instructor introduces the pattern **Verb Stem + 기로 결심하다** (to resolve to do), **기로 결정하다** (to decide to do), or most commonly, **기로 하다** (to decide/plan to do). This structure is typically used in the past tense (**~기로 했어요**) because the decision itself has already been made, even though the action is prospective.

The conjugation is simple: remove **다** from the verb and add **기**.
- **먹다** (to eat) → **먹기로 했어요** (decided to eat)
- **가다** (to go) → **가기로 했어요** (decided to go)

Example sentence:
> **친구랑 저녁 먹기로 약속했어요.** / I promised to eat dinner with a friend.

---

### Starting Actions with ~기 시작하다

To express that one has started a new activity, the pattern **Verb Stem + 기 시작하다** is used. The instructor demonstrates this with several examples:
- **한국 드라마 보기 시작했어요.** (I started watching Korean dramas.)
- **한국 노래 듣기 시작했어요.** (I started listening to Korean songs.)

The instructor shares her own current example: **저는 요즘 모범택시 보기 시작했어요** (I started watching [the drama] Taxi Driver lately).

---

### Expressing Feelings and Evaluations with ~기가 + Adjective

The **~기** form is used when evaluating an action or expressing a feeling about a prospective task. The structure is **Verb Stem + 기가 (or 기) + Adjective**. Common adjectives used here include **싫다** (to hate/not want to), **어렵다** (to be difficult), **쉽다** (to be easy), **힘들다** (to be hard/tiring), **귀찮다** (to be bothersome), and **재미있다** (to be fun).

Examples:
- **설거지 하기 싫어요.** (I don't want to do the dishes / Doing dishes is not likable.)
- **설거지 하기 귀찮아요.** (Doing dishes is bothersome.)
- **매일 공부하기 힘들어요.** (Studying every day is hard.)
- **단어 외우기 어려워요.** (Memorizing words is difficult.)

**Important Usage Note:** While **~기 싫어요** is the standard way to say "I don't want to do something," the opposite "I want to do something" is NOT expressed as **~기 좋아요**. Instead, use the desire marker **~고 싶어요**. If you use **~기 좋아요**, the subject is usually an external factor or environment that is "good for" an activity.
- **여행하기 좋아요.** (It's good for traveling / The conditions are good for travel.)
- **봄은 여행하기 좋아요.** (Spring is good for traveling.)

---

### Cultural Reference: "A Good Day to Die"

The lesson concludes with a famous movie quote that utilizes the **~기** form:
> **거 오늘 죽기 딱 좋은 날씨네.** / Oh well, it's a perfect weather to die today.

The instructor breaks down the components: **거** (filler), **오늘** (today), **죽기** (dying), **딱** (perfectly/just fitting), **좋은** (good), **날씨** (weather), and **네** (sentence ending showing reaction/realization). This line is a famous quote from the gangster movie *New World* (**신세계**) and is frequently parodied in Korean media, including the drama *True Beauty* shown in the video.
