## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor provides a comprehensive guide to Korean adverbs, moving from the basic definition of what an adverb does to the specific grammatical structures used to create them in Korean. An adverb's primary role is to describe a verb, an adjective, or even another adverb, adding detail about how something happens or the way in which something is. While adverbs can modify many parts of speech, they cannot describe nouns or particles.

The lesson covers three main categories: adverbs formed by attaching `~게` to descriptive verb stems, those formed with `~히` (specifically for `하다` verbs), and "pure" or unique Korean adverbs that exist independently of conjugation. The instructor emphasizes that while rules exist, learners should prioritize memorizing common adverbs as individual vocabulary items because natural usage often favors one form over another regardless of the grammatical rule.

---

### Forming Adverbs with ~게
The most common way to transform a descriptive verb into an adverb is by taking the verb stem and attaching `~게`. To find the stem, simply remove the `다` from the dictionary form of the descriptive verb. For example, the instructor demonstrates on the whiteboard that `바쁘다 (to be busy)` becomes `바쁘게 (busily/in a busy way)`. This form can be used to modify action verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs, but it cannot be used with action verb stems.

The instructor explains that `~게` adverbs often translate to "in a [verb] way." This nuance helps learners understand sentences like:
- **바쁘게 지내다** / to pass time in a busy way (to be busy)
- **행복하게 살고 있어요.** / I am living happily.
- **철수는 무섭게 웃었어요.** / Chul-soo smiled scarily.
- **머리카락을 짧게 잘랐어요.** / I cut my hair short (so that it is short).
- **크게 말하지 마세요.** / Don't talk loudly (in a big way).
- **비싸게 사고 싶지 않아요.** / I don't want to buy it expensively (overpay).

A particularly useful application is with the verb `보다 (to watch/see)`. Using `재밌게 보다` means you enjoyed watching something, whereas just saying the movie was enjoyable describes the movie itself. Similarly, `맛있게 드세요` (literally "eat in a delicious way") is the standard way to say "enjoy your meal."

---

### Adverbs Ending in ~히
For descriptive verbs that end in `하다`, such as `안전하다 (to be safe)` or `조용하다 (to be quiet)`, the ending can sometimes change to `~히` instead of `~하게`. In many cases, both are grammatically correct, but one usually sounds more natural. For instance, while `안전하게` and `안전히` are both acceptable, `안전히` is more common. 

The instructor lists several examples where `~히` is preferred or mandatory:
- **천천히** / slowly (from the original verb `천천하다`, though `천천하게` is never used)
- **완전히** / completely (preferred over `완전하게`)
- **조심히** / carefully (much more common than `조심하게`, especially in the phrase `조심히 가세요` for "go safely")
- **조용히** / quietly (from `조용하다`)

Conversely, some `하다` verbs prefer the `~하게` ending, such as `급하게 (urgently/suddenly)`, where `급하게` is favored over `급히`.

---

### Unique Korean Adverbs and Special Cases
Many of the most common Korean adverbs are "unique," meaning they are not conjugated from verbs but exist as independent words. The instructor highlights `빨리 (quickly/hurriedly)` as a key example. While `빠르게` (from `빠르다`) exists and means "in a fast way," only `빨리` can be used to mean "hurry up." For example, `숙제 빨리 해` means "hurry up and do your homework," whereas `숙제 빠르게 해` would literally mean "write your homework at a high physical speed."

Other common unique adverbs include:
- **더** / more and **덜** / less (e.g., `더 주세요` / please give me more)
- **아주, 정말, 진짜** / very, really (e.g., `진짜 빨리 갔어요` / I went really quickly)
- **많이** / a lot (the adverb form of `많다`; used in the parting phrase `많이 파세요` / "sell a lot," wishing a shopkeeper success)

Negative adverbs, which must be paired with a negative verb form, include `전혀 (not at all)`, `별로 (not much)`, and `절대로 (never)`.

---

### Adverbs with ~으로 and Time Adverbs
Adverbs can also be formed using the particle `~으로`. This is common with nouns ending in `~적`. For example, `일반적 (general)` becomes the adverb `일반적으로 (generally/normally)`. Other examples include `주로 (mainly)`, `진짜로 (really)`, and `실제로 (actually)`.

Finally, the instructor covers time adverbs, which describe when an action takes place. These include:
- **오늘, 어제, 내일** / today, yesterday, tomorrow
- **지금, 그때, 나중에** / now, at that time, later
- **이따가** / in a little bit (later today)
- **방금** / just now
- **이미** / already
- **아직** / yet, still (used with negative verbs: `아직 안 먹었어요` / I haven't eaten yet)
- **자주** / often and **가끔** / sometimes

The lesson concludes with a reminder that because there are hundreds of adverbs, it is best to learn them one by one as vocabulary rather than relying solely on conjugation rules.
