## Teaching Points

### Overview
This lesson focuses on the systematic creation of adverbs from descriptive verbs in Korean. While students may already be familiar with fixed adverbs like 더 (more) or 아주 (very), the instructor explains that most descriptive verbs can be transformed into adverbs to describe the manner in which an action is performed. The primary mechanism for this transformation is the addition of the suffix 게 to the verb stem.

The instructor guides the learner through the basic conjugation rules, explores the specific nuances of verbs ending in 하다, and introduces several unique adverbs that do not follow the standard pattern. The lesson concludes with a practical conversation and a deep dive into the specific behavior of the verbs 있다 (to exist) and 없다 (to not exist) when functioning adverbially.

---

### Creating Adverbs with 게
The most common way to create an adverb from a descriptive verb is to take the verb stem and attach the suffix 게. This is equivalent to adding "-ly" to an adjective in English. For example, the verb 늦다 (to be late) becomes the adverb 늦게 (late). The instructor provides a clear example sentence: 선생님이 수업을 늦게 시작했어요 (The teacher started class late).

Visually, the instructor emphasizes that while this form is highly productive, not every adverb created this way will sound natural to a native speaker. The literal meaning of adding 게 is "so that" or "in a manner that." Therefore, a sentence like 행복하게 살고 싶어요 (I want to live happily) literally translates to "I want to live so that I am happy."

---

### Adverbs from 하다 Verbs
Descriptive verbs ending in 하다 follow a slightly more complex pattern. They can often become adverbs in two ways: by attaching 게 to become 하게, or by changing the ending to 히. The instructor notes that the choice between these two is not always interchangeable and often depends on the specific verb.

- **천천하다 (to be slow)** becomes **천천히 (slowly)**. Using 천천하게 would sound awkward and incorrect.
- **조용하다 (to be quiet)** becomes **조용히 (quietly)**.
- **위험하다 (to be dangerous)** becomes **위험하게 (dangerously)**.
- **안전하다 (to be safe)** is flexible and can become either **안전하게** or **안전히**.
- **완전하다 (to be complete)** can also use both **완전히** or **완전하게**.
- **강하다 (to be strong)** and **약하다 (to be weak)** both prefer the 게 form, becoming **강하게 (strongly)** and **약하게 (weakly)**.

Because of these variations, the instructor recommends learning these specific adverbs as individual vocabulary items and consulting a dictionary when in doubt.

---

### Unique and Common Adverbs
The lesson highlights several adverbs that do not end in 게 or 히. Some are words the student likely already knows, such as 빨리 (quickly), 더 (more), and 아주 (very). The instructor introduces several new "unique" adverbs:

- **실수로 (by accident / mistakenly)** and **일부러 (on purpose / intentionally)**.
- **잠시** or **잠깐 (a brief moment)**. While both mean "a moment," 잠깐 is more common in informal speech.
- **혼자서 (alone / without others)**. The instructor notes that the 서 in this word is optional.

Example sentences shown on screen include:
> **제 숙제를 실수로 버렸어요.** / I threw away my homework by accident.
> **저는 일부러 빨리 했어요.** / I did it quickly on purpose.
> **저는 혼자서 서울에서 살고 있어요.** / I am living alone in Seoul.

---

### Adverbs from 있다 and 없다
A special distinction is made for the verbs 있다 (to exist/have) and 없다 (to not exist/not have). While 있다 follows the standard rule and becomes 있게 (in a manner that is...), 없다 does not become 없게. Instead, it changes to 없이 (without).

The instructor explains that 없이 is used directly after a noun, often omitting the subject marker. This is demonstrated through several examples:
- **친구 없이 (without friends)**
- **청소기 없이 (without a vacuum cleaner)**
- **돈 없이 (without money)**

The instructor also covers the adverbial forms of 재밌다 (to be fun) and 맛있다 (to be delicious). 재밌게 and 맛있게 are often translated naturally as "to enjoy." For example, 라면을 맛있게 먹었어요 translates to "I enjoyed the ramen," though it literally means "I ate the ramen deliciously." Similarly, 맛있게 드세요 is the standard way to say "Enjoy your meal."
