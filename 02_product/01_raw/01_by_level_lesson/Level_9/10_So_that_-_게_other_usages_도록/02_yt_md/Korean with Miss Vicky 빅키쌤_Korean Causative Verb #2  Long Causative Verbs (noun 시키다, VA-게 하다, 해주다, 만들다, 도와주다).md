## Teaching Points

### Overview

This lesson focuses on "long" causative verbs in Korean, which are used to express making or letting someone do something. While the previous lesson covered short causative verbs (using suffixes like 이, 히, 리, 기), this session explores more complex structures including the transformation of 하다 verbs into 시키다, the versatile 게 하다 construction, and various nuanced alternatives like 게 만들다 and 게 해주다. The instructor emphasizes that causative expressions often involve a "causer" (the one initiating the action) and a "causee" (the one performing the action).

The learning arc begins with the simplest long form—replacing 하다 with 시키다—and moves into the more universal 게 하다. A significant portion of the class is dedicated to the subtle nuances of particle usage (을/를 vs. 에게/한테 vs. 이/가) for marking the causee, explaining how these choices signal intentionality or the nature of the causer. The final section covers variations that add specific emotional or situational colors, such as forcefulness, inevitability, or helpfulness.

---

### The 시키다 Causative Form

For verbs that end in 하다, the most straightforward way to create a causative meaning is to replace 하다 with 시키다. This transforms "to do" into "to make [someone] do." The instructor provides several examples to illustrate this transformation and its usage in context.

- **공부하다 (to study) → 공부시키다 (to make study)**
  In an active sentence like "아이들이 억지로 공부를 해요" (Children study obligedly), the children are the subject. In a causative sentence like "아이들을 억지로 공부시키지 마세요" (Don't force children to study), the speaker is telling a causer not to make the children study. The causee (children) can be marked with either 을/를 or 한테/에게.
- **말하다 (to speak/talk) → 말 시키다 (to make speak/talk)**
  While "말하기 싫어요" means "I don't want to talk," the expression "나한테 말 시키지 마" (Don't make me talk) is the natural Korean way to say "Don't talk to me." Using 한테 is more common and natural here than using 를.
- **화해하다 (to reconcile) → 화해시키다 (to make reconcile)**
  If two people fight (싸우다), a third party like a parent might need to reconcile them: "부모가 제대로 화해시켜야 해요" (Parents must reconcile them properly).
- **구경하다 (to look around) → 구경시키다 (to show around)**
  This is often used when thanking someone for a tour: "서울을 구경시켜줘서 고마워" (Thank you for showing me around Seoul).
- **감동하다 (to feel moved) → 감동시키다 (to move/touch someone)**
  The instructor notes that 감동하다 is a verb (to feel moved), while 감동적이다 is the adjective (to be moving). A song can move people: "그 노래가 많은 사람들을 감동시켰어요" (That song moved many people).

A critical nuance regarding particles is introduced here: when the causee is marked with 한테 or 에게, it implies the causer acted with specific intention. Because an inanimate object like a song (노래) cannot have human intentions, marking the people it moved with 한테 would sound awkward. In such cases, 을/를 is the correct choice.

### The 게 하다 Construction

The 게 하다 form is the most versatile causative structure because it can be attached to almost any verb or adjective. Its literal meaning is "to do so that [someone/something] [verbs/is adjective]."

The instructor reviews the base 게 grammar, which indicates intention or result (so that/in order to). For example, "아들이 수업에 늦지 않게 일찍 깨웠어요" (I woke my son up early so that he wouldn't be late for class). When combined with 하다, it becomes a formal causative.

A unique feature of 게 하다 is that the causee can be marked with four different particles: 을/를, 에게, 한테, or even the subject markers 이/가. The use of 이/가 is possible because 게 technically attaches to a whole clause, and that clause requires its own subject.

- **집에 가게 하다 (to make someone go home)**
  > **선생님께서 학생들을 집에 가게 하셨어요.** / The teacher made the students go home.
  > **선생님께서 학생들에게 집에 가게 하셨어요.** / (Same, emphasizing teacher's intent)
  > **선생님께서 학생들이 집에 가게 하셨어요.** / (Same, using the clause-subject marker)
- **물 마시게 하다 (to make someone drink water)**
  > **제가 고양이한테 물을 마시게 했어요.** / I made the cat drink water.
  Using 한테 is often the clearest choice to avoid "double object" confusion if the verb already has an object (like 물).

---

### Variations and Nuances: 만들다, 도록 하다, and 해주다

The instructor outlines several variations of the long causative, each carrying a different "flavor" or nuance.

- **V-도록 하다:** This is interchangeable with 게 하다 but is strictly for verbs and sounds much more formal. It is only used for intended actions, never for accidents or inevitable consequences.
- **V/A-게 만들다:** This adds a nuance of forcefulness or inevitability. While "공부하게 해요" is a neutral "makes me study," "공부하게 만들어요" implies the book is so good (or the situation so pressing) that one is bound to study. It is often used for strong emotional results: "그 사람이 나를 사랑하게 만들 거야" (I will make that person fall in love with me).
- **V/A-게 해주다:** This combines the causative with the "favor" auxiliary verb 아/어 주다. It means to help someone do something or to give permission. It indicates a direct, helpful effect from the causer to the causee.
- **V-게 도와주다:** Similar to 게 해주다 but indicates a less proactive form of help. "잠을 깨게 해줘요" (helps me wake up) implies the object is the main reason I woke up, whereas "도와주다" would imply it merely assisted a process already in motion.

---

### 시키다 vs. 게 하다

The instructor addresses the common confusion between these two forms. While they often overlap, 시키다 usually requires a human subject who directly influences the causee. For example, "공부시키다" sounds most natural when a person (parent/teacher) is the causer. If the causer is an inanimate object like a book, "공부하게 하다" or "공부하게 만들다" is preferred. However, some verbs like 감동시키다 are exceptions and can be used freely with inanimate subjects. The instructor concludes that there is no "rule of thumb" that applies to every verb; rather, learners must gain exposure to see which patterns native speakers prefer for specific words.
