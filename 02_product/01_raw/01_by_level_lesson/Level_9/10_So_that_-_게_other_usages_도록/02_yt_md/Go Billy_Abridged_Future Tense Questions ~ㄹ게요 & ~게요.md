### Overview
The instructor introduces two major grammatical forms that end in 게 or 게요, focusing on their distinct functions in expressing future intentions and asking about others' plans. The lesson begins with the common future tense ending ~(을)ㄹ게요, which is used by a speaker to declare an action they intend to perform, often with the nuance of doing a favor or making a promise. This is contrasted with the second form, ~게요, which is primarily used in questions to inquire about someone else's intentions or plans based on information they have just shared.

The learning arc moves from the mechanics of conjugation—handling consonant and vowel endings—to the pragmatic nuances that separate these forms from the standard future tense. The instructor also covers specialized and archaic uses of these endings, such as playful guessing games and historical command forms. By the end of the lesson, students should understand not just how to form these endings, but the specific social contexts in which they are appropriate.

### The Future Tense Ending ~(을)ㄹ게요
The first form is created by taking an action verb stem and attaching 을게요 if it ends in a consonant, or ㄹ게요 if it ends in a vowel. For example, 먹다 (to eat) becomes 먹을게요, and 가다 (to go) becomes 갈게요. If a verb stem already ends in ㄹ, such as 살다 (to live), no additional changes are needed before adding 게요. Although written as 게요, the instructor notes a sound change rule where it is pronounced as a double consonant: 께요.

This form is strictly limited to action verbs performed by the speaker. It cannot be used with descriptive verbs like 피곤하다 (to be tired), nor can it be used to describe the actions of a third party or to ask a question. Its primary function is to say "I will" in a way that implies a promise, a decision, or a favor. The instructor illustrates this with the example of offering to eat the last slice of pizza: 제가 먹을게요 (I'll eat it). This carries a different nuance than the standard future tense ~(을) 거예요, which is a simple statement of fact. Using ~(을)ㄹ게요 suggests "Don't worry, I'll take care of it."

Common examples of this "favor/promise" nuance include:
- **제가 살게요.** / I'll buy it (Don't worry, it's on me).
- **조심할게요.** / I'll be safe (I promise to be careful).
- **먼저 갈게요.** / I'll be going first (I've decided to leave now).
- **사진을 찍어줄게요.** / I'll take a picture for you.
- **제가 한국어를 가르쳐드릴게요.** / I will teach you Korean (using the humble 드리다).

---

### Asking Intentions with ~게요
The second form is constructed by simply adding 게 or 게요 directly to an action verb stem, such as 하게 or 하게요. This form is used to ask for more information about someone's intentions after they have already stated something. It functions similarly to the ~(으)려고 form, meaning "Are you intending to...?" or "Are you planning on...?"

The instructor points out that this form is frequently used with honorifics. Instead of 하게요, you will often hear 하시게요 (from 하시다). This is used to follow up on a situation, such as asking someone who looks ready for bed, "지금 주무시게요?" (Are you planning to sleep now?). Another example provided is "공부는 언제 하게?" (When are you planning to study?). It is a way of seeking clarification on the "why" or "when" of someone's stated plans.

---

### Playful Guessing and Archaic Commands
The ~게 ending has a specific casual and playful usage as a statement to ask someone to take a guess. The most common example is 누구게? (Guess who?), where the verb 이다 (to be) is shortened because the preceding syllable ends in a vowel. This can also be used in the past tense, such as 뭐 샀게? (Guess what I bought?).

Additionally, the instructor touches on historical or archaic usage often seen in 사극 (historical dramas). In this context, ~게 or ~게나 functions as a friendly, casual command form used by an older person to a younger person. For example, 하게 means "do it" and 가게 means "go," but this is not used in modern daily conversation.

---

### Agreement with 그러게요
Finally, the lesson mentions the fixed expression 그러게요. While it shares the same ending, it functions as a standalone phrase meaning "Yes, I agree" or "That's what I'm saying." It is used as a reactive backchannel to show strong agreement with what the other person just said.
