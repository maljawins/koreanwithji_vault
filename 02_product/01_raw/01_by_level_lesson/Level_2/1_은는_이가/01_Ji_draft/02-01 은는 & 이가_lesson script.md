**은/는 and 이/가 Overview**

Now, 은/는 and 이/가 are somewhat "interchangeable" in the sense that your sentence would be grammatically "correct" with either one.

But they do not have the same meaning.

For example,

리사**는** 미국 사람이에요

리사**가** 미국 사람이에요

Both sentences mean “Lisa is American”.

The first one focuses on the information ABOUT Lisa. So, this answers “Where is Lisa from?”

But the second sentence emphasizes **LISA**. In English, you would say it like “***LISA***is American”, like “SHE’s the one who is American.” So, this answers “Who is American?”

Of course, this is an over-simplified explanation and there is so much more to it.

**Terminology**

First of all, let’s establish a clear terminology to avoid confusion.

Some people call **은/는** the **"topic particle"** and **이/가** the **"subject particle."**

[Show Ven diagram]

Now, **은/는** can be used for pretty much any word in a sentence, including the subject, time, location, object, adverbs and even certain particles.

But **이/가** can only be used to mark the **subject,** specifically the subject of **the verb**. I’ll explain what I mean by this later.

But you can see how calling 이/가 the “subject particle” can sound vague and misleading since both **은/는** and **이/가** can be placed after “the subject.”

So, we’re going to use the word “marker” and **call** **은/는** the **"topic marker"** and **이/가** the **"subject marker."**

**Contractions**

Let’s go over some technicalities now.

Both 은/는 and 이/가 are 받침-sensitive.

For topic marker, 은 comes after a 받침, and 는 after a vowel.

And for subject marker, it’s 이 after a 받침, and 가 after a vowel.

What I haven’t showed you is what they look like when contracted. So, here it is.

는 turns into a ㄴ 받침 when it comes after a noun that ends in a vowel.

So, 나는 becomes 난. 저는 becomes 전. 우리는 becomes 우린. 너는 becomes 넌 이거는 becomes 이건.

This also works for regular nouns like 친구는 becomes 친군 엄마는 becomes 엄만 so on and so forth.

But you can’t use contractions for regular nouns in writing. But it’s okay to write the contracted versions for pronouns.

Moving on to the subject marker.

When 가 comes after certain pronouns, it changes a bit.

So, for “I”,

나 becomes 내가, not 나가.

And the humble “I” 저 becomes 제가, not 저가.

And for 너 the casual “you”, it’s not 너가, it’s 네가. – And this one is pronounced 니가 to avoid confusion with 내가. 한글 is usually pronounced as they’re written but this one’s an exception to remember.

Also, “것이” with ‘것 - thing” and “이” combined is shortened to “게”

So, 이것이 becomes 이게 그것이 becomes 그게 and 저것이 becomes 저게

**Honorific versions**

These markers also have **honorific versions**, but they’re **not always necessary** because you can always use the **honorific verb** to show respect instead.

But this is just a little something to keep in mind

So, the subject marker 이/가 in honorific is 께서 and the topic marker 은/는 in honorific is 께서는. And no, these are not 받침-sensitive.

For example, 할머니가 would be 할머니께서 and 할머니는 would be 할머니께서는

**Definitions & Difference 01**

Now, the term "topic marker" and "subject marker" themselves can be confusing since the word “topic” and “subject” are used synonymously in English.

So, the very first thing I want you to do is forget about the concept of the grammar term "subject" as you know it.

Otherwise, it will be really difficult to understand the topic marker and the subject marker.

If you look at the example sentence we used in the "A is NOT B" lesson—이 사람은 제 친구가 아니에요—you can see both 은 and 가, so it might look like there are two subjects: 이 사람 and 제 친구.

And understandably, many people can't wrap their head around this, like "How can there be two subjects in one sentence?" And that’s because these are not the same concept as the subject of a sentence as you know it.

I know this sounds confusing, but I'm going to need you to bear with me here because I promise you'll see what I mean by this when I get to the subject marker.

And this sentence structure is extremely common in Korean. And I call it the TSV sentence structure: Topic, Subject (of the Verb), Verb.

Everyone talks about the SOV structure, but not enough about this one. So, we'll talk about it in our next lesson in detail.

[Show: 나는 네가 좋아 - Literally: "(As for) me, you're good" - Naturally: "I like you"]

So again, it's better to completely forget about the concept of the "subject" in your English-thinking brain and start thinking in terms of **topic of the sentence** and **subject of the verb.**

Let's first find out where their names—topic marker and subject marker—come from and talk about the FIRST difference.

**은/는 The topic marker**

“What about me?” in Korean is “저***는***요?”

저, “Me” is the topic being talked about. This is why it’s called the topic marker.

Go Billy Korean on YouTube explained this beautifully using this analogy.

Imagine there’s a sign over your heads and on it, is the topic of the ongoing conversation.

Now, when you say “저는”, you’re essentially writing “저 – me” on this imaginary signboard letting everyone know “I’m going to say something about me.” So as soon as people hear you say “저는", they know that whatever you say next will be about “저 – me”

The closest thing we have in English would be phrases like “[As for]," "[Regarding]," "[When it comes to]," or “[About noun]”

[Show “**리사는 미국사람이에요”]**

So, this has a similar nuance to “As for Lisa, she’s American,”. So naturally, “는” highlights the information ABOUT Lisa - the fact that she’s American.

And after saying “리사는” once, you don’t have to repeat “리사는” in your next sentence. In fact, it would be unnatural to do that every time. Because remember Lisa is still written on the signboard. So, everyone knows you’re talking about Lisa until you bring up a new topic.

This is exactly the same idea as how you’d replace Lisa with “she” in English.

But Korean omits the entire “topic” unless you wanna bring up something else to talk about.

이렇게 주어가 생략되는 경우가 많기 때문에 누구 혹은 무엇에 대해서 얘기를 하는 건지 context를 읽는 능력이 요구된다. 이건 문화적으로 “눈치 – nunchi” 라는 것을 요구하는데 topic marker의 존재가 한국어가 high-context language라는 것에 기여하는 바가 굉장히 크다.

So for our next sentence, we can omit ”리사는“ say “그리고 제 친구예요.” – And (She) is my friend.

Now let's look at the example sentence from earlier:

**이 사람은 제 친구가 아니에요.**

"(As for) this person, (he is) not my friend."

So as for 이 사람, we're talking about this person and the rest is **information about** 이 사람—the fact that this person is not my friend.

And then in the next sentence, you can follow up with something like 제 삼촌이에요, meaning "(he) is my uncle." and omit “이 사람은” to avoid redundancy.

To sum up, 은/는 marks the topic of the sentence, who or what it’s about, like “As for” and highlights the **information about the topic of your sentence**.

And once it’s been brought up once, you don’t have to repeat the topic which works kind of like replacing that noun with a pronoun in English.

[Show Lisa → She / Uncle → He]

**이/가 The subject marker**

Let’s talk about the subject marker. Now, what 이/가 does is it shows you plain-and-simple that this noun is the ***subject of the verb*** that is, the thing that DOES or IS and nothing more.

**Again, this is NOT the same thing as the “subject of a sentence” as you know it.**

Here's what I mean. Let's compare English and Korean.

**Show: This person is not my friend.**

In this English sentence, "this person" is the subject, and "my friend" is something called “the complement” which refers to the word that **completes the meaning of a sentence.**

**Show: 이 사람은 제 친구가 아니에요.**

However, what completes a Korean sentence is always the verb at the end. This makes it structurally necessary to mark the role of this word - 제 친구

“이 사람은 아니에요.” – This person isn’t.

Let’s say you heard someone say this to you out of context, you’d obviously wonder “What is this person not?” Namely, “what is **the subject of the verb** 아니다?”

In this case, that would be 제 친구 since it corresponds to **who this person is NOT.**

This is where 이/가 comes in. And this is why it’s called **the subject marker** because all it does is it shows you who or what the subject of the verb is.

This is what I mean by “the subject of the verb”. And you can now see why I said forget about the concept of subject in English grammar earlier.

And you can apply the same logic to our very first example sentence “리사**가** 미국 사람이에요”, you can now see that 가 is marking ***who it is*** that’s American. So, “Who is American? – **Lisa** is”

Simply put, all you need to do is think about who, what or which that does or is.

**Difference 2**

We just learned what fundamentally makes the topic marker and subject marker different.

As an extension of this, let me explain the second functional difference between the two markers.

We now know that the topic marker highlights the predicate—the information about the topic.

And because of this function, it is used for general statements or stating facts.

For example, 사과**는** 빨개요. Apples are red. This is a fact.

Now if you were to say “사과**가** 빨개요”, this would have a closer meaning to “The apple is red”, talking about one specific apple.

Another example,

고양이**는** 귀여워요. Cats are cute

고양이**가** 귀여워요. The cat is cute

The first one is stating a general fact whereas the second one has more focus on 고양이 like THE cat. So, “고양이가 귀여워요” is something you can say to a cat owner as a compliment like “This specific cat, namely your cat, is cute.”

**Difference 3**

Now let's talk about the third difference.

We usually use 은/는 when the subject has already been introduced in a previous sentence.

Of course, as I mentioned earlier, you can omit the topic because it’s already been mentioned. But even when it's omitted, the meaning of the topic marker is still there.

For example, when someone asks you “연필은 어디 있어요?” Where is the pencil? If you know the answer right away, you could omit (연필은) and say “책상위에 있어요” – It’s on the desk.

But when you have to think about it, you could start by saying “연필은… The pencil is…” then “아! 책상위에 있어요.”

This is how we use the topic marker for things that have already been mentioned once even when it’s omitted.

But we typically use the subject marker when introducing new information.

Let’s say you met someone for the first time. And you guys are talking about music. But then he asks you, “성함이 어떻게 되세요?” – By the way, what is (your) name?

[Show: We learned this phrase in the lesson “A is B”]

You guys were talking about music before, but then all of a sudden, he brought up “성함 - the name”.

Friendly reminder by the way, 성함 is honorific of 이름 and you’d never use 성함 to refer to your own name.

Now, 성함 is a new information which is why it’s marked with the subject marker 이.

And now that the name has been mentioned, you would answer with “제 이름**은** 지민이에요.” Like “Oh as for my name, it’s Jimin.

Or you could drop “제 이름은” and say “지민이예요” but still, the meaning of “제 이름은” is included in your response.

So, ask yourself these questions:

1. Has it been mentioned before?
2. Do we both know what it is?

If yes, it’s usually 은/는 And if not and you’re bringing it up for the first time, it’s usually 이/가.

**Difference 4**

The fourth difference is that the topic marker implies contrast whereas the subject marker doesn’t.

You might be thinking “What does a topic have anything to do with contrast?”

Well, for this one, you have to “FEEL” it.

Keep this phrase in mind [Well, about noun].

Let’s say you called your brother and asked him to get you some beer on his way home.

And now he’s home.

So, you ask him “Did you get the beer?” And he goes “Well, about that,”

Stop right there.

The second you hear this, you know immediately he didn’t get the beer before he finishes his sentence.

THIS is exactly the feeling you get with this usage of the topic marker.

So, he might say something like “They didn’t have any, so I got soju instead”.

Let’s have this conversation in Korean.

You: 오빠 (형), 맥주는? – Big brother, what about the beer?

Brother: 아, 맥주**는** 없었어. – Well, (about the beer) they didn’t have it

Brother: 그래서 소주 사왔어 - so I bought soju.

Now, you can see how the topic marker implies contrast.

Because of this, it's also frequently used when comparing two different things.

Let’s look at another example.

저는 치킨을 좋아하고 오빠는 소고기를 좋아해요

(As for me) I like chicken and (as for my brother), he likes beef.

As you can see, 은/는 is showing contrast between what I like and what my brother likes by making comparisons.

But if you were to say “제가 치킨을 좋아하고 오빠가 소고기를 좋아해요”, it would sound like someone got confused about who likes chicken and who likes beef.

And you want to correct them and go “No, I’m the who likes chicken and my brother’s the one who likes beef.”

Again, remember the feeling of [Well, about noun]

**은/는 with Other Sentence Elements**

As I mentioned at the beginning of this lesson, 은/는 can be attached after time, location, object, adverbs, and even other particles.

When this happens, it's also used to show contrast, just like we learned.

Let's look at the example sentences from the lesson "SOV Sentence Structure":

저는 동물을 좋아해요.

저는 벌레를 싫어해요.

By replacing the object marker with the topic marker, you can emphasize the contrast like this:

**저는 동물은 좋아해요. 하지만 벌레를 싫어해요.**

"[Well, about animals, I like THEM], but I don't like bugs."

Or:

**저는 동물을 좋아해요. 하지만 벌레는 싫어해요.**

"I like animals. [But about BUGS, I don't like them]."

**이/가 – Embedded sentences**

Lastly, this is a bit more advanced but I thought I’d show it to you anyway just so you can come back later for review.

Now, an embedded sentence is something that looks this - Show: Grape is a fruit that I like, where we have a sentence within a sentence just like “that I like” is embedded to modify “a fruit”

Show: 포도는 제가 좋아하는 과일이에요.

In Korean, “제가 좋아하는” is the part that corresponds to “that I like” and we’ll learn all about them in level 4.

And since we have two verbs now [좋아하다] and [이다], we need to mark who it is that likes the fruit. And thus we get “제가” where 가 shows us that “I” am the subject of the verb “좋아하다”.
