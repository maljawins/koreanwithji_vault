## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces the particles **은/는** and **이/가**, which are notoriously difficult for Korean learners to master. In Korean, particles are words attached after a noun to indicate its grammatical role. Unlike English prepositions that come before a noun, Korean particles are "post-positions." For example, in the sentence **저는 학교에 가요** (I go to school), **에** is the particle equivalent to "to," and it follows the noun **학교** (school). The instructor emphasizes that while these particles are sometimes removable in context, they play a critical role in deciding the general meaning and nuance of a sentence. The lesson is structured to first cover the mechanical rules of conjugation based on final consonants (받침) and then delve into the deep pragmatic differences between the two sets of markers through extensive example sentences and quizzes.

---

### Basic Conjugation Rules

The choice between the two forms within each set depends entirely on whether the preceding noun ends in a final consonant (받침) or a vowel. For the first set, **은** is used after a consonant, and **는** is used after a vowel. For example, **이것** (this) ends in a consonant, so it becomes **이것은**. Conversely, **저** (I) ends in a vowel, resulting in **저는**.

For the second set, **이** follows a consonant, and **가** follows a vowel. Thus, **이것** becomes **이것이**, while **저** becomes **제가**. The instructor highlights a crucial irregular change: when the particle **가** is attached to the first-person pronouns **저** (formal I) or **나** (informal I), or the second-person pronoun **너** (informal you), the stems change their form.

- **저 + 가** becomes **제가** (never **저가**)
- **나 + 가** becomes **내가** (never **나가**)
- **너 + 가** becomes **네가** (often pronounced as **니가** in spoken Korean to distinguish it from **내가**)

---

### Usage 1: General Statements vs. Specific Emphasis

The first major distinction involves the type of information being conveyed. The instructor labels **은/는** as the "first subject marker" and **이/가** as the "second subject marker" for convenience. **은/는** is typically used for general statements or stating facts. For instance, **제 이름은 비키입니다** (My name is Vicky) uses **은** because it is a general fact about the speaker. Similarly, **저는 한국인입니다** (I am Korean) uses **는**.

In contrast, **이/가** is used to put emphasis on the subject itself, often answering a "who" question. If someone asks **누구의 이름이 비키인가요?** (Whose name is Vicky?), the response **제 이름이 비키입니다** uses **이** to emphasize that it is *my* name specifically that is Vicky. The instructor uses an example of Jin from BTS saying **저는 잘생겼습니다** (I am handsome) to show that he is stating his handsomeness as an established general fact.

---

### Usage 2: Follow-up Information and Sentence Flow

The second subject marker **이/가** is used when there is more to say about the subject or when the sentence provides a reason for a follow-up action. The instructor provides a scenario where someone is asked to call Jenny. If the reply is **제니가 자요. 그래서 못해요** (Jenny is sleeping, so I can't), **가** is used because the fact that Jenny is sleeping is the reason for the next sentence. 

When telling a story, the first mention of a subject often uses **이/가** to introduce it into the context, especially if follow-up details are coming. For example, starting a story with **내가 마트에 갔어** (I went to a mart...) suggests the speaker is about to describe what happened there. Using **나는 마트에 갔어** would feel like a simple, isolated statement of fact.

---

### Usage 3: Main Subject vs. Subordinate Subject

In complex sentences with multiple subjects, **은/는** marks the main subject of the entire sentence, while **이/가** marks the subject of a subordinate or minor clause. The instructor analyzes the sentence **나는 네가 행복하길 바래** (I hope that you are happy). Here, **나** (I) is the main subject doing the "hoping," so it takes **는**. The person who is "happy" (**너**) is part of the subordinate clause, so it takes **가** (becoming **네가**). This hierarchy helps listeners track which subject is performing the primary action of the sentence.

---

### Usage 4: Contrast and Fixed Expressions

A primary function of **은/는** is to show contrast, even when it might otherwise be the subject of a specific action. If a speaker says **나는 널 응원해** (I cheer for you) in a context where others do not, the **는** implies "unlike others, *I* cheer for you." The instructor demonstrates this with a breakfast conversation: Person A says **저는 아침을 먹었어요** (I had breakfast), Person B says **저도 먹었어요** (I ate too), and Person C says **저는 안 먹었어요** (I didn't eat). Person C uses **는** to highlight that their situation is different from A and B.

Additionally, certain verbs are traditionally paired with **이/가** in general statements. The most common are **있어요** (to have/there is) and **없어요** (to not have/there isn't). While **연필이 있어요** is the standard way to say "There is a pencil," changing it to **연필은 있어요** immediately adds a nuance of contrast, implying "I have a pencil (but I don't have other things you might be looking for)."
