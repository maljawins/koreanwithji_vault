## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor addresses one of the most notorious and confusing topics for Korean learners: the distinction between the topic markers 은/는 and the subject markers 이/가. She frames this lesson as a "rite of passage" for both teachers and students, acknowledging that while the two sets of particles are often interchangeable in a basic grammatical sense, they carry vastly different nuances and contextual meanings. The difficulty arises because Korean is a highly contextual language where these particles are frequently omitted in casual conversation, yet they remain essential for precise communication.

The core of the confusion lies in the fact that 은/는 and 이/가 are "apples and oranges"—they both function within the realm of subjects, but their pragmatic roles are distinct. While 이/가 strictly marks the grammatical subject of a sentence, 은/는 is more versatile, acting as a "topic marker" that can attach to subjects, objects, or adverbs to add emphasis, contrast, or special meaning. The instructor breaks down these differences into six specific categories, ranging from basic conjugation rules to advanced grammatical restrictions.

---

### Basic Conjugation and Contractions
Before diving into usage, the instructor establishes the technical rules for choosing between the particles within each pair. For the topic markers 은/는, the choice depends on the final sound of the preceding word: words ending in a consonant (받침) are followed by 은, such as 한국은 (Korea) or 가족은 (family), while words ending in a vowel are followed by 는, such as 우리는 (we) or 엄마는 (mom). Similarly, for subject markers, 이 follows a consonant (한국이, 가족이) and 가 follows a vowel (우리가, 엄마가).

In casual spoken Korean, these particles are often contracted. The instructor notes that 는 frequently shrinks to a final ㄴ sound: 나는 becomes 난, 우리는 becomes 우린, and 엄마는 becomes 엄만. Furthermore, when the subject marker 가 follows the first-person pronouns 나 or 저, the spelling of the pronoun itself changes: 나 + 가 becomes 내가, and 저 + 가 becomes 제가.

---

### Difference 1: New Information vs. Known Information
The first major distinction concerns the introduction of information. 이/가 is typically used to introduce a subject for the first time or to present new information. For example, saying 연필이 책상 위에 있어요 (There is a pencil on the desk) suggests the speaker just noticed the pencil or is informing the listener of its existence for the first time. Similarly, asking 이름이 뭐예요? (What is your name?) uses 이 because the name is unknown information.

Conversely, 은/는 is used when the subject has already been mentioned or is known to both the speaker and the listener. If the conversation is already about a specific pencil, one would say 연필은 책상 위에 있어요 (As for the pencil, it is on the desk). When answering a name inquiry, a speaker says 제 이름은 샘물이에요 (My name is Saemmul) because the "name" was already established as the topic by the questioner.

---

### Difference 2: Focus on Subject vs. Focus on Predicate
The second difference involves where the emphasis of the sentence lies. When using 은/는, the focus is on the predicate (what is being said about the subject). When using 이/가, the focus is on the subject itself. The instructor uses the example "Apples are red" to illustrate: if "apple" is the subject and "are red" is the predicate, 은/는 emphasizes the "redness," while 이/가 emphasizes that it is the "apple" (and not some other fruit) that is red.

This is clearly seen in introductions. 저는 샘물이에요 (I am Saemmul) focuses on the identity "Saemmul." However, 제가 샘물이에요 (I am the one who is Saemmul) focuses on "I," perhaps in response to someone asking "Who here is Saemmul?" The instructor provides further examples with competition results: 영희는 3등 했어요 (Young-hee won third place) focuses on the result, whereas 영희가 3등 했어요 (Young-hee is the one who won third place) emphasizes that it was Young-hee specifically who achieved that rank.

---

### Difference 3: Contrast and Comparison
The topic markers 은/는 are specifically used to show contrast or make comparisons between two things. The instructor provides the sentence: 저는 피자를 좋아하고 오빠는 햄버거를 좋아해요 (I like pizza, and [in contrast] my brother likes hamburgers). Using 이/가 in this context would sound awkward unless the speaker was specifically trying to correct someone's confusion about who likes what. For general comparisons of different traits or preferences, 은/는 is the standard choice.

---

### Difference 4: General and Universal Truths
은/는 is the required marker for stating general facts, definitions, or universal truths. Examples shown on screen include 사람은 모두 죽어요 (All people die) and 인생은 짧아요 (Life is short). Because these statements describe the nature of the topic in a broad, timeless sense rather than a specific, one-time event, the topic marker is used. 이/가 is rarely used for these types of universal declarations.

---

### Difference 5: Grammatical Versatility and Emphasis
A critical functional difference is that 이/가 is strictly a subject marker and can only follow the subject of a sentence. 은/는, however, can "jump around" and attach to various parts of a sentence to add nuance. It can replace an object marker to add a sense of "at least" or "only this": 피아노는 칠 줄 알아요 (I can [at least] play the piano) implies the speaker might not play other instruments.

It can also follow adverbs or other particles for emphasis. For instance, adding 는 to the adverb "fast" in 제가 빨리는 못 뛰어요 (I can't run [that] fast) suggests the speaker can run, but speed is the issue. It can even follow the location particle 에서, as in 실내에서는 신발을 벗어주세요 (Please take your shoes off [specifically] indoors), emphasizing that while shoes might be okay elsewhere, they must be removed inside.

---

### Difference 6: Embedded Clauses
The final distinction is a technical grammatical rule regarding complex sentences. In an embedded clause (a sentence within a sentence), the subject of that inner clause must be marked with 이/가, never 은/는. The instructor uses the example: 딸기는 제가 좋아하는 과일이에요 (Strawberries are the fruit that I like). In this sentence, "strawberry" is the main topic (딸기는), but the subject of the embedded clause "I like" must be 제가, not 저는.
