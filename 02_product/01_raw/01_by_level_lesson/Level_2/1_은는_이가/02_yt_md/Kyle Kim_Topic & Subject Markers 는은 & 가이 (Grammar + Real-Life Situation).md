### Overview
The instructor addresses one of the most common hurdles for Korean learners: the distinction between topic markers (은/는) and subject markers (이/가). The lesson is divided into two primary sections. The first section covers the mechanical grammar rules—specifically how to choose the correct particle based on the final sound of a noun. The second section focuses on the pragmatic context, explaining how the choice of particle changes the meaning or emphasis of a sentence depending on the situation.

The core of the pedagogical approach relies on contrasting "general facts" with "specific instances" and "behavioral focus" with "subject focus." This narrative allows students to see how markers shift naturally during a conversation to indicate contrast, answer specific questions, or redirect the focus of a story.

### Basic Grammar Rules: Vowels vs. Consonants
The choice between the two versions of each marker is determined entirely by whether the preceding noun ends in a vowel or a consonant. For topic markers, nouns ending in a vowel take **는**, while nouns ending in a consonant take **은**. The instructor illustrates this with the names Sarah and Megan. Since **사라 (Sarah)** ends in a vowel, it becomes **사라는**. Since **메간 (Megan)** ends in a consonant, it becomes **메간은**.

The subject markers follow an identical structural logic. Nouns ending in a vowel take **가**, and nouns ending in a consonant take **이**. Using the same names, the instructor demonstrates the transformation: **사라** becomes **사라가**, and **메간** becomes **메간이**. A summary chart is provided on screen to help students visualize these four combinations:

- **Topic Particles:** Vowel + **는** / Consonant + **은**
- **Subject Particles:** Vowel + **가** / Consonant + **이**

---

### Irregular First-Person Pronouns
A critical grammatical exception occurs with the first-person pronoun "I." The instructor explains that the word for "I" changes its base form depending on whether it is a topic or a subject. When used as a topic, the word is **저**, which combines with the vowel-rule marker to become **저는**. However, when used as a subject, the base form changes to **제**, which combines with the vowel-rule marker to become **제가**. Students are urged to memorize these two forms—**저는** and **제가**—as they are foundational to nearly every introductory Korean sentence.

---

### General Facts vs. Specific Instances
The first contextual difference between the markers involves the scope of the statement. Topic markers (**은/는**) are used when stating general, widely known facts. For example, to say "Lions are big" as a general characteristic of the species, one says **사자는 커요**. 

In contrast, subject markers (**이/가**) are used to describe a specific thing or a unique situation currently being observed. If a person is at a zoo looking at one particular lion that happens to be small, they would say **사자가 작아요**. The instructor provides a second example with bananas: **바나나는 노란색이에요 (Bananas are yellow)** describes the general nature of the fruit, whereas **바나나가 빨간색이에요 (The banana is red)** describes a specific, unusual banana the speaker is looking at right now.

---

### Behavioral Focus vs. Subject Focus
The second contextual rule concerns what part of the sentence the speaker wants to emphasize. Topic markers focus on the behavior or the action being performed. If someone asks, "What is the child doing?" the focus is on the activity, so the response is **아이는 노래 부르고 있어요 (The child is singing)**.

Subject markers focus on the "who"—the identity of the person performing the action. If someone asks, "Who is singing?" the focus shifts to the subject, resulting in the response **아이가 노래 부르고 있어요 (The child is singing)**. While both sentences translate to the same English sentence, the Korean particles signal whether the speaker is highlighting the act of singing or the specific child who is doing it. This is further demonstrated with the example of learning Korean: **저는 한국어를 배우고 있어요** answers "What are you doing?" while **제가 한국어를 배우고 있어요** answers "Who is learning Korean?"

---

### Contrast and Transition
Topic markers are also used to indicate a contrast or a transition within a conversation. This often translates to "as for [me/this]" in English. The instructor uses a scenario with three people: the first two people say they ate apples, but the third person, who ate a banana, says **저는 바나나 먹었어요**. By using **는**, the third person signals a shift in the topic from apples to their own specific situation. This same logic applies to transitions in introductions; if a group of people are stating their home countries, each person after the first might use **저는** to signal "now, as for me, I am from [Country]."

---

### Real-Life Application: The Candy Mystery
To synthesize all these rules, the instructor presents a story: a piece of Valentine's Day candy (**사탕**) is stolen from a desk. The speaker asks the office, **누가 제 사탕 먹었어요? (Who ate my candy?)**. Because the question asks "who," the suspect Mina answers with a subject marker: **제가 먹었어요 (I ate it)**. 

Later, another suspect, Martin, wants to contrast himself with Mina's confession. He uses a topic marker to signal this transition: **저는 안 먹었어요 (As for me, I didn't eat it)**. When the speaker asks the boss about his behavior (**사장님은 뭐 하고 있었어요?**), the boss responds with a topic marker to focus on his action: **저는 일하고 있었어요 (I was working)**. Finally, when an intern points out the true culprit, he uses a subject marker to identify the "who": **사장님이 사탕 먹었어요 (The boss ate the candy)**. The boss finally confesses using the subject marker to emphasize his identity as the eater: **제가 사탕 먹었어요**.
