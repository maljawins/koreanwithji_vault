## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor, Hyunwoo, addresses a common point of confusion for Korean learners: the difference between verb pairs ending in **하다** and **되다**. Using the specific example of **걱정하다** and **걱정되다**, he explains that while both words share the same root noun **걱정 (worry/concern)**, they convey different nuances regarding the speaker's agency and the nature of the emotion.

The lesson arc moves from the fundamental definitions of **하다 (to do)** and **되다 (to be done/to become)** to practical applications in daily speech. By the end of the video, learners should understand how to choose between active participation and passive experience when expressing emotions like worry, nervousness, or anticipation.

---

### Active vs. Passive: The Core Difference
The fundamental distinction lies in the meaning of the suffixes themselves. **하다** means "to do," which implies an active participation or a choice to act upon a feeling. When you use **걱정하다**, you are actively "doing" the worrying. Conversely, **되다** often means "to be done" or "to become." Therefore, **걱정되다** functions as a passive form, meaning "to be made to worry" or to simply feel worried because of an external factor.

The instructor illustrates this with a simple comparison of completion: **숙제 다 했어요** means "I did all my homework" (active), whereas **다 됐어요** means "It is all done" (passive state). This same logic applies to emotional verbs. If an emotion is happening to you without your conscious effort, **되다** is usually the more natural choice.

---

### Imperatives and Natural Phrasing
Because **하다** is active, it can be used in negative imperatives (commands). You can say **걱정하지 마세요 (Don't worry)** because you are telling someone to stop the "action" of worrying. However, you cannot say **걱정되지 마세요** because you cannot command someone to stop a passive feeling that is being "done" to them by a situation.

When describing a situation or an incident that causes worry, **되다** is required. The instructor highlights the phrase **걱정되는 일이 있어요 (There is something that worries me)**. Using **걱정하는** here would imply that the "work" or "situation" itself is performing the act of worrying, which is logically impossible. If you see someone looking frustrated, the natural question is **무슨 걱정되는 일이라도 있어요? (Is there something worrying you?)**.

---

### Application to Other Verb Pairs
The instructor extends this logic to other common pairs: **긴장하다/긴장되다 (to be nervous)** and **기대하다/기대되다 (to look forward to)**.

For nervousness, **긴장돼요** is used when the feeling arises naturally, such as before a performance. **긴장하지 마** is the command to stop being nervous. Interestingly, **긴장해요** can be used to describe a general tendency or habit, such as **저는 면접 볼 때마다 긴장해요 (I tend to get nervous every time I have a job interview)**.

For anticipation, **기대되다** is the standard way to express excitement about something external, like a new movie. You would say **기대되는 영화 (a movie I'm excited about)**. **기대하다** is used when the focus is strictly on the person doing the expecting, often requiring a subject marker: **제가 기대하는 여행 (the trip that I am looking forward to)**. Using **기대되는** with a subject marker like **제가** is unnatural because the passive form doesn't align with a specific active agent.
