## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces the verb **되다**, noting that while it has many nuances, it possesses three primary uses that are essential for learners to master. The lesson transitions from the most literal dictionary definition to more pragmatic, conversational applications where the verb functions as a standalone response or a component of specific grammar patterns. The instructor uses a clear visual style, writing each meaning and its corresponding examples on a transparent board to demonstrate how the verb adapts to different contexts.

---

### Meaning 1: To Become

The most standard and common usage of **되다** is "to become." This is the definition most students encounter first when learning the verb. The instructor illustrates this with a sentence expressing a personal goal or desire. By combining the noun for teacher with the appropriate particle and the "want to" grammar pattern, he creates a complete thought:

> **선생님이 되고 싶어요.** / I want to become a teacher.

In this context, the verb functions as a process of transformation or reaching a certain status.

---

### Meaning 2: To Work (Functionality)

The second major use of **되다** refers to functionality—whether something "works" or "does not work." This is frequently used when discussing technology, appliances, or the success of a specific action. The instructor demonstrates how to negate this meaning using **안** (not) to indicate a malfunction.

> **안 돼요.** / It doesn't work.

This can be specified by adding a subject. For instance, if a computer is broken, one would say:

> **컴퓨터가 안 돼요.** / The computer doesn't work.

Conversely, if something is functioning well, the adverb **잘** (well) is added:

> **잘 돼요.** / It works well.
> **컴퓨터가 잘 돼요.** / The computer works well.

The instructor notes that you can also use the verb as a simple question, **돼요?**, to ask "Does it work?"

---

### Meaning 3: Okay, Acceptable, or Available

The third usage is perhaps the most versatile in daily conversation, where **되다** translates to "okay," "acceptable," or "possible." In this sense, **안 돼요** takes on a different nuance depending on the context; instead of meaning something is broken, it means "It's not okay" or "You shouldn't do that." This is the common expression used to tell someone to stop an action or to say "no" to a request.

This meaning also extends to checking someone's availability. The instructor provides a common phrase for asking if someone has free time:

> **지금 시간 돼요?** / Do you have time now? (Literally: Is time okay now?)

If the person is available, they can simply respond with **돼요** (It's okay / I'm available).

Finally, the instructor connects this to the permission grammar pattern **-어도 되다**. This pattern literally asks if it is "okay even if" an action is performed. He provides examples of asking for permission to do something or to go somewhere:

> **해도 돼요?** / Is it okay if I do it? / Can I do it?
> **가도 돼요?** / Is it okay if I go? / Can I go?
> **저도 가도 돼요?** / Is it okay even if I go as well? / Can I go too?
