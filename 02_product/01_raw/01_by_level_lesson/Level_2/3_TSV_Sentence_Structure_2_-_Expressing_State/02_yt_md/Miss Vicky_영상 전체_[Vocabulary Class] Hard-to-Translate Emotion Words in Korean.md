## Teaching Points

### Overview
This lesson focuses on twelve specific Korean emotion adjectives that are notoriously difficult to translate directly into English. While common words like "happy" or "sad" have clear equivalents, the words covered in this class carry unique cultural nuances and situational meanings that require an understanding of context and intuition. The instructor emphasizes that these words describe specific feelings or "vibes" that are often left un-verbalized in other languages.

The instructor frames the lesson around the concepts of **기분 (mood/feeling)** and **감정 (emotion)**. Before diving into the vocabulary, a critical grammatical foundation is laid regarding the **-서 conjunction**. This conjunction, derived from **그래서 (so)**, is used to link a cause to an effect within a single sentence. Crucially, it must be attached to the present tense verb stem (e.g., **슬퍼요** becomes **슬퍼서**) rather than the base dictionary stem. This structure is used throughout the example sentences to explain *why* a certain emotion is felt.

---

### 답답하다: Feeling Stuck or Suffocated
The first word, **답답하다**, describes a feeling of being stuck, frustrated, or suffocated because one cannot find a way out of a situation or cannot communicate effectively. A common example is feeling **답답해** because of the COVID-19 pandemic restrictions. It also applies to language barriers; if you cannot get your message across in Korean, you feel **답답해요**.

The instructor introduces a vivid metaphor for this feeling: **고구마 100개 먹은 기분 (the feeling of eating 100 sweet potatoes)**. Just as eating many dry sweet potatoes without water makes your chest feel tight and stuffed, a frustrating situation makes you feel **답답하다**. This word can also describe a person who is stubborn or slow to understand, referred to as a **답답한 사람**.

---

### 민망하다 vs. 창피하다: Embarrassment and Awkwardness
**민망하다** means to feel embarrassed to the point of not knowing what to do. While it overlaps with **창피하다 (to be ashamed/embarrassed)**, **민망하다** is often used for socially awkward situations that aren't necessarily your fault. For example, seeing a **야한 장면 (sexual/suggestive scene)** in a movie while watching with your parents is a classic **민망한** situation.

The instructor notes that **야하다** describes something showy or suggestive. If someone wears clothes that are "too **야해**," the person looking at them might feel **민망해**. It is the feeling of wanting to look away because a situation is too revealing or socially uncomfortable.

---

### 뻘쭘하다: The Slang for Awkwardness
**뻘쭘하다** is a common slang term (often not found in standard dictionaries) that means to feel awkward and out of place. It is similar to **어색하다 (to be awkward)**, but carries a stronger nuance of not knowing what to do with oneself. A typical situation is being left alone with a **모르는 사람 (stranger)**; the resulting silence makes you feel **뻘쭘해요**.

---

### 섭섭하다, 서운하다, and 아쉽다: Degrees of Disappointment
These three words all deal with disappointment but target different subjects. **섭섭하다** is a light disappointment or a feeling of "I wish it were otherwise." It is often used when a friend doesn't show up to a party (**제프가 안 와서 섭섭해요**).

**서운하다** is very similar to **섭섭하다** but is more specifically directed at a person's actions (or lack thereof). If your mother does something that hurts your feelings, you feel **서운해요**. It is a useful word for heart-to-heart conversations to express that your feelings were slightly hurt by someone close to you.

**아쉽다** is used when you feel sorry about a *situation* rather than a person. If a fun festival ends, you say **축제가 끝나서 아쉬워요 (It's a pity/shame the festival ended)**. The instructor warns never to use **아쉽다** in serious situations like a death; in those cases, use **유감 (sorrow/regret)**, as in **유감입니다 (I am sorry for your loss)**.

---

### 시원하다 and 후련하다: The "Refreshing" Release
**시원하다** literally means "cool" (like a summer breeze), but as an emotion, it describes the feeling of a burden or grudge being lifted. **후련하다** is a synonym used specifically for this emotional release. If you finally tell someone a secret you've been keeping, you feel **속이 시원해요 (My inside feels refreshed/relieved)**.

The instructor introduces the slang metaphor **사이다 (Cider/Sprite)**. Because drinking a cold lemon-lime soda is refreshing, a person who says exactly what everyone else was thinking (but was too afraid to say) is said to have made a **사이다 발언 (Cider statement)**. This is the opposite of the "100 sweet potatoes" (**고구마**) feeling.

---

### 시원섭섭하다: Bittersweet Mixed Feelings
This is a combination of **시원하다** and **섭섭하다**. It describes the mixed feeling of being relieved that something is over, but sad to see it end. Celebrities often use this during interviews when a long drama filming finishes (**드라마 촬영이 끝나서 시원섭섭해요**). They are happy to rest, but they will miss the cast and crew.

---

### 속상하다: Feeling Upset or Hurt
**속상하다** literally means "the inside is spoiled/hurt" (**속** = inside/feeling, **상하다** = to go bad/be hurt). It is used when you are upset about a result, such as failing an exam (**시험에 떨어져서 속상해요**) or reading **악플 (bad comments/trolls)** online. It is different from being "annoyed" (**짜증나다**); it is a deeper feeling of being emotionally hurt by a situation.

---

### 삐지다: Getting Pissed in a "Cute" Way
**삐지다** means to be upset or "pissed off," but it usually implies a childish or cute nuance. It is frequently used between couples or toward children. If a boyfriend forgets a gift, the girlfriend might **삐졌어요**. The instructor notes that if you are truly, seriously angry, you would use **속상하다** or **서운하다** instead, as **삐지다** can trivialize the emotion.

---

### 서럽다: Feeling Sorry for Oneself
**서럽다** is a more intense, sorrowful emotion. While **서운하다** is being disappointed in others, **서럽다** is feeling deep pity for your own situation. For example, if you are very sick and have no one to take care of you while everyone else is out having fun, you feel **서러워요**. It is a heavy, sad feeling of loneliness and self-pity.

---

### 힘들다: Physically and Mentally Drained
**힘들다** is a very common word meaning "to be hard" or "to be difficult." It can refer to physical exhaustion from exercise (**운동은 힘들어요**) or mental distress from going through a tough time in life (**요즘 힘들어요**). 

The instructor distinguishes this from **피곤하다 (to be tired)**. **피곤하다** is just a lack of sleep or general tiredness, whereas **힘들다** implies that your energy and strength (**힘**) are being completely drained by a challenge. To encourage someone who is **힘들어**, you say **힘내세요! (Cheer up / Have strength!)**.
