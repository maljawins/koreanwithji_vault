## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces the versatile Korean verb **나다 (to occur/to happen)**. While many learners struggle to find direct translations for English adjectives like "angry," "irritated," or "excited," Korean often expresses these states using the verb **나다** combined with specific nouns. The core concept of **나다** is that something arises or occurs spontaneously, often outside of the subject's direct control.

The lesson follows a logical arc: first, establishing the basic grammar and conjugation of **나다**; second, categorizing its use across accidents, natural disasters, sensory experiences, bodily phenomena, and illnesses; third, explaining its role in memory and thoughts; and finally, diving into "emotional verbs" and the specific grammar rules for using them as noun modifiers.

---

### Basic Grammar and Conjugation of 나다

The instructor explains that **나다** is an intransitive verb, meaning it does not take a direct object. Instead, it uses the subject marking particles **이/가**. The basic structure is `[Noun]이/가 나다`, which literally translates to "Noun occurs" or "Noun happens."

Regarding conjugation, when the verb stem ends in the vowel `ㅏ` and has no final consonant (받침), the `아/어 요` ending merges. Therefore, **나다** becomes **나요** in the polite present tense and **나** in the casual (반말) form. This is the same pattern seen in verbs like **가다 (to go)** becoming **가요**.

---

### Accidents, Disasters, and Sensory Phenomena

The first major category for **나다** involves events that happen or occur. This includes accidents and trouble. For example, **사고가 나다 (an accident happens)** or the common expression **큰일 났다 (to be in big trouble)**. The instructor notes that **큰일 (big matter/trouble)** combined with the past tense of **나다** indicates that a significant, usually negative, situation has arisen.

Natural disasters also use this verb. Examples provided include **홍수가 나다 (to flood)** and **지진이 나다 (an earthquake occurs)**. The instructor provides a sample sentence:
> **지구 온난화 때문에 홍수가 더 자주 나요.** / Floods happen more often because of global warming.

Sensory experiences that "occur" to you without you actively creating them also use **나다**. This includes **맛이 나다 (to have a taste)**, **냄새가 나다 (to smell)**, and **소리가 나다 (to make a sound)**. For taste, one can specify **매운 맛이 나다 (to have a spicy taste)** or **아무 맛도 안 나요 (to have no taste at all)**. Regarding smell, the instructor clarifies that while you can say **좋은 냄새 나 (it smells good)**, simply saying **냄새나** often implies a bad smell.

---

### Bodily Phenomena and Illnesses

**나다** is the standard verb for things that "come out" of or happen to the body. The instructor references the BTS song "Blood, Sweat & Tears" to introduce:
- **피가 나다** (to bleed / blood occurs)
- **땀이 나다** (to sweat / sweat occurs)
- **눈물이 나다** (to cry/tear up / tears occur)
- **콧물이 나다** (to have a runny nose / "nose water" occurs)

When describing symptoms of being sick, **나다** is used for fever, coughing, and sneezing:
- **열이 나다** (to have a fever)
- **기침이 나다** (to cough)
- **재채기가 나다** (to sneeze)

The instructor demonstrates these in a sequence:
> **감기에 걸리면 기침이 나고, 재채기가 나고, 콧물이 나요.** / When you catch a cold, you cough, sneeze, and get a runny nose.

---

### Memory and Thoughts: Active vs. Spontaneous

A crucial distinction is made between active mental effort and spontaneous recall. While **기억하다** means to actively "remember" (as in a command: **기억하세요!**), **기억이 나다** means a memory "occurs" to you or you are able to recall it. Therefore, to say "I can't remember," Koreans say **기억이 안 나요** (The memory isn't happening).

Similarly, **생각하다** is the action of thinking, but **생각이 나다** is when a thought spontaneously crosses your mind or you are reminded of something.
> **맛있는 거 먹으면 엄마 생각이 나요.** / When I eat something delicious, I think of my mom (it reminds me of her).

---

### Emotional Verbs and Noun Modifiers

The instructor explains that many English "emotions" are expressed as `[Emotion Noun] + 나다` in Korean. In conversation, the particle is often dropped:
- **화(가) 나다** (to be angry / anger occurs)
- **짜증(이) 나다** (to be irritated / irritation occurs)
- **신(이) 나다** (to be excited/elated / excitement occurs)
- **신경질 나다** (to be annoyed/get on one's nerves)

A key grammatical point is that because these are technically **verbs** in Korean, they follow verb rules for modifying nouns. While a standard adjective like **좋다 (to be good)** becomes **좋은** before a noun, **나다** uses the verb modifier **-는** for the present tense.
- **화나는 일** (an upsetting/infuriating matter)
- **짜증나는 일** (an irritating matter)
- **신나는 일** (an exciting matter)

However, when these emotions are used to describe a current state that resulted from a past occurrence, the past tense modifier **-ㄴ/은** is used. The instructor explains the logic: a verb in the past tense represents a completed action that resulted in a remaining state, which functions like an adjective.
- **화난 사람** (an angry person — literally, a person to whom anger happened)
- **짜증난 것 같아** (It seems like they are irritated — literally, it seems irritation happened)
