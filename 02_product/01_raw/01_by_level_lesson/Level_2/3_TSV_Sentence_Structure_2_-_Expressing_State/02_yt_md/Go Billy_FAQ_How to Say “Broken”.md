## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor addresses a common point of confusion for Korean learners: the many different ways to translate the English word "broken." Because Korean verbs are often more specific about the *manner* in which something broke—whether it stopped functioning, smashed into pieces, or snapped in half—choosing the wrong word can lead to unintended meanings. 

A critical grammatical distinction made at the start is that all the verbs covered in this lesson are passive. They describe the state of an object being broken or the event of it breaking on its own. They are not used to say "I broke [something]." While many can be converted into active forms, this lesson focuses strictly on describing things that are already in a broken state or have broken down.

---

### Mechanical Failure: 고장이 나다 and 고장나다
The expression **고장이 나다 (to break/break down)**, often shortened to **고장나다**, is used specifically for machines, devices, or large objects that stop functioning or operating correctly. It is the most direct equivalent to the English "to break down."

The instructor notes that while it is commonly used for large items like cars, it is perfectly natural to use it for a phone that has simply stopped working. On screen, the instructor provides a primary example:
> **자가가 고장 났어요.** / The car broke down.

---

### Physical Smashes: 깨지다
The verb **깨지다 (to be broken/smashed)** is actually a shortened form of **깨어지다**. This verb describes physical destruction where an object breaks into pieces or gets smashed. It is typically used for fragile items like glass or ceramics.

The instructor demonstrates this with a kitchen example:
> **그릇이 깨졌어요.** / The plate broke (smashed into pieces).

A nuance note is provided regarding cell phones: if you say your phone **깨졌어요**, it implies the screen is shattered or the device is physically smashed, rather than just having a software glitch or a dead battery.

---

### General Non-Functioning: 안 되다
A very straightforward way to say something is broken is using **안 되다 (to not work/function)**. Since **되다** can mean "to work" or "to function," its negative form simply means it isn't doing what it's supposed to do.

This is a versatile expression that can be used for machines, computers, or even abstract situations. However, it lacks the descriptive detail of the other verbs. For example, using it with a plate (**그릇이 안 돼요**) would sound strange, as if the plate "doesn't work" for its intended purpose rather than being physically broken. A common usage scenario shown is a computer failure:
> **컴퓨터가 갑자기 안 돼요.** / The computer suddenly doesn't work.

---

### Fractures and Snaps: 부러지다
When an object that is typically long or solid—like a stick, a pencil, or a bone—snaps or fractures, the correct verb is **부러지다 (to be broken/fractured)**. This is the specific term used for bodily injuries involving bones.

The instructor emphasizes that you would not use "smashed" or "stopped functioning" for a bone; you use the verb that implies a snap or fracture:
> **손목이 부러졌어요.** / My wrist broke (fractured/snapped).

---

### Shattering into Pieces: 부서지다
The instructor warns learners not to confuse **부러지다** (snapping) with **부서지다 (to be shattered/broken into pieces)**. While they sound similar, **부서지다** is used for things that are crushed or shattered into many small fragments.

An example of a toy being destroyed is shown:
> **장난감이 부서졌어요.** / The toy shattered (broke into pieces).

---

### Collapsing Structures: 무너지는
For large structures or systems that break down or collapse, Korean uses **무너지다 (to collapse/break down)**. This applies to physical buildings falling over, but also to abstract concepts like society or a person's spirit.

The instructor provides a more metaphorical example:
> **사회가 무너지고 있어요.** / Society is collapsing (breaking down).

---

### Ruined Beyond Repair: 망가지다
The final common expression is **망가지다 (to be ruined/broken)**. This implies that something has gone wrong to the point of being ruined or no longer usable. It can describe physical objects, but it is also very frequently used for one's life or plans.

The instructor highlights its use in a dramatic context:
> **내 인생이 망가졌어요.** / My life is ruined.
