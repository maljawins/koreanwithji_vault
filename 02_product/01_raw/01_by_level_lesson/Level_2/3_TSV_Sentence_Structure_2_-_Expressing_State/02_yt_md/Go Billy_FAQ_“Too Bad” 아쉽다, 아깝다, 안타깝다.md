## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor addresses a common point of confusion for Korean learners: how to translate the English expression "too bad." While many students default to the verb 아쉽다, Korean actually uses three distinct descriptive verbs—아쉽다, 아깝다, and 안타깝다—depending on the specific nature of the regret or dissatisfaction being expressed. The lesson breaks down the nuances of each, providing situational examples and grammatical structures to help learners choose the most natural expression for a given context.

---

### 아쉽다: Unsatisfied or Missing Something

The verb 아쉽다 primarily means to be unsatisfying. It is used when you feel upset or regretful because an outcome couldn't be better, or because you missed out on an opportunity. The instructor explains that this feeling often stems from not having something you want or need. For example, if you visit Korea but cannot meet a friend because they are busy, or if you study hard for a test but don't achieve the level you hoped for, 아쉽다 is the appropriate choice. It also applies to small, practical disappointments, such as realizing you have no kimchi in the refrigerator while cooking.

The instructor demonstrates the usage with a written example on screen:
> **함께 캠핑을 갈 수 없어서 아쉬워요.** / It’s too bad that we can’t go camping together.

In this sentence, the speaker feels unsatisfied because the desired action (going camping) is impossible, leading to a sense of regret about the missed opportunity.

---

### 아깝다: A Sense of Waste or Being "So Close"

The verb 아깝다 is used when you feel sad about losing something, whether it is a physical object, money, time, or effort. The core nuance is a sense of waste. The instructor provides several scenarios: buying expensive cheese and letting it expire, driving across town for a low-paying job interview (wasted time), or missing a correct answer on a test by just one or two numbers. In the latter case, 아깝다 captures the feeling of being "so close" but ultimately failing, making the effort feel wasted.

A formal example is shown on screen to illustrate this "close call" nuance:
> **이번 시험에서 한 문제를 틀려서 아깝게 1등을 못했어요.** / I missed one question on this test, so unfortunately (it's too bad) I couldn't get first place.

The instructor also introduces a slang version of this verb: **아까비** (or sometimes **아깝비**). This is frequently used among friends, often sarcastically, to say "too bad" or "tough luck."

---

### 안타깝다: Sadness and Frustration

The third verb, 안타깝다, expresses a feeling of being sad and frustrated. While it shares some overlap with 아쉽다 regarding regret, the instructor clarifies that 안타깝다 focuses more on the speaker's internal emotional state—feeling truly heart-wrenched or frustrated by a situation—rather than the objective fact of missing an opportunity. Because its meaning is more general (sadness/frustration), it can be applied to a wide variety of regrettable situations.

This verb is often used in the adverbial form **안타깝게도**, which translates to "unfortunately." The instructor provides a final example sentence:
> **이번 경기에 제가 좋아하는 선수가 안 나와서 안타까워요.** / It’s too bad (I feel sad and frustrated) because the player I like didn't appear in this game.

Similar to 아깝다, 안타깝다 can also be used sarcastically among friends. For instance, saying **아, 안타깝네** (Ah, too bad) can be a way to mock a friend's minor misfortune.

---

### Comparison and Overlap

The instructor emphasizes that these three verbs are not always mutually exclusive. There are many situations where a circumstance could be described as both 아쉽다 (unsatisfying) and 아깝다 (a waste). Learners should not feel pressured to find a single "correct" word but should instead choose the one that emphasizes the specific nuance they want to convey—whether that is the lack of satisfaction, the sense of waste, or the feeling of frustration.
