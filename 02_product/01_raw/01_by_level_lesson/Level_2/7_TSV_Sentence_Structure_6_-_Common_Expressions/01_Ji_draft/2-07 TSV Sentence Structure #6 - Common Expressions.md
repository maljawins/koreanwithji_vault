**Intro**

Welcome. TSV 레슨 Part 1에서는 이 세 가지 단어들을 예시로 “The Rule of Three”의 개념에 대해 배워보았다.

“좋다 / 좋아하다” “싫다 / 싫어하다” “밉다 / 미워하다”

그리고 TSV sentence을 통해서는 타인의 감정을 직접적으로 표현을 할 수 없고 동사를 “아/어하다”로 바꿔야 한다는 것을 배웠다.

**3rd person – TSV sentence**

However, you will still see a lot of TSV sentences with a 3rd person topic, which confuses a lot of people.

And here’s a sentence that once shocked a student of mine.

**제 여자친구는 머리가 좋아요**

She thought this meant

[My girlfriend likes head]

I know. And No.

That’s not what it means. What it actually means is “My girlfriend is smart.”

Remember, according to the rule of three, ***I*** can’t describe how **SHE** feels, which means I can’t use any verb that describes someone’s subjective feeling. Instead, it should be something that describes things rather quote on quote “objectively”.

[Show 제 여자친구는 머리가 나빠요.]

This is why for the opposite, you have to use “나쁘다 - bad” and not “싫다” because “dislike” is a feeling.

[Show 제 여자친구는 머리가 좋아요]

My student’s confusion actually came from 좋다 because it’s a unique word that can have two meanings: One being the dictionary definition “to be good” and the other “to like”, which is only an interpretation, not the definition.

And now we know that 좋다 here can only mean “good”, not “like”.

That said, there are two ways you can interpret a TSV sentences that have a 3rd person topic in it.

But you should note that neither one is 100% accurate due to the untranslatable nature of the topic marker.

[Show 제 여자친구는 머리가 좋아요 / My girlfriend has a good “crossed out head” - brain]

First one is “T has V S”.

Now, even though there’s technically no “have” in the sentence, you would be safe think of it this way in most cases. So, now it would mean “My girlfriend has a good hea.. (don’t finish saying head), let’s just say a good **brain**.

[Show 제 여자친구는 머리가 좋아요 / My girlfriend’s “crossed out head” - brain]

Or second: “T’s S does/is V” So now it would mean “My girlfriend’s **brain** isgood.” But again, there is no “apostrophe S” in the sentence.

Now, you COULD technically match the grammar of this sentence exactly like English by replacing 는 with the possessive marker 의, which is equivalent to “apostrophe S”.

[Show - “제 여자친구의 머리가 좋아요”]

But this hardly changes the meaning of the original sentence. And more importantly, it sounds really awkward and unnatural although it’s not grammatically “wrong” per se.

So, I advise you to just stick with the topic marker and just learn this expression as a whole like an idiom. Just think “head” and you’ll never forget it.

Now, both interpretations sound weird in English but I’ll stick with the first one from here on out because I feel like this one should make a ***little*** more sense to you than the other one.

[Show 제 여자친구는 똑똑해요 / 멍청해요]

We also have these synonymous descriptive verbs – 똑똑하다 for smart and 멍청하다 for stupid.

There’s not much difference in the meaning just like “Smart / Bright” and “Stupid / Dumb” in English.

So you could just make a simple sentence “제 여자친구는 똑똑해요 or 멍청해요.”

But interestingly, you could also do this:

“제 여자친구는 머리가 똑똑해요 / 멍청해요”

My girlfriend has a smart or a stupid brain.

제 여자친구는 머리가 좋아요

제 여자친구는 똑똑해요

제 여자친구는 머리가 똑똑해요.

So, all three sentences have exactly the same meaning - just a different way of phrasing words.

**More TSV Examples with other verbs**

Let me show you some more common expressions in the TSV structure to help you get used to the idea.

**제 친구는 키가 커요 / 작아요.**

제 친구 means "my friend," 키 means "height," 크다 means "big" and 작다 means “small”

“My friend has a big or small height.”

This is how you say someone’s tall or short in Korean.

By the way, I’ve heard some people say “제 친구는 짧아요”.

짧다 does mean “short” but it’s the opposite of “long”, not “tall” So, it’s only used to describe things, not people.

What you should say instead is “제 친구는 키가 작아요.”

Moving on.

**제 동생은 눈이 나빠요**

동생 means younger sibling and it doesn’t have a gender. If you wanted to be specific, you can use 남동생 for younger brother and 여동생 for younger sister.

눈 is eye or eyes. But in Korean, we don’t make it plural and say “눈들”, it’s just “눈”

나쁘다 means bad

So altogether, this means “My younger sibling has bad eyes.”

**그 식당은 김치가 맛있어요.**

그 식당 is “That restaurant”
김치 is Kimchi

맛있다 means “delicious”

So this means “That restaurant has good Kimchi.”

**한국은 인터넷이 빨라요.**

한국 is Korea

인터넷 is Internet

And 빠르다 means “Fast”

“Korea has a fast Internet (speed).”

**제 남자친구는 돈이 많아요**

“My boyfriend has a lot of money.”

“My boyfriend’s money is a lot.”

**너는 얼굴이 예뻐**

“You have a pretty face.”

“Your face is pretty.”

**우리 엄마는 핸드폰이 아이폰이에요.**

“My mom has an iphone (for a) phone.”

“My mom’s phone is an iphone”

I hope you’re seeing the pattern here.

**1st person T has a V S?**

Now, what if you were use “나” or “저” as the topic? Would it still mean “T has V S?”

It all depends on the verb you use.

Notice the verbs all the example sentences you just saw had nothing to do with emotions.

For example, In “제 친구는 키가 커요”, “크다 – Big” has nothing to do with emotion.

So, the rule of three doesn’t apply ,which means you can simply replace 제 친구 with “저” and say “저는 키가 커요” to say “I’m tall”

또한 context에 따라 쉽게 뜻을 구별할 수 있다.

예시를 보여주겠다.

[Show : “저는 눈이 좋아요”]

Now, 눈 has two meanings. Eye and Snow. And 좋다 ALSO could have two meanings “Good” or “To like”.

So, this could mean two things “I have good eyes” or “I like snow”

[Show a picture of a man in an eyesight test] Which one does it mean now?

[Show a picture of a kid playing in snow] – or now?

[Show a picture of a man eating fish eye] – What about now?

And nobody would ever look at this sentence and go “Oh, that means I have good snow” or “I like eyes”.

My point is all you need is a bit of common sense and it should be intuitive.

**기분이 좋다 / 나쁘다**

Here’s another set of expressions for you to learn as a whole chunk. - 기분이 좋다 and 나쁘다.

Show: 저는 기분이 좋아요 / 나빠요

This is how we say “I feel good” or “I feel bad” in Korean. Using our little formula from before, this would mean “I have good 기분 or bad 기분.

기분 means mood. More precisely, it’s “the way someone or something feels at the moment” – a “temporary vibe” if you will.

And I’m directly describing 기분 - my current emotional state using either 좋다 or 나쁘다.

Now, “저는 기분이 좋아요” doesn’t mean [I like “mood”] because it’s about whether my 기분 is good or bad and this again explains why we use [나쁘다] for the opposite, not 싫다.

[Show 기분 좋아하다 / 기분 나빠하다]

One thing to remember though, is when you apply the rule of three and change it into 아/어하다, it should always be 기분 좋아하다 and 기분 나빠하다 **without the object marker.**

기분**을** 좋아하다 means “to like the feeling itself.” For example, “제 여자친구는 그 기분을 좋아했어요” means “My girlfriend liked **that feeling**”. which is not what you wanted to say.

[Show 제 여자친구는 기분 좋아했어요]

You should instead lose the object marker in order to say “my girlfriend felt good.”

In most other most cases, you CAN use the object marker after changing the verb into a 아/어하다 form, but not for “기분 좋아하다 and 나빠하다”.

They’re an exception to remember. So again, I recommend learning them also as a set expression.

**To feel bad ≠ 기분이 나쁘다?**

“Feeling bad” in English can show sympathy like in “I feel bad for her.”

However, 기분이 나쁘다 has absolutely nothing to do sympathy. It literally just means “the way I feel is bad” so it strictly shows that you’re upset.

[Show 저는 걔가 불쌍해요]

To express the same idea, what you could say instead is “저는 걔가 불쌍해요, which also happens to be another TSV sentence.

불쌍하다 means “pitiful”. So literally, it means “To me, she feels pitiful.”, which pretty much has the same message as “I feel bad for her.” describing the feeling of pity you feel towards her.

BUT Now, what if you wanted to say “My MOM feels bad for her?”

Because mom is a 3rd person, you have to change 불쌍하다 to 불쌍해 하다.

[Show: 불쌍하다 + 하다 = 불쌍해 하다]

So, “Mom feels bad for her” would be “엄마는 걔를 불쌍해 해요”.
