**이/가 좋다**

When we learned SOV, we learned how to say "I like something" with 좋아하다.

So, 저는 피자를 좋아해요 means “I like pizza” quite literally.

But there’s another way to say this in Korean.

저는 피자가 좋아요.

는 marks 저 as the topic of the sentence, showing that I’m talking about me.

And 좋다 means “good” literally. But in this case, it can translate to “likable”.

And 가 lets us know that 피자 is what’s likable to me and is the subject of “좋다”.

So, what this sentence technically means **"(When it comes) To me, pizza is likable."**

This is somewhat similar to how Spanish speakers say “I like pizza”. “Me gusta la pizza”, literally means “To me, pizza is pleasing”.

Back to Korean,

Again, you **cannot** ever say “피자를 좋아요” with an object marker because descriptive verbs cannot have an object - You can’t “good” or “likable” a pizza.

If you wanted to use 를, you’d have to use 좋아하다 which is an action verb and say “저는 피자를 좋아해요.”

**이/가 좋다 VS 을/를 좋아하다**

Show: 저는 피자가 좋아요 VS 저는 피자를 좋아해요

So, what is the difference between these two sentences besides grammar? Well, the difference is not in the meaning but in the “linguistic wiring” I mentioned earlier.

**The Rule of Three**

To explain this, I should establish: “To like something” is a feeling, an emotion - If it’s good, you like it and if you like it, it feels good. No brainer.

Now, in Korean, you are not supposed to describe someone else's feelings directly unlike in English where you can say “you like it” or “she likes it”.

You can only speak for yourself, and not for anybody else because you can’t truly “know” **what others are feeling.**

Think about it. It’s like, “Who am ***I*** to say how YOU feel or how THEY feel?

I’m not you or them. Your feelings belong to you and theirs belong to **them**. So, **I** can only speak about my **OWN** feelings, not yours or theirs.

So, in Korean:

* I can say “I like it” or “We like it”. Yes, “we” also works.
* But I can’t say “YOU like it”. But what I CAN do is **ASK** “Do you like it?”
* And when it comes to a 3rd person, I can’t say “He likes it” and I can’t say “Does he like it?” either because that would mean I’m asking YOU if HE likes it and YOU don’t know that.

One exception though, is when you’re telling a story as an all-knowing entity – like an author of a novel or a narrator in a movie where you’re ***supposed to*** know the feelings of the characters you’re talking about.

하지만 일상 대화에서는 예외없이 지켜지는 룰이다.

I call this rule “The rule of three” and this applies to not only feelings but also thoughts, wants, needs, intentions, and pretty much **everything else that** happens in your mind.

흥미롭게도 대다수의 한국 사람들은 이 룰에 대해 인지조차 못 하고 있다. 나 또한 한국어를 가르치기 전에는 그랬다. 하지만 모든 한국인들이 단 한명도 빠짐없이 “무의식적으로” 따를 만큼 근본적이고 절대적인 법칙이라는 점을 강조하고 싶다.

So, you can probably imagine just how critically important it is to understand this rule. So, please remember the name “The rule of three” as it will be mentioned time after time in the future lessons.

**이/가 좋다 VS 을/를 좋아하다**

Show: 저는 피자가 좋아요 VS 저는 피자를 좋아해요

That said, “the rule of three” applies to only one of the two forms - 이/가 좋다 – because it describes the feeling DIRECTLY like “It is good TO ME”

So, you can say “저(나)는 피자가 좋아요” and “저희(우리)는 피자가 좋아요.”

And you can say “피자가 좋아?” or “피자가 좋아요?” as a question but not as a statement.

And in comes a third person. We’ll call her 지수.

[Show a photo of a massive pizza 먹방 thumbnail by a Korean woman]

Even if you saw her finish this whole pizza by herself, you still couldn’t say “지수는 피자가 좋아요” and you can’t ask “지수는 피자가 좋아요?” to anyone else either.

Instead, you need to change 좋다 to 좋아하다 and say 지수는 피자**를** 좋아해요.

Show - 지수는 피자를 좋아해요.

The grammar is simple, you conjugate the first verb using 아 or 어 then attach 하다. 좋다 ends in ㅗ so it’s 좋**아**하다. The opposite, 싫다 (Dislikable) doesn’t end in ㅗ or ㅏ so it’s 싫**어**하다.

Now 좋아하다 and 싫어하다 are action verbs that can have an object. Thus 피자**를** 좋아해요.

And all there’s left for you to do is conjugate 하다 however you want to like 좋아해요, 좋아했어요 and so on.

Unlike 이/가 좋다, 좋아하다 can be used for **everybody** because it’s not exactly a direct description of the feeling. It refers more to *the expression of the feeling* - to **do liking**, if you will.

A better way to think about this is to treat 하다 like “to act” rather than “to do”.

좋아하다 would then mean “to act in such a way that one likes something.” And this is something I CAN say because now, it’s about my observation, not her feelings.

The same applies to many other verbs that describe feelings and emotions.

By the way, there are so many untranslatable emotion words in Korean. I obviously can’t tell you all of them but we’ll talk about a few common ones in the next lesson.

I wanted to share this word with you which people get mixed up with 싫다 all the time.

**아/어하다 - Compound Verb**

I believe every beginner should learn THIS first before anything more sophisticated because knowing this rule early on will not only speed up your progress, but also deepen your perspective and understanding of the Korean language.
