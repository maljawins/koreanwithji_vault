## Teaching Points

### Overview
This lesson focuses on the grammatical construction `Adjective + -아/어하다`, which transforms descriptive adjectives into verbs. This pattern is essential for describing the feelings, emotions, or sensory perceptions of someone other than the speaker. In Korean, there is a strict distinction between expressing one's own internal state and describing the observed state of another person.

The instructor explains that while a speaker can use a plain adjective to describe their own feelings (e.g., "I am sad"), using that same plain adjective for a third person can sound unnatural or overly presumptive, as if the speaker has telepathic access to the other person's mind. By adding `-아/어하다`, the speaker shifts to a "third-person point of view," indicating that they are observing the subject's outward behavior or expressions and concluding that the subject "seems to feel" a certain way.

---

### The Third-Person Point of View
The primary rule for `-아/어하다` is that it is used when the subject of the sentence is in the second person (you) or third person (he, she, they, or a specific name). It describes an emotion or feeling based on observation. For example, comparing `엄마가 슬퍼요` (Mom is sad) with `엄마가 슬퍼해요` (Mom is sad/seems sad), the first sentence implies the speaker can "see through" the mom's feelings, whereas the second sounds more natural in daily life because it positions the speaker as an observer of the mom's actions or facial expressions.

The instructor provides a specific example involving a friend winning a soccer match. To say the friend was happy, one should use `기쁘다 (to be happy)` combined with `-아/어하다`, resulting in `기뻐하다`. In a past tense sentence:
> **제 친구가 축구 경기에서 이기고 나서 엄청 기뻐했어요.** / My friend was very happy after winning the soccer match.

### Exceptions: When Plain Adjectives are Used for Others
There are three main scenarios where a plain adjective (without `-아/어하다`) can be used for a third person. First, in literature or storytelling, an "omniscient author" who knows everything about their characters may use plain adjectives. For example, an author might write:
> **지수는 경기에서 이겨서 기뻤고 지호는 져서 슬펐습니다.** / Jisu was happy because she won the match, and Jiho was sad because he lost.

Second, plain adjectives are used in the future tense when predicting how someone will feel. Since the speaker is guessing about a future state rather than observing a current one, both forms are acceptable:
> **그 소식을 듣고 엄마가 엄청 기쁠 거예요.** / Mom will be very happy to hear that news.
> **그 소식을 듣고 엄마가 엄청 기뻐할 거예요.** / Mom will be very happy to hear that news.

Third, when asking a direct question to the listener (second person) about their feelings, the plain adjective is more natural:
> **너 슬퍼?** / Are you sad?
> **지수 씨, 기뻐요?** / Jisu, are you happy?

### Transitive Nature and Particle Changes
A critical grammatical shift occurs when an adjective becomes a verb via `-아/어하다`: it becomes a transitive verb. This means the particle marking the "object" of the feeling changes from the subject particle `이/가` to the object particle `을/를`.

For example, when describing one's own difficulty with grammar:
> **저는 이 문법이 어려워요.** / I find this grammar difficult. (Adjective + `이/가`)

When describing many people finding it difficult:
> **많은 사람들이 이 문법을 어려워해요.** / Many people find this grammar difficult. (Verb + `을/를`)

### Common Adjectives used with -아/어하다
The instructor lists several categories of adjectives that frequently take this form. These include pure emotions, sensory feelings, and desires.

**Emotions and Social Feelings:**
- `감사하다 / 고맙다 (to be thankful)` → `감사해하다 / 고마워하다`
- `미안하다 (to be sorry)` → `미안해하다`
- `섭섭하다 (to be disappointed - mild)` → `섭섭해하다`
- `자랑스럽다 (to be proud)` → `자랑스러워하다`

**Sensory Feelings:**
- `덥다 (to be hot)` → `더워하다`
- `춥다 (to be cold)` → `추워하다`
- `배고프다 (to be hungry)` → `배고파하다`
- `맵다 (to be spicy)` → `매워하다`
- `맛있다 (to be delicious)` → `맛있어하다`

**Desire and Interest:**
- `고 싶다 (to want to)` → `고 싶어 하다`
- `어렵다 (to be difficult)` → `어려워하다`
- `지루하다 (to be boring/bored)` → `지루해하다`
- `재미있다 (to be fun/interesting)` → `재미있어하다`

### Describing One's Own Feelings with -아/어하다
While primarily for others, a speaker can use `-아/어하다` for themselves when they want to observe their own tendencies or past self with "distance" or objectivity.
> **저는 어렸을 때 의사가 되고 싶어 했어요.** / When I was young, I wanted to become a doctor. (Observing one's younger self).
> **저는 비가 올 때마다 우울해해요.** / Whenever it rains, I (tend to) feel gloomy. (Observing one's own objective tendency).

### Special Pairs: Like, Hate, and Adore
The instructor highlights specific pairs that are essentially built on this rule but have become normalized as independent verbs.

**좋다 vs 좋아하다 and 싫다 vs 싫어하다:**
`좋다 (to be good)` and `싫다 (to be hated/disliked)` are adjectives, while `좋아하다 (to like)` and `싫어하다 (to hate)` are verbs. For third persons, the verb forms are almost always preferred:
> **엄마는 요리를 좋아하세요.** / Mom likes cooking.

**예뻐하다 and 미워하다:**
`예쁘다 (pretty)` as a feeling means to adore or find someone precious. `밉다 (ugly/hateful)` as a feeling means to hate or loathe someone but often with a sense of intimacy or affection (like "cute whining").
- `예뻐하다`: To adore/cherish (used for people or animals).
- `미워하다`: To loathe/hate (often used among close people, like a child to a parent).
