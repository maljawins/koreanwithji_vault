## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the grammar form **-아/어/여하다**, which is used to describe the feelings, desires, or apparent emotional states of a third party. The core linguistic philosophy behind this form is that in Korean, one cannot claim to know another person's internal state with 100% certainty. While English allows speakers to say "He is sad" or "She wants pizza" directly, Korean requires a grammatical distinction to show that the speaker is observing outward behavior and inferring an internal state.

The lesson covers the conjugation mechanics, the transformation of descriptive verbs into action verbs, and the specific social rules regarding when this form is mandatory versus optional. The instructor also highlights important exceptions, such as common honorific commands and verbs that change meaning significantly when this suffix is applied.

---

### The Third-Party Rule and Conjugation
The primary rule for using **-아/어/여하다** is that it must be used when talking about a "third party"—anyone who is not the speaker (first person) or the person being directly addressed (second person). In Korean, it is considered grammatically incorrect to use a simple descriptive verb to state a third person's feelings. For example, while you can say **저는 가고 싶어요** (I want to go), you cannot say **철수는 가고 싶어요** because you cannot inhabit Chul-soo's mind. Instead, you must use the form **철수는 가고 싶어 해요** to indicate that Chul-soo *appears* to want to go.

To conjugate this form, you take a descriptive verb stem and conjugate it into the standard **-아/어/여** form (the same way you would for the polite **-요** ending), then attach **하다**. For example, **슬프다** (to be sad) becomes **슬퍼** plus **하다**, resulting in **슬퍼하다**. The instructor notes a specific spacing rule: while most verbs combine without a space, **싶어 하다** is a common exception where a space is typically maintained between the conjugated **싶어** and **하다**.

---

### Descriptive Verbs vs. Action Verbs
A critical technical aspect of this grammar is that it transforms a descriptive verb (adjective) into an action verb. This change has significant grammatical implications. Descriptive verbs in Korean cannot be used to form commands or negative commands. However, once they are transformed into action verbs via **-아/어/여하다**, they can be used with imperative endings.

The instructor demonstrates this with the verb **슬프다** (to be sad). You cannot say **슬프지 마세요** to mean "Don't be sad" because **슬프다** is descriptive. Instead, you must use the action verb form: **슬퍼하지 마세요**. By using this form, you are literally telling the person "Don't act/appear sad."

---

### Exceptions and Meaning Shifts
There are a few common exceptions where descriptive verbs are used as commands in daily life due to high frequency of use. These include **건강하세요** (Be healthy), **행복하세요** (Be happy), and **아프지 마세요** (Don't be sick/hurt). While these technically violate the descriptive-verb-as-command rule, they are accepted as standard idiomatic expressions.

Additionally, some verbs undergo a shift in nuance when this form is applied. The verbs **예쁘다** (to be pretty) and **귀엽다** (to be cute) become **예뻐하다** and **귀여워하다**. In these forms, the meaning shifts from a simple description of appearance to an action of "adoring" or "treating someone with affection." For example, **아이가 동생을 예뻐해요** means "The child adores their younger sibling," rather than simply "The child thinks the sibling is pretty."

---

### Examples and Contextual Usage
The instructor provides several sentences to illustrate how this form translates naturally as "to seem," "to appear," or "to act like."

> **영희 씨는 새를 싫어해요.** / Young-hee doesn't like (appears to dislike) birds.
> **철수 씨가 저를 무서워해요.** / Chul-soo seems to be afraid of me.
> **강아지가 자꾸 나가고 싶어 해요.** / The dog keeps wanting (appearing to want) to go outside.
> **다들 저를 부러워해요.** / Everyone is (appears) jealous of me.
> **이런 날씨는 북극곰도 추워해요.** / Even polar bears seem cold in this kind of weather.

The form is also used in causal constructions to explain a subsequent action. For instance, **동생이 배고파해서 라면을 끓여줬어요** (Because my younger sibling seemed hungry, I made them ramen). Here, the speaker is basing their decision to cook on the observed outward signs of the sibling's hunger.
