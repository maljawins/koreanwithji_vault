## Teaching Points

### Overview

This lesson explores the various ways to express the concept of "even" in Korean using the particles 도, 까지, 마저, and 조차. While these can often be translated similarly in English, they carry distinct nuances ranging from simple addition to extreme emphasis and negative implications. The instructor breaks down the grammatical rules for each, including how they interact with other particles and verbs, and provides a framework for choosing the correct form based on the desired level of intensity or the specific context of the sentence.

---

### The Basic Particle 도 (Also, Too, Even)

The most common way to say "even" is by using the particle 도 attached to a noun. Beyond "even," 도 is also the standard way to express "also" or "too." The specific translation depends on the context and which noun the particle is attached to. A critical grammatical rule is that 도 replaces the topic (은/는), subject (이/가), and object (을/를) markers. Combinations like 저는 도 or 사과를 도 are incorrect; the particle must stand alone after the noun.

The placement of 도 changes the focus of the sentence. For example, in the sentence 저는 사과도 좋아해요, the meaning is "I like apples too (in addition to other fruits)." However, if placed after the subject, as in 저도 사과를 좋아해요, it means "I too like apples (just like other people do)." Unlike the major markers it replaces, 도 can be used alongside other particles like 에서 (at/in). In such cases, 도 follows the other particle, resulting in constructions like 도서관에서도 (at the library too).

---

### Using 도 with Verbs and Hypotheticals

도 can also be used with verbs to mean "even if" or "even verbing." To do this, the verb is conjugated into the 아/어 form and followed by 도. This is commonly seen in the construction ~아/어/여도 되다 or ~아/어/여도 괜찮다, which literally means "it is okay even if you [verb]" and is used to ask for or give permission. For example, 가도 돼요? means "Can I go?" or "Is it okay if I go?"

For hypothetical or unlikely situations, the intermediate form ~더라도 is used. While the meaning is similar to the standard ~아/어/여도, it emphasizes that the condition is a "suppose" or "let's just say" scenario. The instructor provides the example: 지금 나가더라도 절대 도착하지 못할 것 같아요, which translates to "Even if you were to leave now, I don't think you'll arrive in time." This form highlights that the speaker considers the action (leaving now) unlikely to change the outcome.

---

### Emphatic "Even" with 까지 and 까지도

The particle 까지, which usually means "as far as" or "until," can function as a more emphatic version of 도. When used to mean "even," it suggests that the situation went further than expected. For instance, comparing 저도 해야 돼요? (Do I have to do it too?) with 저까지 해야 돼요? (Even I have to do it?) shows that 까지 adds a layer of "as far as me?" or "up to me?"

This emphasis can be pushed even further by using 까지도. This combined form is used only with nouns and represents a very strong "even." An example provided is 오늘 눈까지 왔어요 (It even snowed today), implying that on top of everything else that happened, the snow was an extreme or final addition to the situation.

---

### Formal and Negative "Even" with 마저

마저 is an intermediate-level particle that also translates to "even," but it is primarily used in formal writing and carries a specific nuance. It implies that the noun it attaches to is the "last remaining" item or person in a group that completes a situation, usually a negative one. It often appears in sentences where something bad has happened to everything, and now "even this last thing" is affected.

The instructor demonstrates this with the sentence 가방에 있는 사탕마저 다 먹어버렸어 (They even ate the candy in my bag). This suggests the person had already eaten everything else, and the candy was the final item left. Another example, 철수마저 그 사람을 싫어해요, implies that if even the last person you'd expect (철수) dislikes someone, then that person must be truly unpleasant.

---

### Unexpected and Comparative "Even" with 조차

조차 is used for "even" in situations that are unexpected or surprising. Unlike the other particles, 조차 is almost exclusively used with negative verbs or verbs that carry a negative/repulsive meaning (like "to hate" or "to be bothersome"). It indicates that the speaker didn't expect even a basic or small thing to be the case. For example, 우리는 얼굴조차 본 적이 없어 means "We haven't even seen each other's faces," implying they haven't even met at the most basic level.

조차 is frequently paired with the advanced form ~은/는커녕, which means "forget about [noun]" or "far from [noun]." Together, they create a strong comparison: "Forget A, I can't even do B." The instructor provides the example 한국말은커녕 영어조차 못해요 (Forget Korean, I can't even speak English). This structure emphasizes that if the simpler task (English) is impossible, the more difficult task (Korean) is out of the question.
