### Can & Can’t

Now let’s learn how to say “can” and “can’t” in Korean — using the grammar Verb Stem + ㄹ/을 수 있다 or 없다.

### First off — what does 수 mean?

### 수\*\* is actually a noun, and it means \*\*“ability,” “way,” “means,”\*\* or \*\*“possibility.”

So when you say 수 있다, you’re literally saying: 👉 “There is a way (to do it)” or I have the ability to do it.

And when you say 수 없다, you’re saying: 👉 “There’s no way” or “It’s not possible.”

So it could be translated as [be able to] / [not be able to]

### ✅ 수 있다 – Can / Be able to

Let’s take a look at some examples:

📍 저는 노래를 할 수 있어요. → I can sing. [I have the ability to sing]

📍 제 친구는 한국 요리를 할 수 있어요. → My friend can cook Korean food. \*She has the ability to do that.\*

### ❌ 수 없다 – Can’t / Be unable to

Now, this one has a stronger nuance than a simple “I can’t because I’m busy or I’m doing something else.”

It’s more like: 👉 “It’s impossible.” 👉 “I have no way to do that.”

It implies that the situation is out of your control because it literally means “there is no way”,

📍 그 날 파티에 오래 있을 수 없었어요. → I couldn’t stay at the party for long that day.

Maybe something came up or maybe you weren’t feeling well — but the point is, you couldn’t — it wasn’t an option.

📍 제 친구는 올 수 없을 거예요. → My friend won’t be able to come.

Again, it’s not that she just doesn’t \*want\* to — it’s that she can’t, due to some condition or limitation.

So the keyword is 수 which again means “ability” or “way”:

수 있다 = There’s a way → I \*can\*

수 없다 = There isn’t a way → I \*can’t\* because it’s impossible or out of my hands

### 이러다 / 저러다 / 그러다 + ㄹ 수 있다 (없다)

Now, this is a super common expression. You’ll hear all the time — especially when reacting to situations, either to show empathy or shock

You already know the concept of 이 저 and 그. Now, when you combine these with -러다, we get.

* 이러다 → to act like this / or to say so [like this]
* 그러다 → to act like that / or to say so [like that]
* 저러다 → to act like that / or to say so [like that over there]

By the way these are action verbs — not to be confused with 이렇다 / 그렇다 / 저렇다, which are descriptive verbs.

### And if you add -(으)ㄹ 수 있다, we get 이럴 수 있어 / 저럴 수 있어 / 그럴 수 있어

These are used to say:

* “That can happen.” “That’s understandable.” “I can see why.” “Yeah, that makes sense.”

🧠 Especially 그럴 수 있어 is very common and used a lot in everyday speech when reacting to someone’s situation.

It’s basically a way to say: “I get it. That could happen. I understand where you’re coming from.”

Let’s look at an example:

A: 너무 늦게 일어났어. → I woke up too late.

B: 그럴 수 있어. → Yeah, that happens. / Understandable.

Now, as for 없다 part, You’ll often hear: 이럴 수가… / 저럴 수가… / 그럴 수가… (Without saying 없어)

These phrases are extremely common in Korean — especially in emotional or dramatic situations — and they typically end with just “수가…”, trailing off.

Technically, this expression comes from: 이럴 수가 없어 = “There’s no way this could happen”

But in real speech, we drop the “없어” because the emotion is already clear from the tone and context.

🧠 Think of it like: “No way…” “How can this be…” “This can’t be happening…”

So 이럴 수가 on its own already delivers the full emotional punch. There’s no need to say 없어

### 할 수 없다 vs 못하다 vs 하지 못하다

### And this one the only form that’s 받침 sensitive.

### 🔹 못 + Verb

This is the most common and versatile way to say “can’t” in Korean. 못 works like 안, the negation we already learned.

### This can mean:

* You don’t know how / You’re bad at it / Or You can’t do it because of circumstances

### So, this form is flexible — it covers both skill and situation.

Like you can emphasize what you can’t do, or how completely you’re unable to do it.

### 🔸 Expression 1: 가지 못해요. This is the standard 지 못하다 form.

### 🔸 Expression 2: 가지를 못해요

### Showing Contrast ~지만

There are many ways to express contrast in Korean, and today we’re focusing on two forms you’ve probably seen before

First is 지만. 지만 comes from the word 하지만, which you might already know means 'but.'

Now, 지만 is often translated as “but”. — but it’s actually closer to 'however.'

### 📌 하지만 vs. ~지만

* 하지만 can only be used at the beginning of a sentence.
* If you want to connect two contrasting ideas in one sentence, you’ll use ~지만 attached to a verb stem.

It’s super simple: Just take the verb stem and add ~지만.

There’s no need to worry about 받침.

And This form connects two ideas and clearly contrasts them—just like \*“but”\* or \*“however”\* in English.

"Let’s go over some examples together.

1. 오늘은 바쁘지만 내일은 한가해요.

→ \*I’m busy today, but I’m free tomorrow.\*

This sentence shows a contrast in time or schedule—busy today, free tomorrow."

All of these sentences clearly contrast two ideas:.

### Showing contrast ~데

### Now, we have ~데, which comes from 그런데, meaning \*“but”\*

### By the way 그런데 is almost always shortened to 근데 in spoken Korean

### Unlike ~지만, which directly shows a clear contrast, ~데 feels softer, more subtle, and can sometimes even feel like “and” or “yet,” depending on the context.

\*\*It’s similar to how you can say but/and/yet in English in this example:

▶ \*I’ve been working for 12 hours straight, but/and/yet I’m still at the office.\*

Now this is what ~데 feels like.\*\*

### So instead of thinking “but = contrast,” think of ~데 as showing a background, setup, or a mild contrast between two ideas.

### By the way, 그런데 is almost always shortened to 근데 in spoken Korean. You’ll hear this version a lot more in casual conversation.

\*\*Conjugating ~데 is a bit trickier than the ~지만 form.

Here’s what you need to do:\*\*

### First, you need to identify what type of verb you’re dealing with.

### If the verb is an action verb, then it’s pretty simple—regardless of whether the verb stem ends in a consonant or not, you just add ~는데.

### For example, 가다 becomes 가는데, and 믿다 becomes 믿는데.

### But when you’re working with descriptive verbs, the rule changes depending on whether there’s a 받침.

### If the verb stem ends in a consonant, you add ~은데.

### For example, 작다 has a 받침, so it becomes 작은데.

### If the verb stem doesn’t have a 받침, you add ~ㄴ데. So 예쁘다 becomes 예쁜데.

### But there are a few exceptions with this rule.

### First, with action verbs that end in ㄹ, which we call ㄹ irregulars, you actually remove the ㄹ and then add ~는데. For example, 살다 turns into 사는데.

### And for descriptive verbs that end in ㅂ. In this case, you replace the ㅂ with 우, and then add ~운데. So 쉽다 becomes 쉬운데.

### Lastly, we have two verbs that don’t follow the usual descriptive verb pattern—있다 and 없다. These are always conjugated as 있는데 and 없는데, no matter what. Just memorize these as fixed forms.

### So again—action verbs are usually simple, descriptive verbs depend on 받침, and then we’ve got some irregulars and a couple of exceptions to keep in mind.

### Once you get used to spotting the verb type, this form becomes a lot easier to use naturally.

### ~데 examples (Connector)

### Now, we have more example sentences here. We’re going to look at a couple of them here

\*\*First, 이 식당은 작은데 음식이 정말 맛있어요.

→ The restaurant is small, but the food is really good.

Here, we’re showing contrast. You’d expect a small place to have average food, but the food is surprisingly good. So 작은데 is giving us that unexpected twist.\*\*

\*\*친구를 만났는데 같이 밥을 먹었어요.

→ \*I met a friend, and we ate together.\*

Here, ~는데 is just connecting two actions. It’s not contrast—it’s more like a natural flow from one event to the next. I met a friend → and then we ate.\*\*

\*\*Last, 방이 넓은데 조용해요.

→ \*The room is spacious and quiet.\*

This is another example where ~데 works almost like “and.” There’s no contrast here. You’re simply linking two positive qualities about the room.\*\*

### ~데(요) Ending (Context marker)

### Just like ~고요, we can end a sentence with ~데요, but the nuance is a little different. ~데요 is often used to imply contrast, surprise, hesitation, or curiosity—without saying it directly.

### You can think of it like the English word “though” at the end of a sentence. It’s not as direct as saying “but,” but it still gives a hint of contrast or a shift in thought.

### This ending has 4 main purposes.

\*\*4. To ask with curiosity

Let’s say your boyfriend hangs up a phone call, and you want to know who it was.\*\*

\*\*You can ask: 누군데? → \*Who was it, though?\*

That ~데 adds a light, casual feeling of curiosity. You’re not demanding an answer—you’re just asking for more info in a natural way.\*\*

### ~데요 can be used to politely disagree, show surprise, express hesitation, or ask with curiosity.

It’s all about tone, context, and delivery. You’ll hear it a lot, and once you get used to it, you’ll find it super useful in conversations.

### ~데(요) examples (Ending)

A: 이 가방 어때요?

→ \*How is this bag?\* \*(Do you like the bag?)\*

B: 예쁜데, 너무 비싼데요.

→ \*It’s pretty, but it’s a little expensive, though.\*

You’re giving your honest opinion, but softening it. It’s not a hard “no, I don’t want it.” But you’re just pointing out.

A: 이 쿠키 엄마가 만들었어.

→ \*Mom made this cookie.\*

B: 이거 맛있는데요!

→ \*It’s delicious!\*

You’re surprised in a good way. Maybe you didn’t expect it to taste that good —and ~데요 captures that reaction.

A: 통화 가능하세요? → \*Is this a good time to talk?\*

B: 지금 바쁜데… → \*I’m busy, but…\*

You’re not flat-out rejecting the call. You’re giving a soft, hesitant answer. That trailing tone leaves space for follow-up.

A: 저 사람 누구예요? 왜 따라와요? → \*Who is that person? Why is he following us?\*

B: 제 친구인데요. → \*He’s my friend, though.\*

You’re gently correcting the assumption—without sounding defensive.

A: 회원이신가요? → \*Are you a member?\*

B: 오늘 처음 왔는데요. → \*I came today for the first time, though.\* \*(I’m new, though.)\*

Here, you’re not just saying “no.” You’re giving context and being polite about it.

### ~지만 VS ~데

Alright, let’s compare ~지만 and ~(으)ㄴ/는데.

~지만 comes from 하지만 and has a similar nuance to “however,” so it has a stronger, clearer sense of contrast.

~데, on the other hand, comes from 그런데, which is softer. The contrast it shows feels more indirect or implied. So, depending on the context, ~데 can mean “and,” “but,” “yet,” or even “while.”

Also, ~지만 can only be used to connect sentences, whereas ~데 can be used both as a sentence connector and as a sentence ending.

### When

Next, we’re going to learn how to say if and when. First, we have ㄹ/을 때.

Here’s how to make it

If the verb ends in a 받침 – You add 을 때:

* 받다 → 받 + 을 때 = 받을 때 (When I receive)

And if it doesn’t, You add ㄹ 때:

* 가다 → 가 + ㄹ 때 = 갈 때 (When I go)

For irregular ㄹ, you simply remove ㄹ, and add ㄹ 때, so essentially, all you need to do it add only 때.

So, 살다 becomes 살 때 (When I live)

For irregular ㅂ, you replace ㅂ with 우 and add ㄹ 때. So, 덥다 becomes 더울 때

Now, 때 means "moment" or "time." Think of it as "at the time when". This form is used to refer to specific moments or situations.

For example:

* 집에 갈 때 전화해 → Call me when you go home (at the time when you go home)
* 제가 필요할 때 문자 주세요 → Text me when I’m needed (when you need me)

So again, this form focuses on definitive situations or specific moments contrary to what we’re going to learn next.

### ㄹ/을 때 More examples

Here are more example sentences using ㄹ/을 때.

Notice that the 때 clause comes first in the sentence. For example:

* 음악을 들을 때 집중이 잘 돼요.

→ I can focus well when I listen to music.

In this sentence, "음악을 들을 때" (when I listen to music) comes first, followed by "집중이 잘 돼요" (I can focus well).

The rest of the examples are straightforward, and you can review them after the lesson.

### 으면 vs 때

Now let’s compare 때 and 면 side by side, so you can really feel the difference.

ㄹ/을 때 is used when something is definite or at least seemingly definite. So, it refers to a specific moment or time which means you’re talking about something that happened, is happening or will happen.

And 면 / 으면 is used for more hypothetical situations. So, it can mean both if and when depending on the context.

### 면 / 으면 좋겠다

Now this form 면 / 으면 + 좋겠다 is a super common structure to express hope or wish in Korean.

Literally, it means: \*“It would be good if…”\* But it naturally translates to: “I hope…”

And it’s very simple to use it. You take the verb stem and attach (으)면 좋겠다.

For example:

### 저는 너가 새로운 환경에서 잘 지내면 좋겠어.

Literally: \*As for me, if you do well in a new environment, it would be good.\*

But what it really means is: I hope you do well in your new environment.

Now, you can also use the past tense stem with (으)면 좋겠다. It still means the same thing, but it adds a little extra emotion or emphasis — like the English “If it \*were\* like this, that would be nice.”

Here’s a great example:

### 빨리 여자친구가 생겼으면 좋겠어요.

Literally: \*If a girlfriend appeared soon, it would be good.\*

But this naturally means: I hope I get a girlfriend soon.

And by the way, in Korean, we don’t say “to get a girlfriend.”

We say “a girlfriend appears” or “happens.” Just one of those fun little differences.

### 되다

Before we move on to how to say things like should or have to. We first need to know about the concept of 되다.

되다 is one of the most important verbs in Korean, right up there with 하다.

It has a lot of meanings depending on the context, but they all come back to four core ideas. And that last one? Super important for grammar and sentence building later on.

Let’s go through them.

First, 되다 means “to become.” This is probably the most standard meaning.

For example: 저는 건축가가 되고 싶어요

→ \*I want to become an architect.\*

Next, 되다 is the passive form of 하다, so it means \*“to be done” or “to be made.”\*

You’ll hear this all the time in daily conversation.

집중이 잘 돼요 → I can easily focus — literally “\*Focus is done well\*”

Another one is just: 됐어요.

Literally, “it’s done”, but depending on tone, this can mean \*“Forget it”\* or \*“No thanks.”\*

Third, 되다 can also mean “to work” or “to function.” 컴퓨터가 잘 안 돼요

→ \*The computer doesn’t work well.\*

And finally, 되다 is used to mean “to be okay” or “to be acceptable.”

This is where it gets really common in everyday expressions.

You’ve probably heard 안 돼 — it means \*“You can’t”\* or \*“It’s not okay.”\* Very different from just saying 아니요.

Here’s a good example:

집에 가도 돼요? → \*Is it okay if I go home?\*

Or more naturally: \*May I go home?\*

### 되다

Let’s look at how to conjugate 되다 in different tenses and speech levels.

First, just remember that 되 + 어 becomes 돼. So 되 and 돼 sound the same, but the spelling is different. This is one of the most common mistakes even native speakers make when writing.

Now, here’s how 되다 changes in different tenses:

* In the present tense, it becomes 돼요 in casual/formal speech or 됩니다 in very formal or polite situations.
* In the past tense, it becomes 됐어요.
* And in the future tense, it turns into 될 거예요.

### Connectors + 되다

In Korean, we don’t have helping verbs like “should,” “may,” or “have to” like in English. Instead, we use 되다 after certain connectors to express these ideas.

When 되다 is used after connectors, it often means “to be okay” or “to be acceptable.” I’m going to go over each form in detail, but first, let’s just take a look at this table for you to see to get a full picture on what’s going on. And you can always refer back to it for review.

We’ll focus on three key usages: 면, 도, and 야.

### 면 / 으면 되다

We just learned 면. When combined with 되다, it can be translated as “To be okay if,” which gives us the meaning of “Could / Can just / Just have to.”

For example:

* 이제 뭐 하면 돼요? – What is okay if I do now? [What do I do now?]
* 여기서 기다리면 돼요? – Is it okay if I wait here? [Can I just wait here?]

On the other hand, when combined with 안 되다, it means "Not okay if," and it can be translated as “Shouldn’t.”

For example:

* 그거 만지시면 안 돼요. – It’s not okay if you touch that. [You shouldn’t touch that.]
* 거기 앉으시면 안 돼요. – It’s not okay if you sit there. [You shouldn’t sit there.]

### 아/어/여도 되다

We learned the particle 도 the other day. And it means even. So, Verb stem + 아/어/여 + 도 means “even if,” “even when,” or “even though,” depending on the context.

It can be used to express a situation where something is possible.

For example:

* 돈이 없어도 행복할 수 있어요. – Even when you don’t have money, you can be happy. [You can be happy without money.]

You can also add the adverb 아무리 in front of the phrase to emphasize how extreme the situation is.

* 아무리 게임이 좋아도 밤을 새지 마세요. – Even though you like video games so much, don’t stay up all night. [No matter how much you like video games, don’t stay up all night.]

When used with 되다, it can be translated as “may” in positive sentences, where it’s okay even if you do something, and “don’t have to” in negative sentences, where it’s okay even if you don’t do something. It’s commonly used when asking for or giving permission.

For example:

* 그림을 만져봐도 돼요? – Is it okay even if I try touching the painting? [May I touch it?]

If you want to add emphasis, you can use the adverb 꼭 [Necessarily, certainly] in front of the sentence.

* 바다에서는 수영복을 꼭 입지 않아도 돼요. – It’s okay even if you don’t wear a swimsuit at the beach. [You don’t have to.]

### 아/어/여야 되다 / 하다

The suffix “야” means “only if.” When combined with the verb stem, it indicates that something is true or happens only under a specific condition.

For example:

* 김치는 매워야 맛있어요

– Kimchi is tasty only if it’s spicy.

[맵다 → 매우 + 어야 = 매워야]

* 아침에 샤워를 해야 개운해요

– It’s refreshing only if you take a shower in the morning.

[하 + 여야 = 해야]

Now, when combined with 되다, 야 means "must," "have to," or "need to." Literally, it means something is acceptable only if a certain condition is met.

For example:

* 사람들을 더 많이 모아야 돼요

– We need to gather more people.

* 내일 아침에 출근해야 됩니다

– I have to go to work tomorrow morning.

Notice that ~야 하다 means exactly the same thing as ~야 되다.

The only difference is that ~야 하다 is slightly more formal and less commonly used in spoken Korean. For example:

* 이제부터 더 자주 운동해야 해요

– I need to exercise more frequently from now on.
