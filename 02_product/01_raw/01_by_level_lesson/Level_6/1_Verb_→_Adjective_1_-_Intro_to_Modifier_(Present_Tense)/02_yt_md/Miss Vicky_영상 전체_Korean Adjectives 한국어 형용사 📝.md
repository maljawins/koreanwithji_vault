## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces Korean adjectives by highlighting their two primary functions: acting as a verb to complement a subject (e.g., "Jeff is sad") and acting as a modifier for a following noun (e.g., "sad cat"). While English adjectives require a "be" verb to function as a predicate, Korean adjectives are treated exactly like verbs and are conjugated directly. The lesson is structured to first cover conjugation rules for adjectives functioning as verbs, followed by the specific rules for transforming adjectives into their modifying forms.

---

### Adjectives as Verbs: Basic Conjugation

When an adjective functions as a verb, it follows the same general conjugation rules as Korean verbs. The instructor demonstrates this with the base form `슬프다 (to be sad)`. To say "Jeff is sad," the subject `제프 (Jeff)` is followed by the subject marker `가`, and the adjective is conjugated into the present tense `슬퍼요`. Similarly, for "The cat is small," the subject `고양이 (cat)` takes the marker `가`, and the adjective `작다 (to be small)` conjugates to `작아요`. Adjectives can be conjugated into present, past, or future tenses just like action verbs.

---

### Group 1: Stems ending in 아 or 오

The first group of conjugation follows the rule of adding `아` to the stem. This applies when the last vowel of the stem is `아` or `오`. For adjectives like `좋다 (to be good)`, which has an `오` vowel and a final consonant (받침), the stem becomes `좋아`. If the stem ends in the vowel `아` without a final consonant, such as `싸다 (to be cheap)` or `비싸다 (to be expensive)`, the `아` is deleted to avoid redundancy, resulting in `싸` and `비싸`. The instructor notes that while verbs like `오다 (to come)` or `보다 (to see)` combine `오+아` into `와`, there are very few adjectives that follow this specific contraction pattern.

---

### Group 2: Stems ending in other vowels

The second group adds `어` to the stem when the last vowel is anything other than `아` or `오`. Adjectives with a final consonant like `적다 (to be few/less)` become `적어`, `넓다 (to be wide)` become `넓어`, and `길다 (to be long)` become `길어`. If the stem ends in the vowel `이` without a final consonant, the `이` and `어` combine into `여`. Examples include `느리다 (to be slow)` becoming `느려`, `흐리다 (to be cloudy)` becoming `흐려`, and `시다 (to be sour)` becoming `셔`.

---

### Group 3: 하다 Adjectives

Adjectives ending in `하다` follow the simplest rule: `하` changes to `해`. The instructor provides examples such as `편하다 (to be comfortable)` becoming `편해` and `불편하다 (to be uncomfortable)` becoming `불편해`. She notes that the prefix `불` comes from a Chinese character meaning "not," effectively negating the adjective. Other examples include `뚱뚱하다 (to be overweight/fat)` becoming `뚱뚱해` and `날씬하다 (to be skinny)` becoming `날씬해`.

---

### Group 4: Irregular 으 and 르 Adjectives

Adjectives with stems ending in the vowel `으` require looking at the preceding syllable. If the preceding syllable contains `아` or `오`, `으` is replaced by `아`. For example, `나쁘다 (to be bad)` becomes `나빠`, `바쁘다 (to be busy)` becomes `바빠`, `배고프다 (to be hungry)` becomes `배고파`, and `아프다 (to be sick/hurt)` becomes `아파`. If the preceding vowel is not `아` or `오`, or if there is no preceding syllable, `으` is replaced by `어`. Thus, `슬프다 (to be sad)` becomes `슬퍼`, `예쁘다 (to be pretty)` becomes `예뻐`, and `쓰다 (to be bitter/to write)` becomes `써`.

A special sub-rule applies to `르` endings. When the stem ends in `르`, an extra `ㄹ` is added as a final consonant to the preceding syllable, and the `르` changes to `라` or `러` based on the vowel rules. For example, `다르다 (to be different)` becomes `달라` and `빠르다 (to be fast)` becomes `빨라`. The instructor encourages learners not to over-analyze these rules but to rely on constant exposure for natural acquisition.

---

### Group 5: ㅂ Irregular Adjectives

Many common adjectives end in a `ㅂ` final consonant. For these, the `ㅂ` is deleted and replaced with `워` in the present tense. Examples include `맵다 (to be spicy)` becoming `매워`, `아름답다 (to be beautiful)` becoming `아름다워`, `덥다 (to be hot)` becoming `더워`, `춥다 (to be cold)` becoming `추워`, `어렵다 (to be difficult)` becoming `어려워`, `쉽다 (to be easy)` becoming `쉬워`, `외롭다 (to be lonely)` becoming `외로워`, and `귀엽다 (to be cute)` becoming `귀여워`. The instructor clarifies that this rule only applies when `ㅂ` is the sole final consonant, not in double consonants like `짧다 (to be short)`.

---

### Adjectives as Modifiers: The ㄴ/은 Rule

To use an adjective as a modifier before a noun, the stem is modified by adding `ㄴ` or `은`. If the stem ends in a vowel (no final consonant), add `ㄴ`. If the stem ends in a consonant, add `은`.

- `좋다` (has consonant) → `좋은 친구` (a good friend)
- `짧다` (has consonant) → `짧은 머리` (short hair)

Special irregular rules apply to modifiers as well. For `ㅂ` irregulars, the `ㅂ` is deleted and replaced with `운`. Thus, `아름답다` becomes `아름다운 눈` (beautiful eyes), `귀엽다` becomes `귀여운 고양이` (a cute cat), and `맵다` becomes `매운 음식` (spicy food). For adjectives ending in `ㄹ` like `길다 (to be long)` or `달다 (to be sweet)`, the `ㄹ` is deleted and replaced with `ㄴ`, resulting in `긴 머리` (long hair) and `단 커피` (sweet coffee).

---

### The 있다 and 없다 Exception

Adjectives derived from `있다 (to exist/to have)` and `없다 (to not exist/to not have)` follow a different modifier rule. Instead of `은`, they take `는`. This is because they are technically treated as verbs in their modifying form.

- `맛있다 (to be delicious)` → `맛있는 음식` (delicious food)
- `맛없다 (to be tasteless/bad)` → `맛없는 음식` (bad food)
- `재미있다 (to be fun/interesting)` → `재미있는 영화` (a fun movie)
- `재미없다 (to be boring)` → `재미없는 책` (a boring book)

The instructor explains that while these function as adjectives, their root stems from verbs, which is why they use the verb modifier suffix `는`.
