## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor begins by clarifying a fundamental difference between English and Korean grammar: how adjectives function. In English, adjectives like "green" or "small" are a distinct category of words used to describe nouns. In Korean, however, what we call adjectives are actually 형용사 (descriptive verbs). These words behave like verbs because they must be conjugated to function as modifiers. The lesson covers the mechanics of transforming these descriptive verbs into adjective forms for the present, past, and future tenses, as well as how to handle irregular stems and connect multiple adjectives together.

---

### The Concept of Descriptive Verbs

In Korean, you do not simply place a dictionary-form word before a noun to describe it. For example, the word 작다 means "to be small." You cannot say 작다 고양이 to mean "small cat." Instead, you must conjugate the descriptive verb into a modifier form that can sit directly before the noun. The instructor emphasizes that learners should always memorize the dictionary form (ending in 다) rather than the conjugated adjective form. This is because the dictionary form is the "root" from which all other forms—including sentence endings like 작아요 (it is small) and various tense modifiers—are derived. Learning only the adjective form 작은 (small) would leave a student unable to say "the cat was small" or "the cat is small" at the end of a sentence.

---

### Present Tense Conjugation Rules

To turn a descriptive verb into a present-tense adjective, the instructor outlines a two-step process based on the verb stem. First, remove the 다 to find the stem. If the remaining stem ends in a consonant (받침), you attach 은. For example, the stem of 작다 (to be small) is 작, which ends in a consonant, so it becomes 작은. This allows for the phrase 작은 고양이 (a small cat). Similarly, 좋다 (to be good) becomes 좋은, as in 좋은 영화 (a good movie). If the stem ends in a vowel, you simply attach the letter ㄴ to the bottom of the stem. The verb 크다 (to be big) has the stem 크, which ends in a vowel, so it becomes 큰, resulting in 큰 문 (a big door). This rule also applies to the verb 이다 (to be); the stem 이 becomes 인, which is used to describe what something "is," such as 미국 사람 인 선생님 (a teacher who is an American).

---

### Handling Irregular Stems (ㅂ, ㄹ, and ㅎ)

Several common descriptive verbs follow irregular conjugation patterns when becoming adjectives. For stems ending in ㅂ, such as 어렵다 (to be difficult) or 쉽다 (to be easy), you must remove the ㅂ, add a ㅜ, and then add ㄴ to that ㅜ. This results in 어려운 (difficult) and 쉬운 (easy), used in phrases like 어려운 시험 (a difficult test). For stems ending in ㄹ, like 길다 (to be long) or 멀다 (to be far), you remove the ㄹ entirely and then add ㄴ to the remaining vowel. Thus, 길다 becomes 긴 and 멀다 becomes 먼, as in 먼 산 (a far mountain). Finally, for stems ending in ㅎ, such as 빨갛다 (to be red), you remove the ㅎ and add ㄴ to the bottom. This creates 빨간, as in 빨간 모자 (a red hat). The instructor notes that 좋다 (to be good) is an exception to the ㅎ rule because it keeps its ㅎ and follows the standard consonant rule to become 좋은.

---

### Special Cases: 있다 and 없다

The verbs 있다 (to exist/to have) and 없다 (to not exist/to not have) follow a unique but simple rule. Regardless of the consonant ending, they always take 는 instead of 은. This applies to any compound verb containing them, such as 맛있다 (to be delicious) or 재미없다 (to be uninteresting). To say "delicious cake," you take 맛있다, remove 다, and add 는 to get 맛있는 케이크. These forms are very common and should be memorized as a distinct pattern.

---

### Past and Future Tense Modifiers

To describe a noun in the past tense, such as "a cat that was small," the instructor provides a formula based on the 요 form of the verb. You conjugate the verb to its past tense 요 form, remove the 요, and attach 던. For example, the past tense of 작다 is 작았어요; removing the 요 and adding 던 results in 작았던, as in 작았던 고양이 (the cat that was small). For the future tense, which often carries the nuance of "a noun that will be [adjective]" or "I think it will be [adjective]," you attach 을 to stems ending in a consonant and ㄹ to stems ending in a vowel. The same irregular rules for ㅂ, ㄹ, and ㅎ apply here. For instance, 덥다 (to be hot) becomes 더울, often used in the phrase 더울 것 같아요 (I think it will be hot).

---

### Connecting Multiple Adjectives

When using two adjectives to describe a single noun, such as "a long and heavy bridge," you do not need to conjugate both into adjective forms. Instead, you take the first descriptive verb, remove the 다, and attach the connector 고 (meaning "and"). Only the final adjective in the sequence is conjugated into the modifier form. Using 길다 (to be long) and 무겁다 (to be heavy) to describe 다리 (bridge), you combine them into 길고 무거운 다리. This is much more efficient than trying to string together multiple conjugated adjectives.
