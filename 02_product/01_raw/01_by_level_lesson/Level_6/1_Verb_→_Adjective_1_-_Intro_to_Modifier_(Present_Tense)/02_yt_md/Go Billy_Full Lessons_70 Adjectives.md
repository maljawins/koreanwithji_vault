## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the fundamental concept of Korean adjectives, explaining that they function quite differently from English adjectives. While English adjectives are distinct words used to describe nouns, Korean adjectives are actually derived from "descriptive verbs." This means that to use an adjective in front of a noun, a learner must understand how to conjugate the base descriptive verb into its adjective (modifier) form.

The lesson covers the basic conjugation rules for regular descriptive verbs, as well as several important irregular patterns involving stems ending in specific consonants or vowels. By learning these conjugation rules, students can expand their vocabulary exponentially, as they will be able to transform any descriptive verb they encounter into a functional adjective.

---

### Descriptive Verbs vs. Adjectives
In English, a word like "big" is simply an adjective. In Korean, the base form is a descriptive verb: **크다 (to be big)**. To use it to describe a noun like **집 (house)**, you cannot just place the verb in front of the noun. You must conjugate it. The instructor demonstrates this with two primary examples: **크다 (to be big)** becomes **큰 (big)**, and **작다 (to be small)** becomes **작은 (small)**. These transformed words are then placed directly before the noun, just like in English.

> **큰 집** / a big house
> **작은 집** / a small house
> **큰 머리** / a big head
> **작은 머리** / a small head

The instructor emphasizes that memorizing the descriptive verb form is more efficient than memorizing adjectives and verbs separately. Knowing the verb allows you to use it in sentences as a predicate (e.g., "The house is big") and as a modifier (e.g., "The big house").

---

### Basic Conjugation Rules
The process of turning a descriptive verb into an adjective starts with removing the dictionary ending **다**. The remaining part is the verb stem. The conjugation then depends on whether that stem ends in a vowel or a consonant.

If the stem ends in a vowel, you add **ㄴ** to the bottom of the stem. For example, the stem of **크다** is **크**. Since it ends in the vowel **ㅡ**, you add **ㄴ** to get **큰**. Another example provided is **친절하다 (to be kind)**, which becomes **친절한 (kind)**.

If the stem ends in a consonant, you add **은** after the stem. For example, the stem of **작다** is **작**. Since it ends in the consonant **ㄱ**, you add **은** to get **작은**. Another example is **좋다 (to be good)**, which becomes **좋은 (good)**.

> **철수 씨는 친절한 사람이에요.** / Chul-soo is a kind person.
> **정말 좋은 생각입니다.** / That's a really good idea!

---

### Irregular Conjugations: ㅂ and ㄹ
Some descriptive verbs follow irregular patterns when changing into adjectives. The instructor highlights two common cases: stems ending in **ㅂ** and stems ending in **ㄹ**.

For stems ending in a single **ㅂ**, you must first remove the **ㅂ**, then add the syllable **우**, and finally add **ㄴ** to the bottom of that **우**. The instructor uses **어렵다 (to be difficult)** as an example. Removing **다** leaves **어렵**. Removing the **ㅂ** leaves **어려**. Adding **우** and **ㄴ** results in **어려운**. Note that this rule does not apply to double consonants like in **예쁘다 (to be pretty)**, which follows the regular vowel rule to become **예쁜**.

> **어제 어려운 시험을 봤어요.** / Yesterday I had a difficult test.

For stems ending in **ㄹ**, the process is simpler: you remove the **ㄹ** and replace it with **ㄴ**. For example, **멀다 (to be far/distant)** has the stem **멀**. You remove the **ㄹ** and add **ㄴ** to get **먼**.

> **먼 나라로 여행 가고 싶어요.** / I want to travel to a distant country.

---

### Verbs Ending in 있다
A special rule applies to descriptive verbs that end in **있다 (to exist/to have)**, such as **맛있다 (to be delicious)** or **재미있다 (to be interesting)**. Even though **있다** technically functions as an action verb in some contexts, it appears frequently in descriptive expressions. To turn these into adjectives, **있다** always becomes **있는**. Therefore, **맛있다** becomes **맛있는**.

> **맛있는 음식을 먹고 싶어요.** / I want to eat delicious food.
