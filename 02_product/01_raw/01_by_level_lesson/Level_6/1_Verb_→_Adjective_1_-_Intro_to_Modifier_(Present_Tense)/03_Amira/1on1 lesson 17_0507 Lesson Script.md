**Slide 2: Topics**

Alright, today we’re going to talk about how we change verbs into other forms.

First, changing a verb into an adjective — so it can describe a noun.
Things like:
“A person who studies”
“A puppy that is cute”
These are called modifiers. And then we’ll look at some common words that follow them.

Second, we’ll look at how to turn a verb into a noun — called nominalization.
Like “to do” → “doing,” or “to work” → “working.”

There are actually three forms of nominalization, but we’ll focus on two:
**~는 것** and **~기**
And we’ll learn some common expressions that use these forms.

Let’s get into it!

**Slide 3: What is a modifier? Verb → Adj.**

"Modifier" is just a fancy name for an adjective — but only when it directly describes a noun.
Like: *“A cat that is cute”* or a cute cat and *“A man who goes home.”*

(Showing the table)

And this is how you make it. And you’re probably familiar with this already because it follows the exact same rule as the connector **~데**, like in *하는데* or *쉬운데*, just without the **데**.

So once again, whether you use **은**, **ㄴ**, or **는** depends on the type of verb.

Let’s first look at descriptive verbs.

**Slide 4: Descriptive Verbs - Modifiers - Present**

The rule is simple.

🔹 If the stem **ends in a 받침**, add **은**.
작다 becomes **작은** → *작은 컵*, a small cup.
짧다 becomes **짧은** → *짧은 치마*, a short skirt.

🔹 If the stem **ends in a vowel**, add **ㄴ**.
크다 becomes **큰** → *큰 박스*, a big box.
웃기다 becomes **웃긴** → *웃긴 남자*, a funny man.

🔹 이다 becomes **인**.
This is useful for phrases like *흰색인 것* → “The thing that is white.” Or more naturally, something white.

a few exceptions.

1. **ㄹ-ending verbs**: Remove ㄹ and add ㄴ.
   멀다 becomes **먼** → *먼 곳*, a far place.
2. **ㅂ-ending verbs**: Replace ㅂ with **우**, then add ㄴ.
   춥다 becomes **추운** → *추운 날씨*, cold weather.
3. **ㅎ-ending verbs**: Remove ㅎ, and add ㄴ.
   어떻다 becomes **어떤** → *어떤 음악*, which kind of music.
   This is where the question word **어떤** comes from!

✅ One thing to note: **좋다** is **regular** — It becomes **좋은** → *좋은 사람*, a good person.

🔸And **있다** and **없다** are exceptions, too. You just add **는**
So they become **있는** and **없는**.
*거기 있는 펜* → the pen that is there
*아무도 없는 곳* → a place where nobody is.

Let’s look at action verbs next.

**Slide 5: Action Verbs – Modifiers- Present**

받침 doesn’t matter for action verbs.
You just add **는** to the stem. And that’s it. Super simple.

So, 좋아하다 becomes **좋아하는**
*내가 요즘 가장 좋아하는 노래는 핑크 플로이드의 “Coming Back to Life”야*
→ The song that I like the most these days is “Coming Back to Life” by Pink Floyd.

We don’t really have a word for “favorite.” So we just say “the thing I like the most” — **가장** or **제일 좋아하는**. You’ve actually used this before, remember? way back in the beginning of our lessons. Well, this is where it comes from.

And 찾다 becomes **찾는**
*지도로 식당을 찾는 중이야*
→ I’m in the middle of looking for a restaurant with a map.

Recognize this? **~는 중이다?** Here **찾는** is modifying **중** — “the middle” And this is we make it.

There’s one exception though with verbs ending in ㄹ. Drop ㄹ and add **는**.

알다 becomes **아는**
*내가 아는 사람이 있는데, 그 분이 도와주실 거야*
→ There’s someone I know who’ll help you.

팔다 becomes **파는**
*비가 와서 양말이 다 젖었어요. 근처에 양말을 파는 데가 있나요?*
→ It rained, so my socks got all wet. Is there a place nearby that sells socks?

Slide 6: **곳 vs 데 Place where \_\_\_**

Now, **곳** and **데** both mean “place” and they show up a lot after modifiers — but they’re used a little differently.

곳 specifically refers to a physical location.
For example:
*제가 사는 곳은 날씨가 좋아요* → “The place I live has good weather.”

You can also use 이 / 그 / 저 with it, like:
*이 곳이 제가 자는 방이에요* → “This place is the room I sleep in.”
And it has exactly the same meaning as 여기 / 거기 / 저기. Here, there and over there

Now, **데** can also mean a location, just like 곳 —
But the key difference is that **데** can also refer to **situations**.

That’s because in Korean, we often treat situations “like places.”
And when that happens, we use **데**, not **곳**.

데 is actually a bigger topic, but I’m just giving you a quick overview here so you can start recognizing it in context — especially when you’re doing listening or reading practice.

Let’s look at this example: *머리 아픈 데에 먹는 약 있어요?
→ “Do you have medicine to take for when your head hurts?”*

By the way, we drop the particle 에 most of the time because 데에 sounds repetitive

If you said **곳** here instead, it would sound like:
“Is there medicine **at a place where** my head hurts?” — which doesn’t make any sense*.*

Another one:
*살 빼는 데 좋은 음식*
→ Food that is good **for losing weight**.
Here, **데** refers to the **purpose**, not a location.

And one last thing you **can’t** use **이 / 그 / 저** with **데**.

So, *이 데가 제가 자는 방이에요* ❌ is wrong.

Now, the difference between these two forms is very subtle — Kind of like **“moving” and “to move.”**

**Slide 9: ~는 것(거) VS ~기 Comparison**

Now let’s compare **~는 거** and **~기**

They *can* be interchangeable sometimes — but not always.

The difference comes down to **nuance**. And it’s really subtle — even for native speakers.

And I will do my best to help you see the difference but don’t worry if it doesn’t make total sense at first.

Instead, focus on learning them through **fixed expressions**, just like you did with phrases like ~기 싫다 and ~기 때문에.

You’ll also see a lot of **contracted forms** today, so let’s quickly go over those:

* **것은 → 건**
* **것이 → 게**
* **것을 → 걸**
* **기를 → 길**

1. ~는 거 (것)

Now, 거 means “thing,” right? so ~는 거 literally means “the thing that \_\_\_.” And it refers to the idea, thought, fact, or concept of doing something.

저는 음악을 듣는 걸 좋아해요 → I like listening to music.

So here, it’s about the whole idea of listening to music.

Or you can think of it like “Listening to music is a thing that I like”

And 기 feels more like an **activity** or **task.** It sounds like it will happen in the near future.

**And I call this the “listing” form because you’ll see it in:**

* To-do lists
* Instructions or signs
* And tests

**저는 음악을 듣기를 좋아해요**
→ I like to listen to music. I like listening to music as an activity.

And this is my inbox and on the bottom it says 더 보기 right?

더 means more and 보기 comes from 보다, so it means see more. This is a task so 더 보는 거wouldn’t work here

Now, if you compare these two sentences, there’s almost no difference in **just like English**:

* “I like listening to music”
* “I like to listen to music”

But they’re not always interchangeable like this.

**Slide 10: ~는 것(거) VS ~기 Feel the difference 1**

So, someone asks 왜 여자친구한테 전화를 안 해요? Why don’t you call your girlfriend?

Now, you can answer this in two different ways — and both are correct, but the meaning is slightly different.

If you say, 여자친구한테 전화하*는 게* 무서워요, **전화하는 거** refers to the whole idea or concept of calling her — not just the task.

Maybe she’s yelled at me before, or maybe she talks forever and just won’t hang up.

Whatever the reason, what’s scary is **the whole idea of talking to her on the phone**.

So, it means I’m scared *of calling* my girlfriend

And if you said 여자친구한테 전화하기(가) 무서워요. **전화하기** refers to the **task itself**.

So, here I’m just scared to call her at that moment.

Now, **“I’m scared of calling her”** and **“I’m scared to call her”** mean two completely different things in English.
So this could be a great example to help you feel the difference between **~는 거** and **~기**.

But — and this is important — Don’t make the mistake of thinking that “~는 거 = doing” and “~기 = to do.” It doesn’t work like that. Korean doesn’t match English one-to-one that way.

And before we move on, on a side note, **무섭다** means both “scared” and “scary.”

So does **지루하다** — it can mean both “bored” and “boring.”

Korean doesn’t make that distinction the way English does. So, you have to look at the **context** to figure out the intended meaning.

**Slide 11: ~는 것(거) VS ~기 Feel the difference 2**

Let’s look at couple more examples.

이 카페에서 책 읽는 게 좋아요.

책 읽는 거 refers to the act of reading at this place. You enjoy **the act** of reading here. So, this sentence is more **subjective** — it’s about **how you feel** about reading in this café.

이 카페에서 책 읽기가 좋아요.

Now, this one sounds more **objective**.
You’re not just saying *you* like reading here — you’re saying this **café itself** is a good place **for the activity of reading**.

I want you to remember this ~기 좋다 form as a whole, which means it is good to, as in something being suitable. So, here, you’re saying “This café is suitable for 책 읽기, the activity of reading.”

So again — same verb, but different **perspective**.

One’s about **your personal feelings**, and the other’s about the **quality** of the activity itself.

Let’s try another pair.

**3. 사람들 앞에서 말하는 게 어려워요**

→ Speaking in front of people is difficult (for me).

Here, **말하는 게** refers to **the idea of public speaking itself**, so it emphasizes your **personal struggle**.
Maybe you feel nervous, or shy, so it’s difficult for you personally.

**4. 사람들 앞에서 말하기(가) 어려워요**

→ It’s difficult to speak in front of people.

But this time, it’s not necessarily about your feelings. You’re just saying that **public speaking as a task** is hard. This is a more **objective** description of the **activity** itself.

**Slide 12: ~는 것(거) VS ~기 – Feel the Difference 3**

Alright, let’s tackle one more example

First up:

**저는 놀리는 게 싫어요** → *I don’t like teasing (in general).*

* Here, **놀리는 게** refers to the *concept* of teasing. And I don’t like the *idea* itself.
* **저는 놀리기가 싫어요** → *I don’t want to tease.*
  This time, it’s about **my action** — the **task or activity** of teasing.’

※ And We learned ~기 싫다 as a fixed expression for ‘don’t want to do.’ This is why it’s better to learn the form as a whole.

Now, what if you wanted to say, I don’t like people teasing me? We should add a full clause. 사람들이 저를 놀리다 - People tease me.

* **저는 사람들이 절 놀리는 게 싫어요** ✅
  → *I don’t like people teasing me.*
  In that case, you should use 놀리는 게 to refer to the whole idea of people teasing me.
* **저는 사람들이 절 놀리기가 싫어요** ❌
  Which is why this one is wrong because **놀리기** refers to the activity of teasing. So, it’s like saying, ‘I don’t want to *do* the activity people teasing me’ — which makes no sense.

Got it? Great!

**Slide 13: When Only ~는 거 Is Acceptable**

So, we just learned that ~는 거 refers to the idea of an action.

So naturally, when it works like “what” – the thing that and “that” – the fact that in English, you can only use ~는 거, not ~기. Let’s look at 2 examples.

**네가 원하는 걸 모르겠어.** ✅ I don’t know what you want or more precisely, I don’t know the thing that you want.

**나는 이미 티파니가 준호를 좋아하는 걸 알았어.** ✅

I already knew that or the fact that Tiffany likes 준호.

**Slide 14: ~는 거다 (것이다) – Adding Emphasis & Nuance**

You already know **~는 거**, but if you add **이다** (*to be*), you can add more emotion to your sentence.

Literally it means “It is that~” but it sort of has a similar feeling to **“So!” “The thing is…” “You see…” “What I’m saying is…” “Look” “Here’s the deal”**

So, you can use it when you’re telling a story like

*내가 어제 걔한테 전화를 했는데 안 받는 거야. 그래서 문자도 보냈어.*
→ You see I called him yesterday, but *he doesn’t answer*. So I texted him.”

**※ Why present tense? Just like in English! “So, this guy walks into a bar…” To grab attention**

근데 우리 내일 집에 몇 시에 가는 거예요?
**→ *(But wait, or but the thing is)* “What time are we going home tomorrow?”**

뭐 하시는 거예요? – This one is super common. And this one’s all about **how** you say because It can either make you sound more curious or aggressive

You can be like 뭐 하시는 거예요? Or 뭐 하시는 거예요!!

You can basically think of this form like a verbal emoji.

**Slide 15: ~는 것 같다 – Softening Opinions**

Now, 는 것 plus 같다 to be like, literally means [it’s like the thing that] but this form is often translated as “I think” even though there’s no “I think” in this form.

You can think of this form as “It seems like” and this is a very common way to express your opinion.

And I think this is a great example of how language shows culture. In Korea, and in many Asian countries, people value group over individual opinions.

So ~는 것 같다 softens your tone like “It seems like~” rather than literally saying “내가 생각해요” “I” think, like it’s MY opinion. And I think Koreans overuse this form even for things that are factual. So, you’ll hear it everywhere.

**제 남동생은 이성 친구가 많은 것 같아요.**
→ *“It seems like my younger brother has many friends of the opposite sex.”*

**엄마가 하는 음식이 좋아서 집에 자주 가는 것 같아.**
→ *“I guess I go home often because I like Mom’s cooking.”*

**제가 케이팝을 잘 모르는 이유는 티비를 보지 않기 때문인 것 같아요**

**I think** the reason why I don’t know much about K-pop is because I don’t watch TV

By the way, to modify a reason, always use 이유 not 때문.

**Slide 16: Fixed forms with ~기**

Now, there’s a lot of fixed ~ 기 forms just like 기 싫다, 기 때문에.

~는 거 doesn’t work here. At all. So, for now, forget about ~는 거 and treat these ~기 forms like English idioms.

For example:

* Why do we say *‘listen****to****music’* and not *‘listen music’*? I have no clue! But we know it as one phrase.
* “might as well” is just impossible to translate in Korean but I know its meaning by heart

Same with these ~기 forms!

So, we’re going to learn a couple new ones today — **plus their opposites** that *don’t* use ~기. Don’t think about *why* they work this way. Just learn **the whole phrase.**

**Slide 17: ~기 전 & ~은/ㄴ 후 – Before & After**

**전** = *before*, **후** = *after*. They’re **ordinary nouns** referring to the moments before and after respectively. So you can use them with **time markers** (부터, 까지, 에):

* **일요일 전에 전화 주세요**

**- Call me before Sunday**

* **30분 후에 저녁을 먹을 거예요**

**- We’ll eat dinner after(in) 30 minutes**

* **그 전부터 계속 너를 좋아했어**

**- I’ve always liked you since before that**

**1. ~기 전 = Before Doing**

* **Rule: Action Verb Stem + 기 전에**
* **Example:**
* **아빠는 식사하기 전에 항상 기도를 하세요.
  → *“Dad always prays before eating.”***
* **운동하기 전에 아무것도 먹지 마세요**

**- Don’t eat anything before working out**

* **저는 결혼하기 전에 돈부터 많이 벌고 싶어요**

**- I want to make a lot of money first before marrying**

When 부터 marks a thing, it means this first before anything else.

Now after doing **Rule** is simple

* + 받침 O → **은 후에**
  + 받침 X → **ㄴ 후에**
* **밥을 먹은 후에 양치를 하세요**

**- Brush your teeth after eating**

* **수업이 끝난 후부터 밤까지 계속 공부했어요**

**- I kept studying until nighttime after class ended.**

**은/ㄴ** is a past tense modifier. It’s like saying *“the thing that****happened****”*

**Slide 18: ~기로 하다 – Making Decisions & Promises**

**1. What Does ~기로 하다 Mean?**

* + The particle **로** here marks a choice, like choosing options:
    - **스테이크는 미디움으로 할게요.** → *“I’ll have the steak medium.”*
* So **~기로 하다** means to set an action as a choice or to choose to do an action. That’s why it’s often translated as “decide to” or “supposed to” because it establishes a plan, choice, or commitment.
  + And it’s usually used in the past tense like these two sentences.

**우리는 다음 주에 만나기로 했어요.** → *“We decided to meet next week.”*

친구랑 **등산가기로 했는데** 다리를 다쳤어요 - I was **supposed to go hiking** with

a friend but I hurt my leg.

To say decide not to you can use either one like 안 하기로 하다 or 하지 않기로 하다

**And when it’s used in Present Tense, it’s usually suggestive** like “Let’s”

* **차를 갖고 와서 운전해야 되니까 술은 안 마시기로 해요**
* **차를 갖고 와서 운전해야 되니까 술은 마시지 않기로 해요**

**- Let’s [agree] not to drink because I have to drive since I brought a car**

**Alternatively, you can use these verbs to clarify the *type* of commitment:**

**Let’s look at this one 마음을 먹다. 마음 means mind or heart but this doesn’t mean to eat a heart.**

**It means to make up one’s mind. Super common expression.**

**드디어 유학가기로 마음먹었어요**

**I finally made up my mind to go abroad to study**

**So you can use ~기로 하다 to:**

* **Formalize decisions (*“We chose to…”*).**
* **Make promises (*“I swore to…”*).**
* **Suggest plans (*“Let’s agree to…”*).**

**Slide 19: ~기 시작하다 / 그만 – Starting & Stopping Actions**

**This is the last ~기 form for today. To start doing something and to stop doing something**

**1. ~기 시작하다 = To Start Doing**
