**~는 것 같다 – Softening Opinions**

Now, 는 것 plus 같다 to be like, literally means [it’s like the thing that] but this form is often translated as “I think” even though there’s no “I think” in this form.

You can think of this form as “It seems like” and this is a very common way to express your opinion.

And I think this is a great example of how language shows culture. In Korea, and in many Asian countries, people value group over individual opinions.

So ~는 것 같다 softens your tone like “It seems like~” rather than literally saying “내가 생각해요” “I” think, like it’s MY opinion. And I think Koreans overuse this form even for things that are factual. So, you’ll hear it everywhere.

**제 남동생은 이성 친구가 많은 것 같아요.**
→ *“It seems like my younger brother has many friends of the opposite sex.”*

**엄마가 하는 음식이 좋아서 집에 자주 가는 것 같아.**
→ *“I guess I go home often because I like Mom’s cooking.”*

**제가 케이팝을 잘 모르는 이유는 티비를 보지 않기 때문인 것 같아요**

**I think** the reason why I don’t know much about K-pop is because I don’t watch TV

By the way, to modify a reason, always use 이유 not 때문.

**Slide 16: Fixed forms with ~기**

Now, there’s a lot of fixed ~ 기 forms just like 기 싫다, 기 때문에.

~는 거 doesn’t work here. At all. So, for now, forget about ~는 거 and treat these ~기 forms like English idioms.

For example:

* Why do we say *‘listen****to****music’* and not *‘listen music’*? I have no clue! But we know it as one phrase.
* “might as well” is just impossible to translate in Korean but I know its meaning by heart

Same with these ~기 forms!

So, we’re going to learn a couple new ones today — **plus their opposites** that *don’t* use ~기. Don’t think about *why* they work this way. Just learn **the whole phrase.**

**Slide 17: ~기 전 & ~은/ㄴ 후 – Before & After**

**전** = *before*, **후** = *after*. They’re **ordinary nouns** referring to the moments before and after respectively. So you can use them with **time markers** (부터, 까지, 에):

* **일요일 전에 전화 주세요**

**- Call me before Sunday**

* **30분 후에 저녁을 먹을 거예요**

**- We’ll eat dinner after(in) 30 minutes**

* **그 전부터 계속 너를 좋아했어**

**- I’ve always liked you since before that**

**1. ~기 전 = Before Doing**

* **Rule: Action Verb Stem + 기 전에**
* **Example:**
* **아빠는 식사하기 전에 항상 기도를 하세요.
  → *“Dad always prays before eating.”***
* **운동하기 전에 아무것도 먹지 마세요**

**- Don’t eat anything before working out**

* **저는 결혼하기 전에 돈부터 많이 벌고 싶어요**

**- I want to make a lot of money first before marrying**

When 부터 marks a thing, it means this first before anything else.

Now after doing **Rule** is simple

* + 받침 O → **은 후에**
  + 받침 X → **ㄴ 후에**
* **밥을 먹은 후에 양치를 하세요**

**- Brush your teeth after eating**

* **수업이 끝난 후부터 밤까지 계속 공부했어요**

**- I kept studying until nighttime after class ended.**

**은/ㄴ** is a past tense modifier. It’s like saying *“the thing that****happened****”*

**Slide 18: ~기로 하다 – Making Decisions & Promises**

**1. What Does ~기로 하다 Mean?**

* + The particle **로** here marks a choice, like choosing options:
    - **스테이크는 미디움으로 할게요.** → *“I’ll have the steak medium.”*
* So **~기로 하다** means to set an action as a choice or to choose to do an action. That’s why it’s often translated as “decide to” or “supposed to” because it establishes a plan, choice, or commitment.
  + And it’s usually used in the past tense like these two sentences.

**우리는 다음 주에 만나기로 했어요.** → *“We decided to meet next week.”*

친구랑 **등산가기로 했는데** 다리를 다쳤어요 - I was **supposed to go hiking** with

a friend but I hurt my leg.

To say decide not to you can use either one like 안 하기로 하다 or 하지 않기로 하다

**And when it’s used in Present Tense, it’s usually suggestive** like “Let’s”

* **차를 갖고 와서 운전해야 되니까 술은 안 마시기로 해요**
* **차를 갖고 와서 운전해야 되니까 술은 마시지 않기로 해요**

**- Let’s [agree] not to drink because I have to drive since I brought a car**

**Alternatively, you can use these verbs to clarify the *type* of commitment:**

**Let’s look at this one 마음을 먹다. 마음 means mind or heart but this doesn’t mean to eat a heart.**

**It means to make up one’s mind. Super common expression.**

**드디어 유학가기로 마음먹었어요**

**I finally made up my mind to go abroad to study**

**So you can use ~기로 하다 to:**

* **Formalize decisions (*“We chose to…”*).**
* **Make promises (*“I swore to…”*).**
* **Suggest plans (*“Let’s agree to…”*).**

**Slide 19: ~기 시작하다 / 그만 – Starting & Stopping Actions**

**This is the last ~기 form for today. To start doing something and to stop doing something**

**1. ~기 시작하다 = To Start Doing**

**Structure is simple: Action Verb Stem + 기 시작하다**

* **시작하다 means *“to begin”* and is often used in past tense.**

**올해부터 한국어를 배우기 시작했어요.

**줄(을) 알다 & 모르다**

Alright, the next form we’re going to learn is **ㄹ/을 줄 알다 and 모르다**. Many learners find this form a bit tricky because they don't know what "줄" means. But even Koreans don't know what "줄" means.

And that means you don’t need to, either.

"줄" is a **bound noun**. And you won't see "줄" in any other forms verbs besides 줄 알다 (to know) and 모르다 (to not know). And it only has two meanings: **"that"** or **"how."**

Since "줄" is a bound noun, it needs a **modifier** before it, just like any other noun. So, you'll see modifiers like ~는, ~ㄴ/은, ~ㄹ/을, or ~던 before "줄."

Note that ~던 줄 is almost never used.

The meaning of the entire form changes slightly depending on **the tense of the modifier** before "줄," and also on **the tense of 알다 or 모르다** itself.

Also, the meaning of 알다 can sometimes be interpreted as "think that" rather than the literal "know that," depending on the context. The keyword here is **"expectation."**

You'll see how the meaning of the entire form changes by looking at the table on the slide. I'll give examples for each case.

**ㄹ/을 줄(을) 알다 & 모르다 Future + 줄**

Let's first look at examples where the modifier's tense is **future tense** (~ㄹ/을 줄).

Okay, to make things easy, the only two cases where “줄” means “how” is when 알다 or 모르다 is in present or future tense.

This is one of the only two combinations where **"줄" means "how."**

* **Example 1:** 남자친구분 한국말 할 줄 아세요?
  + (Does your boyfriend know how to speak Korean?)
  + This is a common question you'd hear if your boyfriend is a foreigner. It literally means "Do your boyfriend know the *way/method* to speak Korean?"

**Example 2:** 열심히 배워서 아마 조금 할 줄 알 거예요.

* (He's worked hard, so he must know how to speak a little bit.)
* Here, 알 거예요 (future tense of 알다) expresses a guess or expectation that he *will know how to* speak.

These two examples are the only cases where "줄" means "how." In all other cases, "줄" means **"that."**

Now when “줄” means that, 알다 can be interpreted as "know" or "think," depending on the situation. The keyword for this form is **"expectation."** Just think: "What happened to your expectation?"

* If you expected it to rain, and it *does* rain as you expected, you'd say 비가 올 줄 알았어요. (I knew it would rain.) - Your expectation was met.
* If you expected it to rain, but it *doesn't* rain contrary to your expectation, you'd say 비가 올 줄 알았어요. (I thought it would rain.) - Your expectation was *not* met.

It's quite simple, isn't it?

There's a very common phrase: **그럴 줄 알았어!** (That's what I thought! / I knew it!) Sometimes, it's even used like "I told you so!"

**ㄹ/을 줄(을) 알다 & 모르다 Future + 줄 Examples**

해당 슬라이드 내에 예문 5개 추가 하지만 문장 설명은 2개만 하고 넘어가기.

 저는 운전할 줄 몰라요. (I don't know how to drive.)

* Here, 운전할 줄 means "the way to drive" or "how to drive."

 내일 시험이 어려울 줄 알았어요. (I knew/thought the exam would be difficult tomorrow.)

* This depends on whether the exam actually *was* difficult. If it was, you "knew." If it wasn't, you "thought."

 그 사람이 그렇게 빨리 올 줄 몰랐어요. (I didn't know/think that person would come so quickly.)

* This expresses surprise because your expectation (that they wouldn't come quickly) was not met.

 아미라 씨가 이렇게 한국말을 잘 할 줄 몰랐어요! (Amira, I didn't know you could speak Korean this well! / I didn't think you would speak Korean this well!)

* This expresses surprise or a revised expectation based on your current observation of Amira's Korean skills.

 그 영화가 그렇게 슬플 줄 몰랐어요. (I didn't know/think that movie would be so sad.)

* You went in with an expectation (e.g., it wouldn't be that sad), and that expectation was not met.

**Present / Past + 줄(을) 알다 & 모르다 - Present / Past + 줄 Examples**

Alright, now let's look at the remaining cases: when the modifier is in **Present tense** or **Past tense** before "줄."

In all these other cases, "줄" means **"that."** It doesn't matter if the modifier is in present tense or past tense. So, their meanings should be pretty straight forward.

And the meaning of 알다 and 모르다 can still be interpreted as "know" or "think," depending on the context.

I'll explain this further as we go through the examples. Just stop me anytime if there’s anything unclear.

Present + 줄 문장 6개 [Descriptive verb 3개 / Action verb 3개]

* 그 사람이 착한 줄 알았어요. → I knew/thought that person was kind.

▶ If they were kind, you “knew.” If they turned out not to be kind, you “thought.”

* 이 문제가 쉬운 줄 알았는데 아니네요. → I thought this problem was easy, but it wasn’t.

▶ Your expectation (that it was easy) was not met.

* 아미라 씨가 한국어를 좋아하는 줄 알아요. → I know that Amira likes Korean.

▶ This is a fact. So, I “know” you like Korean

* 친구가 지금 자는 줄 알았어요. → I **knew/thought** my friend was sleeping now.

▶ If they were sleeping, you "knew." If they weren't, you "thought."

* 엄마가 요리하는 줄 알았는데 TV를 보고 있었어요. → I thought Mom was cooking, but she was watching TV.

▶ Your expectation (that she was cooking) was not met.

Past + 줄 문장 6개 [Descriptive verb 3개 / Action verb 3개]

**Past + 줄**

* 그녀가 이미 도착한 줄 알았어요. - I knew she had already arrived.

▶ 이미 means “Already” So, you “knew” she had arrived.

* 친구가 어제 집에 간 줄 알았어요. - I knew/thought my friend went home yesterday.

▶ If they did go home, you "knew." If they didn't, you "thought."

* 시험이 쉬웠던 줄 알았는데 점수가 안 좋네요. - I thought the exam was easy, but my score isn't good.

▶ Your expectation (that it was easy) was not met.

* 그 가수가 데뷔한 줄 몰랐어요. - I didn't know that singer had debuted.

▶ This expresses surprise or new information.
