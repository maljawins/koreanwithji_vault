## Lesson Overview
Continuing from Part 1, where Ji explained the nuances between **~는 것** and **~기** using examples of fear and difficulty, this part expands on specific contexts where only one form is acceptable. Ji then introduces the emphatic sentence ending **~는 거다 (것이다)** and the soft opinion-marker **~는 것 같다**. The lesson concludes by transitioning into fixed grammatical patterns that utilize the **~기** nominalization, specifically focusing on "before" and "after" actions. This bridges the conceptual discussion of Part 1 with the practical, fixed expressions that Amira will practice in Part 3.

## Teaching Points
*   **Exclusive Usage of ~는 것:**
    *   Used when the meaning is "What" (the thing that): *네가 원하는 걸 모르겠어* (I don't know what you want).
    *   Used when the meaning is "That" (the fact that): *티파니가 준호를 좋아하는 걸 알았어* (I knew that Tiffany likes Joonho).
*   **Emphatic Ending ~는 거다 (것이다):**
    *   Adds emotional weight or clarifies a situation.
    *   Functions like a "verbal emoji" to imply there is more to the story or to grab attention.
*   **Softening Opinions with ~는 것 같다:**
    *   Literally "It is like the thing that...", used to express "I think" or "It seems" without being too assertive.
    *   Reflects Korean cultural values of group harmony over strong individual "I" statements.
*   **Time-based Modifiers:**
    *   **~기 전:** Before doing (Action Verb Stem + 기 전에).
    *   **~은/ㄴ 후:** After doing (Action Verb Stem + 은/ㄴ 후에). Note that "after" uses the past tense modifier form.

## Lesson Flow
(Planned Flow)
Ji begins by clarifying that while **~는 것** and **~기** are often interchangeable, **~는 것** is mandatory when translating "what" or "that" in English clauses. He provides examples like "I know that you like him" to show that these represent facts or concepts rather than tasks.

Next, Ji introduces **~는 거다**. He explains that adding the copula **이다** to the nominalized verb creates an emphatic tone. He describes it as a storytelling device—similar to how English speakers use the present tense to tell a joke ("So this guy walks into a bar...")—to make the narrative more engaging.

The lesson then covers **~는 것 같다**. Ji explains that Koreans rarely use the verb "to think" (**생각하다**) for opinions, preferring the softer "it seems like" to avoid sounding too direct. He notes a common pronunciation shift where **같아** sounds like **같애**.

Finally, Ji introduces the first set of fixed **~기** expressions: **~기 전** (before) and **~은/ㄴ 후** (after). He highlights the grammatical logic: "before" uses the nominalized task form, while "after" uses the past tense modifier because the action is completed. This leads directly into the final set of expressions (deciding, starting, stopping) covered in Part 3.

## Student Notes
*   **Cultural Insight:** Use **~는 것 같다** frequently to sound more natural and polite. It softens the blow of an opinion and makes you sound less "bossy" or assertive.
*   **Grammar Rule:** When using "because" with a reason, always use **이유** (reason) as the noun being modified (e.g., *~는 이유는 ~기 때문이에요*).
*   **Pronunciation:** Be aware that many native speakers pronounce **~것 같아** as **~것 같애**.
*   **Time Markers:** Remember that **전** (before) and **후** (after) are nouns. They can take particles like **에**, **부터**, or **까지**.
