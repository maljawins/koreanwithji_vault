## Teaching Points

### Overview
This lesson introduces a highly common way to express thoughts, opinions, or soft assertions in Korean using the structure ~것 같다. While learners may already be familiar with the verb 생각하다 (to think), the instructor emphasizes that ~것 같다 is the preferred form for spoken conversation, whereas 생각하다 is more common in formal writing. Using ~것 같다 allows a speaker to sound less blunt and more polite by framing their statement as an "appearance" or "likelihood" rather than a hard fact.

The core of this lesson involves a complex set of conjugations. To use ~것 같다, one must first transform a verb—whether it is an action verb or a descriptive verb—into an adjective form. This transformation varies significantly based on the type of verb and the intended tense (past, present, or future). The instructor provides a comprehensive breakdown of these adjective-making rules, covering regular and irregular forms, to ensure students can accurately express thoughts like "I think it is good," "I think he ate," or "I think it will be spicy."

---

### The Basic Structure of ~것 같다
The instructor explains that the basic formula for this expression is to take a verb, change it into its adjective form, and then add 것 (thing/fact) or the shortened 거, followed by the descriptive verb 같다 (to be like/to seem). When combined, the phrase literally translates to "It is like the thing that [verb]," which functions as "I think..." or "It seems that..." in English. For example, using a descriptive verb in the present tense, one would say 좋은 것 같아요 (I think it is good) or 너무 비싼 것 같아요 (I think it is too expensive).

---

### Conjugating Descriptive Verbs into Adjectives
The rules for changing descriptive verbs into adjectives depend on the tense. For the present tense, the instructor reviews the standard rule: attach 은 to a verb stem ending in a consonant or 니은 (ㄴ) to a stem ending in a vowel. Irregularities include removing a final ㄹ before adding 니은 (e.g., 길다 becomes 긴) or changing a final ㅂ to 우 before adding 니은 (e.g., 맵다 becomes 매운).

For the future tense, the meaning shifts to "a noun that will be [descriptive]." The rule is to attach 을 to stems ending in a consonant or ㄹ to stems ending in a vowel. If the stem ends in ㄹ, it remains as is (e.g., 길다 becomes 길). If it ends in ㅂ, the ㅂ changes to 우 before adding ㄹ (e.g., 맵다 becomes 매울). Example sentences include 좋을 것 같아요 (I think it will be good) and 조금 매울 것 같아요 (I think it will be a little spicy).

For the past tense, meaning "a noun that was [descriptive]," the instructor introduces the 던 suffix. One must first conjugate the descriptive verb into the standard past tense, remove the final 다, and attach 던. Thus, 좋다 becomes 좋았던, 길다 becomes 길었던, and 맵다 becomes 매웠던. This results in sentences like 좋았던 것 같아요 (I think it was good) or 조금 매웠던 것 같아요 (I think it was a little spicy).

---

### Conjugating Action Verbs into Adjectives
Action verbs follow a different set of rules for adjective transformation. In the present tense, which describes someone who "does" something, the rule is to simply attach 는 to the verb stem. If the stem ends in ㄹ, the ㄹ is removed before adding 는 (e.g., 살다 becomes 사는). Examples include 김치를 좋아하는 것 같아요 (I think [he] likes kimchi) and 점심을 먹는 것 같아요 (I think [he] is eating lunch).

In the future tense, describing someone who "will do" something, the rule mirrors the descriptive future: attach 을 to consonant stems or ㄹ to vowel stems. Stems ending in ㅂ change to 우 before adding ㄹ. This allows for sentences like 김치를 좋아할 것 같아요 (I think [he] will like kimchi) or 점심을 먹을 것 같아요 (I think [he] will eat lunch).

In the past tense, describing someone who "did" something, action verbs use the 은/ㄴ suffix (which is the present tense rule for descriptive verbs). Attach 은 to consonant stems or 니은 (ㄴ) to vowel stems. Again, ㄹ stems drop the ㄹ, and ㅂ stems change to 우 before adding 니은. Examples include 김치를 좋아한 것 같아요 (I think [he] liked kimchi) and 점심을 먹은 것 같아요 (I think [he] ate lunch).

---

### Important Exceptions and Nuances
The instructor highlights that the verbs 있다 (to be/to have) and 없다 (to not be/to not have) are exceptions in the present tense, conjugating as 있는 and 없는. However, in the past and future tenses, they follow the standard rules: 있었던/없었던 for the past and 있을/없을 for the future.

Additionally, the instructor notes the usage of certain adverbs within these structures. 너무 (too much) is often used in informal speech to simply mean "very" or "so" for exaggeration. 그렇게 (like that/that) is frequently placed before adjectives to mean "that [adjective]," such as 그렇게 맛있는 (that delicious). Finally, the instructor clarifies that negative thoughts are formed by using the 지 않다 structure; for descriptive verbs, this becomes 지 않은 것 같다, while for action verbs, it becomes 지 않는 것 같다.
