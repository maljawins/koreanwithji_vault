## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces two primary ways to express "I think" in Korean: the indirect and common `~것 같다` form, and the more direct `~고 생각하다` form. The lesson focuses on the grammatical construction of these forms, their nuanced differences in meaning, and specific conjugation rules for adjectives, action verbs, and the copula `이다 (to be)`.

The learning arc begins with the literal meaning of the word `같다 (to be the same/like)` and expands into how it functions as a grammatical marker for thoughts and suppositions. The instructor then contrasts this with the literal verb for "to think," `생각하다`, explaining when to use each based on the desired level of directness and the tense of the sentence.

---

### The Core Meaning of 같다
Before using it to express thoughts, it is essential to understand that `같다` is a verb that means "to be the same" or "to be like." The instructor demonstrates this by comparing objects, such as saying a specific blue color is the same as the blue on the Korean flag, or an eraser is like a rectangle. When used in the "I think" grammar pattern, this literal meaning of "it is like" or "it seems like" persists, making the expression more indirect than the English "I think."

---

### Expressing Thoughts with ~것 같다
The most common way to say "I think" is using the structure: **Adjective + 것 같다**. The instructor explains that `것` means "thing" or "fact," so the literal translation is "It is like the thing/fact that..." This form requires an adjective, which can be derived from either a descriptive verb or an action verb.

To say "I think it is cold today," you take the descriptive verb `춥다 (to be cold)`, turn it into the adjective `추운 (cold)`, and add `것 같다`. This results in:
> **추운 것 같아요.** / I think it's cold. (Literally: It seems like it's cold.)

For action verbs, the instructor notes that you must use the `~는` adjective marker. For example, to say "I think I like BTS," you use `좋아하다 (to like)` to create the phrase:
> **BTS 좋아하는 것 같아요.** / I think I like BTS.

A pronunciation tip is provided: while written as `같아요`, speakers in Seoul often pronounce it as `같애요`. However, the instructor warns that writing it as `같애요` is grammatically incorrect.

---

### Direct Thinking with 생각하다
The second method uses the verb `생각하다 (to think)`. This is more direct and specifically identifies the statement as your personal thought or opinion. The structure is: **Plain Form + 고 생각하다**.

The instructor highlights a specific exception for the copula `이다 (to be)`. Instead of the standard plain form + `고`, it becomes `이라고` after a consonant or `라고` after a vowel. To say "I think Billy is a Korean," you would say:
> **빌리가 한국 사람이라고 생각해요.** / I think Billy is a Korean.

This form is also useful for expressing past thoughts ("I thought..."), which is done by conjugating the end to `생각했어요`.

---

### Nuances and Tense Restrictions
There are critical differences between the two forms. `~것 같다` is generally restricted to the present tense when used to mean "I think." Using it in the past or future tense to express a thought can sound awkward. If you need to say "I thought" or "I will think," `생각하다` is the appropriate choice.

Furthermore, the instructor explains how to handle negative thoughts. In English, we often say "I don't think it is..." In Korean, it is much more natural to say "I think it is not..." using the negative adjective form. For example:
> **빌리가 한국 사람이 아닌 것 같아요.** / I think Billy is not a Korean. (Natural)
> **빌리가 한국 사람인 것 같지 않아요.** / I don't think Billy is a Korean. (Used only for strong emphasis)

---

### Alternative: 제 생각에는
As an alternative to complex grammar patterns, the instructor introduces the phrase `제 생각에는`, which means "In my thoughts" or "In my opinion." This can be placed at the beginning of any sentence to frame it as a personal thought without needing to change the verb ending to a specific "think" form.
> **제 생각에는 빌리가 한국 사람이에요.** / In my opinion, Billy is a Korean.
