**Transitive & Intransitive verbs**

Alright, we've gone over your assignment, and great job as always!

Now, we're going to talk about **"Types of Verbs."**

To understand the different types of verbs, we first need to understand this core idea: Can a verb take an object?

**If you think about raise and rise.**

**You can “raise” a glass, with glass being the object but you can’t rise a glass. It can only just “rise” on its own.**

**This is the difference between Transitive verbs and Intransitive verbs.**

* **Transitive verbs can take an object.**
* **Intransitive verbs can't take an object. They describe an action that happens on its own, or a state of being. So, all descriptive verbs are intransitive.**

**If you go to the Naver dictionary and switch it to 국어사전 (Korean dictionary), it will tell you if the verb is 타동사 (transitive) or 자동사 (intransitive). You don't need to memorize these Korean terms, but it's important that you understand the *concept*.**

**Passive Voice**

Now that we understand transitive and intransitive verbs, let's talk about **Passive Voice**.

In English, we usually use "be" verbs and add suffixes like "-en" or "-ed" to make a verb passive, like "The kid *was bitten*."

But in Korean, the verb itself actually changes its dictionary form to become passive like 물다 and 물리다.

And in passive voice, the particles change too! the subject does the action. But in passive voice, the subject is the **"RECEIVER"** of the action of a transitive verb. Now that the receiver is the subject, it’s typically marked by the subject marker, 이/가.

On a side note, you could also say 개한테 애가 물렸어요. Do you remember when we talked about word order, and how the word closer to the verb is usually the most important information?

So, this has a slightly different nuance than the other one. This might be said in response to 누가 물렸어요? (Who was bitten?), emphasizing 애가 (the kid).

**Particle By**

So, when we use passive voice, we often want to say *who or what* caused the action, right? In English, we use "by." Let's look at how to say "by" in Korean for passive sentences.

It depends on whether the "doer" of the action is a person/animal or a thing.

1. For **people & animals**, you use **에게** or **한테**.
   * For example, 저는 많은 분들에게 사랑받고 있어요. (I am being loved by many people.) 사랑하다 (to love) becomes 사랑받다 (to be loved).
2. For **things, organizations, or even insects**, you use **에**.
   * Like, 최근 이란이 미국에 공격당했어요. (Lately, Iran was attacked by the U.S.) 공격하다 (to attack) becomes 공격당하다 (to be attacked).
   * Or, 벌에 쏘이면 병원에 바로 가야 합니다. (If you're ever stung by a bee, you must immediately go to the hospital.) 쏘다 (to sting) becomes 쏘이다 (to be stung).

There's also 에 의해서, which means "by" or "by means of." But this is very formal and mostly used in written or official contexts. You'll rarely hear it in everyday spoken Korean.

**Passive Verb**

Now, here's a super important rule about passive verbs: **Only transitive verbs can be turned into passive verbs.**

Think about it logically: In an active sentence, the object receives the action, right? When we change it to passive, that original object becomes the *subject* of the passive sentence.

An intransitive verb, by definition, doesn't have an object. So, if there's no object in the active voice, there's nothing to become the subject in the passive voice! For example, 웃다 (to laugh) is an intransitive verb. You can't "laugh something." So, you can't say something like "Something is laughed by you." It just doesn't make sense!

And once a verb is turned into a passive verb, it technically becomes **intransitive “in principle” You’ll see what I mean by “in principle” later today.**

A passive verb take an object marker (을/를). For example, you can't say 애를 물렸어요 (X) like "The kid was bitten something." You just say 애가 물렸어요 (O) (The kid was bitten).

**Passive Voice usage**

In Korean, just like in English, we generally use active voice more often. However, there's a really interesting usage of passive voice in Korean that English doesn't really have: We use passive voice to express **"can" and "can't"**!

The key difference from using ~(으)ㄹ 수 없다 or 못 is that passive voice for "can't" puts the focus on the **situation or the "what"** rather than the "who." It's used when something is impossible not because of *your* fault or lack of ability, but because of an external factor.

Here are three very common examples:

1. **보다 (to see)** → **보이다 (to be seen / to be visible)**
   * Think of it as "to be visible."
   * Look at the slide: The person is saying, 여기 콘서트 장에 여자 때문에 뒷 사람이 안 보여요. (Because of the woman at the concert, the person behind me is not visible/I can't see.) It's not his fault he can't see the performance; it's that woman blocking the view!
2. **듣다 (to hear)** → **들리다 (to be heard / to be audible)**
   * Think of it as "to be audible."
   * The person with the megaphone is asking, 이제는 들리세요? (Can you hear me NOW? / Is it audible now?) They're asking if the sound is *audible* from your side.
3. **믿다 (to believe)** → **믿기다 (to be believed / to be unbelievable)**
   * This is often used to say "I can't believe it!" but with the nuance that the words themselves are just so unbelievable, not that *you* lack the ability to believe.
   * Example: 진짜? 안 믿겨. (Really? It's unbelievable.) It's not your fault you can't believe it; the information itself is hard to believe.

**Passive Verb Formation**

Now, I have to be honest with you: this topic is notoriously famous among Korean learners for being difficult!

That's because there are no clear, fixed rules for how to turn active verbs into passive verbs.

So, I highly recommend learning these verbs one by one, just like you learn any new word, and pay attention to how they’re used.

However, I can show you some common patterns. But you don’t really need to memorize these “rules”.

I’m just showing you these to help you **infer the meaning** when you come across passive verbs in your comprehensible input practice. Think of them as guidelines, not rules.

There are generally three main patterns for passive verb formation. We'll cover two of them today: the **이/리/히/기 pattern** and the **Noun + 하다 verb changing to 되다/받다/당하다**.

The third pattern, 아/어지다, we'll cover in more detail in a separate lesson because it's super versatile and deserves its own time.

The 이/리/히/기 pattern usually applies to **Pure Korean transitive verbs**.

Let's go through each of these suffixes (이, 리, 히, 기) and see which verbs they typically attach to.

**Stem + 이다**

This pattern is typically used when the verb stem **does end in a 받침**

**OR you'll also find some transitive verbs ending in ㅎ, ㅍ, or ㄲ batchims that follow this pattern, though there aren't as many of those.**

**For now, just focus on recognizing these patterns, and you can review the individual meanings of the verbs on your own later.**

**Stem + 리다**

Next, we have **Stem + 리다** pattern.

This applies when the active verb stem **ends with a ㄹ batchim**.

For most of these, you'll simply add 리다 to make them passive. Like 걸다 (to hang) becomes 걸리다 (to be hung).

You'll also see this 리다 pattern with some **ㄷ irregular verbs**. Remember those? The verbs where ㄷ changes to ㄹ when conjugated? They often follow this 리다 pattern for passive formation, too.

And finally, some **르 irregular verbs** also fit into this pattern.

So, you'll see a bit of a consistent pattern if you remember our irregular verb lessons!

**Stem + 히다**

This 히다 pattern commonly attaches to verb stems ending in ㄱ batchim, ㅈ batchim, ㅂ batchim, and also to **regular ㄷ verbs**.

For example, 적다 (to jot down) becomes 적히다 (to be jotted down). 막다 (to stop) becomes 막히다 (to be stopped/blocked). And 밟다 (to step on) becomes 밟히다 (to be stepped on).

You can see the various examples on the slide for each of these batchim types.

**Stem + 기다**

And finally, the 기다 pattern.

This one is pretty much for **all the rest of the batchim-ending verbs** that don't fit neatly into the 이, 리, or 히 categories.

Again, these are just common patterns to help you recognize and infer meanings. So, you don’t need to try to memorize every single rule.

But you can review these passive verb formation patterns in more detail. I’ll share a link to Vicky’s Youtube Video on this topic.

**Noun + 하다 Verb**

Turning Noun + 하다 verbs is a lot easier because it has a more logical pattern than the ones we just looked at.

In Vicky’s video, she’ll tell you that it’s about whether the subject is a person or not but it’s really not about that. It’s more about “Is the noun before 하다 something you can directly 'receive'?”

When the answer is no, you just replace 되다 with 하다.

For example, 준비, you don’t really receive preparation, right? Same with 취소, cancellation.

So, you get 준비되다 and 취소되다. And most noun+하다 verbs become noun+되다.

But when you can “receive” the noun, just use 받다 which literally means to receive.

사랑받다. You can receive love

존경받다. You can receive respect

And 당하다 is a special case and it’s used when it’s something against the subject’s will.

So, 해고하다 means to lay off or to fire someone. Nobody wants to be fired, so you’d say 해고당하다.

Pay attention to the particles in parentheses. 받다 and 당하다 are actually **transitive verbs** themselves, even though they're used to show a passive meaning. This means they *can* take the object marker 을/를. This is why I said "in principle" passive verbs are intransitive; 받다 and 당하다 are like exceptions.

**Intro to Causative Verbs**

Alright, let's briefly touch on **Causative Verbs**. This is quite an advanced topic, so for now, I just want you to grasp the core concept.

We’ll revisit this topic later once you learn the 게 suffix which means “So that”.

So, a causative verb, as the name suggests, is a verb that causes someone or something to do the action.

It means to *make* someone or something to do something

So, for example, we have 웃다 again, an intransitive verb.

you can't "laugh something." But you *can* **make someone laugh**, right? So, when you add a causative suffix, 웃다 becomes **웃기다**. 웃기다 is now a **transitive verb**!

Look at the example: 친구가 나를 너무 웃겨서 배가 아파요. (My friend made me laugh so much that my stomach hurts.) You see 나를 (me, with the object marker)?

Causative verbs can be tricky because they look very similar to passive verbs (they also use 이, 리, 히, 기, plus 우, 추).

Because of this, it's best to learn each causative verb separately, just like passive verbs. But for now, just be aware of the concept!

**Compound verbs**

The next type of verb we’re going to look at is compound verbs – these are basically two verbs combined i**n this structure to create** a new meaning

**[Verb Stem] + 아/어 + [Another verb]. Just like 좋아하다 and 싫어하다.**

To actually use these in a sentence, all you do is conjugate the *second* verb however you need to. So, you can have 좋아해요, 좋아했어요, 좋아합니다, and so on.

And there is a LOT of compound verbs in Korean. And the thing is, they're not always super "logical" in the sense that you can’t just take any two verbs and combine them.

And there’s no clear-cut rules about which ones work together and which ones don't.

But if you know what both of the original base verbs mean, you can often make a pretty good guess about the new meaning.

For example, we have 알아보다 which is **알다** and **보다** combined. And it has two meanings

1. *To know what you see* → To recognize
2. *To look in order to know* → To inquire, to look into, or to research

✅ And you can easily figure out the meaning by looking at the context.

Here are some examples:

* **아기가 엄마를 알아보네요** – Oh, *the baby recognizes (his/her) mom.*
* **한국어 책을 알아보고 있어요** – *I’m researching Korean books.*

For the first one, you know right away it can't mean "to inquire." Unless there’s a baby going “Hmm, which mom should I be born to?” going through a list of pregnant women, right?

And the second one is pretty obvious, too. You *could* technically "recognize" a Korean book, in this context, it clearly means "to research."

So, when it comes to compound verbs, just like with almost everything else in Korean, context is super important because there aren't always strict rules.

But there ARE really common patterns so, we're going to focus on those today.

**아/어하다**

The first compound verb we’re going to look at is 아/어하다.

Now, most of the time, we use this form when we're talking about **other people's feelings or thoughts**.

In English, you can say "I'm sad," "you're sad," or "she's sad." But in Korean, **we can't truly "know" what another person is thinking or feeling, even when it seems super obvious.**

슬프다 means to be sad. And if someone else is sad, we have to change 슬프다 to 슬퍼하다.

Note that it’s now an action verb.

Now, Go Billy Korean says that you can translate this form as “to seem” or “to look” But he’s actually wrong. We actually have a completely separate form for that and that’s 아/어 보이다.

So, again, this form does NOT mean “to seem”

Instead, you can think of 하다 here as "to act" or "to behave." So, 슬퍼하다 could translate to "to **act** sad" or "to be **outwardly** sad." like "I can see they are *behaving* in a sad way."

I don’t *know* for sure if they’re sad but from what I see, I can tell they’re sad – is what it means

Let’s break it down by person:

**1st Person (I / We):** Obviously, we know how we feel! So, you can definitely say 저는 슬퍼요 (I'm sad) or 저희는 슬퍼요 (We're sad). No need for 아/어하다 here.

**2nd Person (You):** It’s perfectly natural to ASK “Are you sad?” So, you can say 슬프세요? But if you're telling someone “Don’t be sad”, you **cannot** say 슬프지 마세요. Instead you have to say “슬퍼하지 마세요”

For 3rd person, even if someone is literally crying right in front of you, you still couldn't say 얘가 슬퍼요 (He/She is sad) directly. You have to say 얘가 슬퍼해요 instead.

This is the very same reason why you **cannot** use ~고 싶다 (to want to) with a third person. You can't *know* what someone else wants. So, if you wanted to say "Jisoo wants to eat kimbap," saying 지수는 김밥을 먹고 싶어요 would be wrong.

Instead, it **should be 지수는 김밥을 먹고 싶어해요** because you’re saying what you’re observed from the way Jisoo’s *behaved* and figured she acts like she wants 김밥.

아/어하다 beyond feelings

Now, there are actually a few cases where 아/어하다 is not used for things that are “emotions”, per se.

We have:

* **예뻐하다** (from 예쁘다 - to be pretty)
* **귀여워하다** (from 귀엽다 - to be cute)

You know, pretty and cute are not emotions. So, they don't mean "to act pretty" or "to act cute", they mean **"to adore someone"** or "to cherish someone."

And this is actually the logic behind 좋아하다 and 싫어하다.

* 좋다 (to be good) + 하다 = **좋아하다** (to like)
* 싫다 (to be dislikable) + 하다 = **싫어하다** (to dislike)

"to do the act of liking" or "to do the act of disliking."

It’s like feeling temporarily upset with someone you’re close to or at least have some type of feelings for.

**아/어 있다**

Alright, let's move on to our next compound verb: **아/어 있다**.

The idea behind this form is that **an action has been completed, and it continues to "exist" in that state.** It's kind of like how we use the "-en" ending in English like in "broken".

For example, when 살다 combines with 있다 and become 살아있다, it means “to be alive” – the action of living is complete, and that state continues.

예문 할머니가 아직 살아계실 때 더 자주 찾아뵈어야겠어요. 설명

By the way, 살아남다 (to survive) is also a compound verb, which means "to live and remain," or "to survive." That phrase 꼭 살아남아야 한다 you sometimes hear means "You MUST survive!"

Now, do you remember ~고 있다? We learned that it's used for something that's *currently going on*, like an action in progress. It uses the *same* verb 있다

It’s sort of like “~en” like in “broken”.

By the way, 살아남다 is also a compound verb, literally meaning to live and remain – Survive. Let me zoom in a bit so you can see the text on the poster.

꼭 살아남아야 한다 means “you MUST survive”

**~고 있다 VS 아/어있다**

If you remember “~고 있다” refers to something that’s currently going on. It’s the same 있다 but it has a different meaning.

Let’s compare the two forms.

창문이 깨지고 있어요 – The glass is breaking. (right now)

창문이 깨져 있어요 – The glass is broken.

Let’s look at another example 앉다, to sit.

아기가 앉고 있어요 – The baby is sitting down. (Currently in action)

아기가 앉아 있어요 – The baby is sitting down (He is seated)

In English, you’d say the baby is sitting down for both cases, but it’s different in Korean

**아/어있다 restriction**

Here's an important restriction for **아/어 있다**: it **can only be used with verbs that can remain in that state** (i.e., verbs that describe a lasting state), and most of the time, those verbs are **intransitive verbs.**

For example, 닫다 (to close) is a **transitive verb**. You *close* something actively. So, saying 닫아있다 (X) doesn't make sense; you can't "be in the state of having closed the door" yourself. This makes no logical sense in Korean.

Therefore, you need to use **닫히다**, which is the **passive form of 닫다** and is **intransitive**. So, you'd correctly say **문이 닫혀있다** (O) (The door is [in the state of having been] closed).

In English, you use close for both cases like I “close” the door and the door closes but in Korean, it’s different.

어제 약국에 갔는데 문이 닫혀있더라고요. (I went to the pharmacy yesterday, but I recall the door was closed.) Here, 닫혀있더라고요 clearly indicates the *state* of the door.

If you want to express this idea using a **transitive verb**, you would typically use the ~고 있다 form that we learned previously. For instance, 입다 (to wear) is a transitive verb. Even though you might *stay dressed*, 옷을 입어있다 (X) sounds awkward. The natural way to say "I'm wearing clothes" is **옷을 입고 있다** (O). This form can mean both "currently wearing" (the action in progress) and "stay dressed" (the resulting state).

**아/어 주다**

Next, we have 아/어 주다. This means is you’re giving the act of doing “FOR SOMEONE” as a favor.

And you know that 주다 becomes 주시다 in honorific, and 드리다 in humble.

For example,

남자친구가 점심을 사줬어요 – My boyfriend bought me lunch

엄마가 파스타를 만들어 주셨어요. – Mom made pasta for me, for the family, or someone else.

And 아/어 주세요 is often translated as “please” because it’s used for polite requests.

For example, 가세요 like in 공항으로 가세요 sounds like “GO to the airport,” like you’re giving an order because it’s still a “command” even though you’re saying it honorifically.

So, we say “공항으로 가 주세요” instead like “kindly give the act of going to the airport for me.”

Let’s look at some common 주다 verbs.

**도와주다**

You actually had this word on your notes for today’s assignment.

Now, 도와주다 comes from 돕다 to help. But 돕다 is rarely used on its own because helping, by nature, is giving, so just memorize 도와주다 as is.

돕다 is an irregular verb which ends in ㅂ. So you have to replace ㅂ with 우 then add 아.

우아 when said fast, sounds like 와, this is how you get 도와주다

저 좀 도와주세요, please help me.

도와드릴까요? Can I help you?

What is 좀? 좀 is a shortened version of 조금 which means “a little”. So, it’s informal and used mostly in spoken Korean.

But 좀 itself has one usage that 조금 doesn’t. It softens the tone of requests and commands. Kind of like the word “some” in English.

So, if you said, 물 주세요 it sounds like “Give . me . water, please”, a bit too stiff and a bit rude.

So, 물 좀 주세요 would be much more polite and natural like “Could you give me some water, please?

**알려주다**

Another common verb, 알려주다. 알리다 is a causative verb that comes from 알다 which means to know. And it means to inform or to notify.

So notification is 알림 in Korean.

And again, informing, by nature, is “giving” information to someone.

Example

이건 비밀이에요. 아무도 알려주지 마세요.

Now, 이건 is short of 이것”은”, the topic marker. And 비밀 means secret.

I hope you now have a better understanding about 은는 and 이가 but I figured I’d use this example to touch on it again.

If you used a subject marker and you said, 이”게” 비밀이에요, this would mean this is THE secret. So, there definitely needs to be some context behind this sentence.

Maybe it’s the secret that they’ve been hiding, that you wanted to talk about or something.

**빌려주다**

빌리다 means to borrow, now if you combine it with 주다, giving the act of borrowing, that means to lend.

예문 설명

* **나 방금 핸드폰이 꺼졌거든. 네 것 좀 빌려줄 수 있어?**

**– (Just so you know) My phone just turned off (died). Could you lend (me) yours?**

* **제가 지금 현금이 없네요. 이천원만 빌려주시면 내일 갚겠습니다.**

**– (I just realized) I don’t have cash. If you (could) lend me 2000 won, I’ll pay you back tomorrow**

**보내주다**

보내주다 comes from 보내다, which means to send.

And you send something “TO” someone.

And if you look at this example, it can also translate as “to let someone go”.

너무 피곤해요. 이제 집에 보내주세요. Send me home as in let me go home.

**보여주다**

Now, we learned 보이다 earlier but 보이다 is also the causative of 보다. So it also means “to show”

But 보이다 as a causative verb is rarely used on its own just like in 내보이다 to reveal and 선보이다 to present.

Now, showing something to someone is an act of giving. So, 그냥 “보이다” 보다 “보여주다”가 훨씬 더 자주 쓰인다. 그렇기 때문에 보여주다도 마찬가지로 그냥 하나의 동사로 생각하고 배우는 게 좋다.

예문 설명

우리의 능력을 보여주자

샘플은 아직 안 보여주셨습니다.

Let’s say you came back from a business meeting and your boss asks you, how did it go? Did you get the sample?

And you would go “샘플은” – Well, about the sample 아직 안 보여주셨습니다, using 은 to imply contrast. So you might follow up with something like but they did show me the photo or the price.

**아/어 보다**

아/어 보다 is often translated as “to try” but this doesn’t mean “to put effort into something”.

There’s a separate word “노력하다” for that.

It simply means to do something and see how it goes or what it’s like.

쟈켓 입어 봤는데 I wore the jacket and saw how it looked on me, meaning I tried it on

저한테는 조금 크네요 but it’s a little too big for me.

직원에게 길을 물어볼까요? Should we try asking the staff for directions? and see if he knows how to get there? Or see what he has to say? We can try asking him is what it means.

And because of this, 아/어 봤다 is very commonly used to say that you’ve experienced something.

Let’s look at this example.

김밥 먹어 봤어? Have you eaten Gimbab?

김밥은 안 먹어봤어 Well about Gimbab, I haven’t tried it BUT! 하지만 김치는 먹어 봤어 But I “HAVE” tried 김치.

This is another example of how the topic marker can show contrast.

**물어보다 여쭤보다**

We just used 물어보다 in our last example. And it comes from 묻다 to ask, which is another regular verb. The way it works is you replace ㄷ 받침 with ㄹ and conjugate it by adding 어.

여쭈다 is a humble form of 묻다

And 묻다 and 여쭈다 are also rarely used on their own because you have to wait and “see” what the other person has to say in response to your question.

왜 그걸 저한테 물어보세요? Why’re you asking ME that?

선생님한테 여쭤 볼까요? Should we ask the teacher?

묻다 by the way has 3 meanings and the other two are regular verbs.

To bury and to get stained with things like dirt or liquid.

**봐 봐**

This is a super common expression in Korean and it has 3 meanings. And it’s all about the delivery.

**아/어본 적(이) 있다 없다**

This form has almost identical meaning to 아/어보다.

But it has more emphasis on the experience.

Literally, it means to Have the experience of doing something and seeing what it’s like.

Now, 적 means experience. But it’s never used like a regular noun. It’s a bound noun, meaning it’s bound to a grammar form. The regular word that means experience would be 경험.

And 본 comes from 보다 and it’s a past tense modifier. So 본 is modifiying 적.

Let’s compare these two sentences

한국에 가봤어 VS 한국에 가본 적 있어?

The second one has more focus on the experience.

The way you do this in English, is you just add EVER like “Have you EVER been to Korea?”

**아/어보이다**

Finally, we have 아/어보이다

보이다 here is the passive form of 보다 (to see). You can think of it as "to be seen" or “To seem”

**THIS is the form that means "to seem" or "to look like,"** not 아/어하다. Remember how I kept stressing that 아/어하다 does NOT mean "to seem"? Well, this is the one!

Examples:

* 행복해 보이다 comes from 행복하다 (to be happy). So, 행복해 보이다 means "to seem happy" or "to look happy."
* The example on the slide: SNS에서는 모두가 행복해 보이지만 사실은 안 그래요. (On social media, everyone seems happy, but the truth is, they're not.)
* Another example: 화장을 하니까 완전히 달라 보이네요. (Wow, you look completely different with makeup on!)
