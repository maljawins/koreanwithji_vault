## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces the grammar form ~걸(요), a versatile ending used to express the speaker's thoughts, opinions, or assumptions. While it is frequently taught as a way to contradict someone, the instructor emphasizes that its core function is to softly emphasize the speaker's opinion. This "softness" allows a speaker to disagree or present a different perspective without appearing rude or blunt. The lesson covers how to conjugate this form across future, present, and past tenses, noting how the nuance shifts from making a guess to expressing surprise or making an observation depending on the tense used.

---

### Basic Construction and Pronunciation

To create this form, the speaker takes a verb stem and transforms it into an adjective form. In the most common usage (future tense/assumption), the stem is attached to either ㄹ or 을. If the stem ends in a vowel, ㄹ is added; if it ends in a consonant, 을 is added. For example, the verb 하다 (to do) becomes 할, and 먹다 (to eat) becomes 먹을. The syllable 걸 (or the polite 걸요) is then attached. 

A key pronunciation rule applies here: although spelled with a single ㄱ, the consonant in 걸 is always pronounced as a double consonant (ㄲ) due to the preceding ㄹ. Therefore, 할 걸요 is pronounced as "할껄요" and 먹을 걸요 as "먹을껄요." This form is strictly for informal, spoken Korean and should not be used in formal essays or highly formal speech.

---

### The Core Meaning: Soft Contradiction and Emphasis

The primary use of ~걸(요) is to softly contradict what someone else has said. It functions like the English words "actually" or "probably," but with a specific tone that suggests, "I have a different opinion, but I don't want you to take it the wrong way." For instance, if someone claims that pineapple is terrible on pizza, a speaker who disagrees might use this form to say, "Well, I think it's not that bad." 

At its essence, the form emphasizes the speaker's opinion. It is most often used to make an assumption or a guess about someone or something else. When speaking, this form requires a rising intonation on the final syllable, similar to asking a question, to maintain the soft and suggestive nuance.

---

### Usage in the Future Tense (Assumption)

When used with the future tense adjective form (ㄹ/을), the ending expresses a prediction or a "should be" feeling. The instructor provides several examples to demonstrate this:
- **괜찮을 걸요.** / It'll probably be okay. (Or "It should be okay.")
- **별로 안 추울 걸요.** / It's probably not going to be that cold.
- **이게 더 맛있을 걸요.** / This'll probably taste better. (Or "Actually, this should taste better.")
- **내일은 날씨가 좋을 걸요.** / Tomorrow the weather should probably be good.

The instructor notes that even past events can use this "future" construction if the speaker is making a guess about a past state. For example, **지금도 늦지 않았을 걸요** translates to "We're probably still not late," where the speaker is making an assumption about their current status based on a past timeframe.

---

### Usage in the Present Tense (Observation and Surprise)

While less common than the future tense usage, ~걸(요) can be used with present tense adjectives. For action verbs, the stem attaches to 는; for descriptive verbs, the stem attaches to 은 or ㄴ. Using the present tense changes the nuance from a "guess" to an "observation" or "expression of surprise." 

The instructor illustrates this with the sentence:
> **바깥보다 실내가 더 추운 걸요?** / (Actually/I think) it is colder indoors than outdoors.

If the speaker used the future form (추울 걸요), they would be predicting that the inside *will* be colder. By using the present form (추운 걸요), they are observing that it *is* currently colder, often expressing surprise at this fact. Other examples include:
- **하지만 지금은 비가 오는 걸요.** / But it's raining now (to my surprise/observation).
- **기대하지 않은 답인 걸.** / Well, that's an answer I didn't expect.

---

### Usage in the Past Tense

The past tense construction is unique and sometimes misused even by native speakers. Technically, it requires the past tense verb stem (the part of the verb before the final 어요/습니다) followed by 는걸(요). For example, with 예상하다 (to expect), the past stem is 예상했, resulting in **예상하지 못했는 걸요** (Nobody could have expected that). 

The instructor points out that many Koreans mistakenly use the past tense adjective form (ending in ㄴ/은) because the future and present versions use adjective forms. While you might hear people say **한 걸요** instead of **했는 걸요**, the latter is the grammatically correct version for making an observation about a past event. Another example provided is:
> **그 빵은 아침에 다 팔렸는 걸요.** / Actually, I think that bread all sold out in the morning.

---

### Expressing Regret: ~ㄹ/을 걸 (그랬다)

A separate but related grammar point involves expressing regret, meaning "I should have [done something]." This form looks similar—using the future adjective form (ㄹ/을) followed by 걸—but it has three distinct differences:
1. **Intonation:** It ends with a falling intonation like a regular sentence, not a rising one.
2. **Spacing:** There is a space before the word 걸.
3. **Optional Addition:** It can be followed by the verb 그랬다 (conjugated as 그랬어요, 그랬어, etc.).

This form is used for the first person (I/we). Examples include:
- **한국어를 더 열심히 공부할 걸 그랬어요.** / I should have studied Korean harder.
- **카메라를 가져올 걸.** / I should have brought my camera.
- **나도 갈 걸 그랬나?** / Should I have gone too?

The instructor clarifies that to say "You should have [done something]" (second person), a different form is used: **~지 그랬다** (e.g., **너도 가지 그랬어** / You should have gone too).
