## Teaching Points

Ji covers several advanced grammar points and provides corrections on Amira's previous work:

*   **Refining "For":** Ji explains that while `~를 위해(서)` (for/to dedicate) is grammatically correct, it often sounds too formal for casual conversation. He suggests using `~께`, `~한테`, or `~에게` as more natural alternatives in sentences like "I made lunch for my grandmother."
*   **The Particle `마다` (Each/Every):** Ji distinguishes between `모든` (all/every) and `마다` (each). `모든` refers to a whole group, while `마다` refers to each individual instance (e.g., `날마다` / each day).
*   **Short Quoting Form `~대요`:** This is a shortened version of `~다고 해요`. Ji clarifies that while past tense `~됐어요` exists, the present form `~대요` is much more common even when quoting something from the past, as Korean is often flexible with tense matching in embedded clauses.
*   **The Connector `~더니`:** Used to connect two observations. It can indicate the result of an action (e.g., `약을 먹었더니 좀 나아졌어요` / After taking pills, I feel better) or the discovery of something new (e.g., `만나봤더니 친절한 사람이었어요` / Now that I've met them, they turned out to be kind).
*   **Expressing "While" in Korean:**
    *   **`~(으)면서`:** Used for two actions happening simultaneously by the same subject. It can also show contrast. Ji notes that the first clause usually stays in the present tense even if the main action is past.
    *   **`~는 동안`:** Indicates duration ("during"). Unlike `면서`, it can be used with different subjects in the two clauses.
    *   **`~ㄴ/은 채(로)`:** Describes an action occurring while in a specific *state* resulting from a completed action (e.g., `누운 채로 잠들었어요` / I fell asleep while [in the state of] lying down).
    *   **`~다가`:** Indicates an interruption where Action A is stopped by Action B (e.g., `집에 가다가 친구를 만났어요` / While going home [interrupted], I met a friend).

## Lesson Flow

The lesson transitions into the main topic: "While." Ji introduces `~(으)면서` first, explaining its "at the same time" meaning and its requirement for matching subjects. Amira asks if it's just a combination of `면` (if) and `서` (continuity), which Ji confirms. 

Next, they move to `~는 동안`. Amira asks a insightful question about how to refer to "childhood" periods. Ji uses the on-screen whiteboard to list options like `어린 시절 동안`, `어린 시절에`, and `어렸을 때`, explaining that `시절` has a retrospective, reminiscent feel.

Ji then contrasts `면서` with `~ㄴ/은 채(로)`, using the example of falling asleep. He explains that `누우면서` implies falling asleep *while actively in the motion* of lying down, whereas `누운 채로` correctly describes falling asleep while already in the state of lying down.

Finally, Ji introduces `~다가` as the "interruption" version of while. He uses the example of meeting a friend coincidentally on the way home to show how Action A (going home) was interrupted by Action B (meeting the friend).
