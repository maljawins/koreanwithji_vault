## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the grammar ending ~던, characterizing it as a tool primarily for intermediate and advanced learners. While beginners learn how to use descriptive verbs to modify nouns in the present tense, ~던 allows speakers to describe nouns based on past states or habitual actions. The lesson focuses on three main applications: using it with descriptive verbs to indicate a past state, using it within the "I think" (~것 같다) structure to discuss the past, and using it with action verbs to describe habitual past actions or things one "used to" do.

The learning arc moves from simple noun modification to more complex subjective expressions. A key nuance explored toward the end of the video is the distinction between using the present tense stem versus the past tense stem with ~던, which signals whether a past habit has completely ceased or might still be ongoing.

---

### Modifying Nouns with Past States
In basic Korean, descriptive verbs are turned into adjectives by attaching ~은 or ~ㄴ to the verb stem, such as `작다 (to be small)` becoming `작은 집 (a small house)`. To describe a house that *was* small in the past, the instructor demonstrates the use of ~던. The conjugation involves taking the past tense form of the verb, removing the final ~다, and attaching ~던. For example, `작다` becomes the past tense `작았다`, and by dropping ~다 and adding ~던, we get `작았던 집 (a house that was small)`. 

While this specific usage—a house that was small—might feel slightly awkward in isolation, it provides the grammatical freedom to build more complex sentences, such as describing a house that was small but is now gone. The instructor notes that while grammatically correct, using descriptive verbs in the past tense modifier form like this is less common than the other uses covered in the lesson.

---

### Expressing Subjective Thoughts about the Past
A very frequent use of ~던 occurs within the ~것 같다 structure, which is used to say "I think..." or "It seems like...". To express a thought about a present state, one uses the standard adjective form:
> **김밥이 맛있는 것 같아요.** / I think the gimbap is delicious.

However, to express a thought about a past state or action, the ~던 ending is required. This is formed by attaching ~던 to the past tense stem. The instructor provides several examples of this pattern:
- **김밥이 맛있었던 것 같아요.** / I think the gimbap was delicious.
- **작년이 더 추웠던 것 같아요.** / I think last year was even colder.
- **시험이 아주 어려웠던 것 같아요.** / I think the test was very difficult.
- **미국 사람이었던 것 같아요.** / I think they were American. (Note: `이다` becomes `이었던` after a consonant or `였던` after a vowel).
- **이미 했던 것 같아요.** / I think I already did it.

---

### Describing Habitual Past Actions ("Used To")
When used with action verbs, ~던 conveys the meaning of "had done" or "used to do." This describes a recurring action or a state that lasted for a period in the past. Interestingly, with action verbs, you can attach ~던 to either the past tense stem or the present tense stem, and the meaning remains largely the same. For instance, `좋아하다 (to like)` can become either `좋아했던` or `좋아하던`.

The instructor illustrates this with several example sentences:
- **제가 좋아했던 음식을 먹었어요.** / I ate food that I used to like (or had liked).
- **제가 원했던 거예요.** / It's what I used to want.
- **이게 일요일마다 입었던 셔츠예요?** / Is this the shirt you used to wear every Sunday?
- **제가 옛날에 살던 집이 이제 없어요.** / The house that I used to live in is now gone.
- **제가 다니던 학교예요.** / It's the school I used to attend (or had attended).

---

### Nuance: Present Stem vs. Past Stem with ~던
The instructor highlights a subtle but important distinction when using ~던 with action verbs. Using the present tense stem + ~던 (e.g., `다니던`) implies an action that you used to do, but it leaves it ambiguous whether you are still doing it or not. For example, `다니던 학교` could mean a school you used to go to and might still be attending.

Conversely, using the past tense stem + ~던 (e.g., `다녔던`) emphasizes that the action is completely finished and no longer happens. Therefore, `제가 다녔던 학교예요` strongly implies that you no longer attend that school. If no such emphasis is needed, the two forms are often used interchangeably in casual conversation.
