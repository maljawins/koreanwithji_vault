## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor addresses a frequent request from learners: how to express the concept of "really" or "very" in Korean. While English often relies on a few versatile words, Korean offers a wide array of adverbs that carry specific nuances, levels of politeness, and emotional registers. The lesson categorizes these words into standard polite forms, casual slang, emphatic markers, and words that change meaning based on context.

The instructor establishes a foundational grammatical rule: all the words discussed in this video are adverbs. In Korean, these adverbs can stand alone as a response or be placed directly before a verb or descriptive verb. For example, just as one says "really nice" in English, in Korean, one places the adverb for "really" immediately before the word for "nice."

---

### Standard and Casual Ways to Say "Really"

The most standard and polite way to say "really" is **정말** or its slightly less common variation **정말로**. It is the safest choice for any situation where politeness is required. It can be used as a standalone question or statement. The instructor demonstrates several forms on screen: **정말요?** (Really?), **정말이에요?** (Is that real/really?), and the more formal **정말입니다** or **정말입니까?**.

In contrast, **진짜** is perhaps the most common word learned by beginners, but it carries a casual connotation. The instructor advises avoiding it in formal situations or when speaking to someone who requires extra politeness. It is perfectly suited for casual speech, such as **진짜?** or **진짜야?**. Interestingly, **진짜** also functions as an adjective meaning "real" or "legitimate," as in the example **진짜 금 (real gold)**.

---

### Emphatic and Descriptive Adverbs

When a speaker wants to emphasize that something is "extremely" so, they might use **되게**. This is a casual, emphatic word. For instance, **되게 더워!** translates to "It's extremely hot!" or "It's really hot!"

For a standard, versatile option that means "very" or "really," the instructor introduces **아주**. It is neither strictly casual nor formal, making it appropriate for almost any situation. An example provided is **아주 맛있어요 (It's very/really delicious)**. Notably, **아주** has a secondary meaning of "for good" or "altogether." The instructor writes the example **아주 가버렸어요**, which means "She/he left for good" or "left forever."

**매우** is similar to **아주** but is slightly more formal and polite. Because of its formal tone, it is less common in casual conversation. An example of its use is **매우 기뻐요 (I am very happy)**.

---

### Nuanced Adverbs: Negative, Positive, and Accomplishments

Some Korean adverbs for "really" carry specific emotional weight. **몹시** means "very" or "really," but specifically implies that something "cannot be any more so." It is typically used in a negative sense, similar to "awfully" in English. The instructor illustrates this with **몹시 추운 날씨 (awfully cold weather)**, implying the cold is at an extreme, negative limit.

On the positive side, **굉장히** means "greatly," "very," or "really," and is generally reserved for wonderful or positive attributes. For example, saying someone is **굉장히 똑똑해요** means they are "greatly" or "really" smart in a positive way.

**대단히** also means "extremely," but it is frequently used to describe impressive accomplishments or to add weight to formal social formulas. The instructor notes it is common in phrases like **대단히 감사합니다 (I am extremely thankful)** or **대단히 죄송합니다 (I am extremely sorry)**. It can also describe a movie as **대단히 재미있어요 (extremely entertaining)**.

---

### Truly, A Lot, and "So"

The word **참** translates to "truly," "really," or "very much." While the instructor notes it is used slightly more by older generations, it remains common among all ages as a polite adverb. A typical use case is a compliment like **참 예뻐요 (You are truly pretty)**. It is also famously used in the interjection **아 참!**, which means "Oh, yeah!" or "Oh, I just realized/remembered!"

**많이** literally means "a lot," but it can function as "really" or "very" when paired with certain descriptive verbs. For example, **많이 취했어요** literally means "a lot drunk," but is naturally translated as "He is really/very drunk."

**너무** has undergone a shift in usage. In formal writing or essays, it strictly means "too much" (often with a negative implication), such as **너무 많이 먹었어요 (I ate too much)**. However, in casual spoken Korean and slang, it simply means "so" or "really" in a positive sense. Speakers often extend the sound for emphasis (**너무~~ 맛있어요**) or repeat it (**너무 너무 맛있어요**). The instructor cautions that while these are common in speech, they should be avoided in formal writing.

---

### Completeness and Slang

**완전** comes from the word **완전히**, which means "completely." In casual spoken Korean, the **히** is often dropped. It can mean "completely really," as in **완전 실패였어요 (It was a complete failure / It was really a failure)**. It can also be used with negative verbs to mean "not at all."

Finally, the instructor mentions two slang terms used primarily by teenagers and children:
- **레알**: A Korean phonetic spelling of the English word "real."
- **진심**: Literally means "sincerity," but used as slang to mean "Are you serious, bro?"

The instructor recommends avoiding these in work-related or formal situations as they can make the speaker sound immature.
