## Lesson Overview

This lesson focuses on the various ways to transform Korean verbs into other parts of speech, specifically adjectives (modifiers) and nouns (nominalization). The first half of the lesson covers how to change a verb into a modifier so it can directly describe a noun, such as turning "to be cute" into "cute puppy" or "to study" into "a person who studies." Ji explains the grammatical rules for both descriptive and action verbs in the present tense, including several important irregular patterns.

The second half of the lesson delves into nominalization—the process of turning a verb into a noun (e.g., "to move" into "moving" or "movement"). Ji introduces three forms of nominalization: the fixed noun form (~음/ㅁ), and the two most common conversational forms, ~는 것 and ~기. A significant portion of the lesson is dedicated to the subtle nuances between ~는 것 and ~기, helping the student understand when to use each based on whether they are discussing a general concept or a specific task.

Finally, the lesson covers several fixed grammatical expressions that utilize these nominalized forms. These include expressing "before" and "after" an action, making decisions or promises (~기로 하다), and starting or stopping activities. Ji emphasizes learning these as whole phrases rather than individual components to better grasp their natural usage in conversation.

### Slide 4 — Descriptive Verbs Modifiers
- **On screen:** Rules for Descriptive Verbs in the present tense.
    - **After 받침:** Stem + 은 (작다 → 작은 컵; 짧다 → 짧은 치마).
    - **After Vowel:** Stem + ㄴ (크다 → 큰 박스; 웃기다 → 웃긴 남자; 이다 → 인).
    - **Irregulars:** ㄹ (Remove ㄹ + ㄴ: 멀다 → 먼 곳), ㅂ (Replace ㅂ with 우 + ㄴ: 춥다 → 추운 날씨), ㅎ (Remove ㅎ + ㄴ: 어떻다 → 어떤 음악).
    - **Exceptions:** 있다/없다 → 있는/없는.
    - **Note:** 좋다 is regular (좋은 사람).
    - A small table shows 어떻다, 이렇다, 그렇다, 저렇다 combined with 거 (어떤 거, 이런 거, etc.).
- **Ji's teaching:** The rule is simple. If the stem ends in a **받침**, add **은**. 작다 becomes **작은** as in *작은 컵* (a small cup). 짧다 becomes **짧은** as in *짧은 치마* (a short skirt). If the stem ends in a **vowel**, add **ㄴ**. 크다 becomes **큰** as in *큰 박스* (a big box). 웃기다 becomes **웃긴** as in *웃긴 남자* (a funny man). 이다 becomes **인**, which is useful for phrases like *흰색인 것*—"The thing that is white," or more naturally, "something white." There are a few exceptions. For **ㄹ-ending verbs**, remove ㄹ and add ㄴ; 멀다 becomes **먼** as in *먼 곳* (a far place). For **ㅂ-ending verbs**, replace ㅂ with **우**, then add ㄴ; 춥다 becomes **추운** as in *추운 날씨* (cold weather). For **ㅎ-ending verbs**, remove ㅎ and add ㄴ; 어떻다 becomes **어떤** as in *어떤 음악* (which kind of music). This is actually where the question word **어떤** comes from! One thing to note: **좋다** is **regular**, so it becomes **좋은** as in *좋은 사람* (a good person). Finally, **있다** and **없다** are exceptions too; you just add **는**, so they become **있는** and **없는**. For example, *거기 있는 펜* (the pen that is there) or *아무도 없는 곳* (a place where nobody is).

### Slide 5 — Action Verbs Modifiers
- **On screen:** Rule: Stem + 는.
    - **Note:** "받침 doesn’t matter with action verbs."
    - Examples: 좋아하다 → 좋아하는; 찾다 → 찾는.
    - Irregular ㄹ: Remove ㄹ + 는 (알다 → 아는; 팔다 → 파는).
    - Box: "가장 & 제일 : Most" (Favorite).
    - Box: "This is where ~는 중이다 comes from."
- **Ji's teaching:** 받침 doesn’t matter for action verbs. You just add **는** to the stem. It's super simple. So, 좋아하다 becomes **좋아하는**. For example: *내가 요즘 가장 좋아하는 노래는 핑크 플로이드의 “Coming Back to Life”야* (The song that I like the most these days is “Coming Back to Life” by Pink Floyd). We don’t really have a word for “favorite” in Korean, so we just say “the thing I like the most”—**가장** or **제일 좋아하는**. You’ve actually used this before way back in the beginning of our lessons; well, this is where it comes from. 찾다 becomes **찾는**, as in *지도로 식당을 찾는 중이야* (I’m in the middle of looking for a restaurant with a map). Do you recognize this? **~는 중이다**? Here **찾는** is modifying **중** (the middle), and this is how we make it. There’s one exception with verbs ending in ㄹ: drop ㄹ and add **는**. 알다 becomes **아는** (*내가 아는 사람이 있는데...* — There’s someone I know who’ll help you). 팔다 becomes **파는** (*근처에 양말을 파는 데가 있나요?* — Is there a place nearby that sells socks?).

### Slide 7 — 곳 vs 데
- **On screen:** Comparison of "Place where ___".
    - **곳:** Specifically refers to a location. Works with 이/그/저 (이 곳 = 여기가).
    - **데:** Much broader. Refers to locations AND situations. Does NOT work with 이/그/저.
    - Example: 머리 아픈 데(에) 먹는 약 (Medicine for when your head hurts).
    - Note: "We often treat situations like places in Korean."
- **Ji's teaching:** (note: script marker said Slide 6) Now, **곳** and **데** both mean “place” and they show up a lot after modifiers, but they’re used a little differently. **곳** specifically refers to a physical location. For example: *제가 사는 곳은 날씨가 좋아요* (The place I live has good weather). You can also use 이 / 그 / 저 with it, like *이 곳이 제가 자는 방이에요* (This place is the room I sleep in), which has exactly the same meaning as 여기 / 거기 / 저기. Now, **데** can also mean a location, just like 곳, but the key difference is that **데** can also refer to **situations**. That’s because in Korean, we often treat situations “like places.” When that happens, we use **데**, not **곳**. **데** is actually a bigger topic, but I’m giving you a quick overview so you can start recognizing it in context. Look at this example: *머리 아픈 데에 먹는 약 있어요?* (Do you have medicine to take for when your head hurts?). By the way, we drop the particle 에 most of the time because 데에 sounds repetitive. If you said **곳** here instead, it would sound like “Is there medicine at a physical place where my head hurts?” which doesn’t make sense. Another one: *살 빼는 데 좋은 음식* (Food that is good for losing weight). Here, **데** refers to the purpose or situation, not a location. One last thing: you **can’t** use **이 / 그 / 저** with **데**. So, *이 데가 제가 자는 방이에요* is wrong.

### Slide 8 — Verb Nominalization
- **On screen:** Title "Verb Nominalization: Verb → Noun."
    - Three ways to nominalize 움직이다 (To move):
    1. A.V Modifier + 것 (움직이는 것)
    2. A.V Stem + 기 (움직이기)
    3. Verb Stem + 음/ㅁ (움직임)
    - Note: The difference between 1 and 2 is "very subtle" (Moving vs. To move).
    - Note: 3 creates "independent nouns" (~ment / ~tion).
- **Ji's teaching:** (note: script marker said Slide 7) Nominalization just means turning verbs into nouns—things like "moving," "to move," or "movement." In Korean, we have three ways of doing this, but we’re going to focus on these two: **~는 것** and **~기**. The difference between these two forms is very subtle, kind of like “moving” and “to move” in English. How would you explain that difference to someone learning English? It feels almost impossible, right? We’ll take a closer look at those soon. The third form, the **음/ㅁ** form, creates **independent nouns**. They’re like the Korean version of **~ment** or **~tion**, like *movement* or *action.* Let’s take a quick look at this form before we learn about the other two.

### Slide 9 — Verb Stem + 음(ㅁ)
- **On screen:** "Fixed noun form."
    - Rules: After 받침 add 음 (Irregular ㄹ: 삶); After vowel add ㅁ.
    - Table of common nouns: 움직임 (Movement), 가르침 (Teachings), 꿈 (Dream), 잠 (Sleep), 깨달음 (Realization), 느낌 (Feeling), 삶 (Life), 믿음 (Belief), 기쁨 (Joy), 슬픔 (Sadness), 바람 (Wish), 쉼터 (Shelter).
    - Usage: "음슴체" slang used in reports, texting, social media. Sounds "cold, factual, emotion-less."
- **Ji's teaching:** (note: script marker said Slide 8) The first way we use this form is to create **independent nouns**. You can technically make it by adding **음** or **ㅁ** to the verb stem, but honestly, don’t worry too much about how to make it. Not every verb works with this form, and there’s no clear rule for which ones do. I highly recommend just **learning them as separate vocabulary**, like the common ones on this list. People also use this form “like” a speech level. It’s not polite nor casual; it’s somewhere in between. It’s called **음슴체** in slang. It feels **cold** and **emotionless**, and it sounds **retrospective**, like you're writing in the past tense, though technically it’s not. You’ll see it in reports, captions, comments, and texts online. Nobody really talks like this in real life; when we do, it feels childish or rude, so it's probably not a good idea to speak this way in general.

### Slide 10 — ~는 거(것) VS ~기 Comparison
- **On screen:** Comparison table and rules.
    - **Contracted forms:** 것은 → 건; 것이 → 게; 것을 → 걸; 기를 → 길.
    - **~는 거 (것):** The idea, thought, fact, or concept of doing. (듣는 것: I like the idea of listening).
    - **~기:** The activity or the task at hand. Listing form (To-do lists, signs, tests). (듣기: I like the activity of listening).
    - Note: "Extremely subtle difference... sometimes interchangeable."
- **Ji's teaching:** (note: script marker said Slide 9) Now let’s compare **~는 거** and **~기**. They can be interchangeable sometimes, but not always. The difference comes down to **nuance**, and it’s really subtle even for native speakers. I will do my best to help you see the difference, but don’t worry if it doesn’t make total sense at first. Instead, focus on learning them through **fixed expressions**, just like you did with phrases like ~기 싫다 and ~기 때문에. You’ll also see a lot of **contracted forms** today, so let’s quickly go over those: **것은** becomes **건**, **것이** becomes **게**, **것을** becomes **걸**, and **기를** becomes **길**. First, **~는 거**. Since **거** means “thing,” **~는 거** literally means “the thing that ___.” It refers to the idea, thought, fact, or concept of doing something. *저는 음악을 듣는 걸 좋아해요* (I like listening to music). Here, it’s about the whole idea of listening to music. On the other hand, **~기** feels more like an **activity** or **task**. It sounds like it will happen in the near future. I call this the “listing” form because you’ll see it in to-do lists, instructions, signs, and tests. *저는 음악을 듣기를 좋아해요* (I like to listen to music). This emphasizes the activity itself. Look at this screenshot of my inbox; on the bottom it says **더 보기** (See more). **보기** comes from 보다; this is a task, so *더 보는 거* wouldn’t work here. In these two sentences, there’s almost no difference, just like in English between "I like listening" and "I like to listen," but they aren’t always interchangeable.

### Slide 11 — ~는 것(거) VS ~기 Feel the difference 1
- **On screen:** Example: "Why don't you call your girlfriend?"
    - **전화하는 게 무서워요:** The idea of calling her is scary. Refers to the concept, not necessarily "now."
    - **전화하기(가) 무서워요:** The task of calling her scares me. Refers to the specific activity/task.
    - **Side Note:** 무섭다 (scared/scary) and 지루하다 (bored/boring) depend on context.
- **Ji's teaching:** (note: script marker said Slide 10) Someone asks: *왜 여자친구한테 전화를 안 해요?* (Why don’t you call your girlfriend?). You can answer in two ways. If you say *여자친구한테 전화하는 게 무서워요*, **전화하는 거** refers to the whole idea or concept of calling her. Maybe she’s yelled at me before, or she talks forever and won’t hang up. Whatever the reason, what’s scary is **the whole idea of talking to her on the phone**. If you say *여자친구한테 전화하기(가) 무서워요*, **전화하기** refers to the **task itself**. Here, I’m just scared to call her at that specific moment. In English, “I’m scared of calling her” and “I’m scared to call her” mean different things, and this is a great example to help you feel that difference. But don’t assume “~는 거 = doing” and “~기 = to do” always matches English one-to-one. Also, a side note: **무섭다** means both “scared” and “scary,” and **지루하다** can mean both “bored” and “boring.” Korean doesn’t make that distinction, so you have to look at the context.

### Slide 12 — ~는 것(거) VS ~기 Feel the difference 2
- **On screen:** Comparison of personal preference vs. objective description.
    - **책 읽는 게 좋아요:** I like reading (personal preference/subjective).
    - **책 읽기(가) 좋아요:** It is good to read (objective description of the place/activity).
    - **말하는 게 어려워요:** Speaking is difficult (personal nervousness/subjective).
    - **말하기(가) 어려워요:** Public speaking as an activity is difficult (objective description of the task).
    - **Fixed expressions:** ~기(가) 좋다 (It is good to), ~기(가) 어렵다 (It is difficult to).
- **Ji's teaching:** (note: script marker said Slide 11) Let’s look at more examples. *이 카페에서 책 읽는 게 좋아요* refers to the act of reading at this place; you enjoy **the act** of reading here. This is **subjective**—it’s about **how you feel**. *이 카페에서 책 읽기가 좋아요* sounds more **objective**. You’re saying this **café itself** is a good place **for the activity of reading**. I want you to remember the **~기 좋다** form as a whole, meaning "it is good to" or "suitable for." Here, you’re saying the café is suitable for the activity of reading. Same with speaking: *말하는 게 어려워요* emphasizes your **personal struggle**, like feeling nervous or shy. *말하기(가) 어려워요* means that **public speaking as a task** is hard in general. It’s an objective description of the activity.

### Slide 13 — ~는 것(거) VS ~기 Feel the difference 3
- **On screen:** Teasing examples.
    - **놀리는 게 싫어요:** I don't like teasing (concept/idea).
    - **놀리기(가) 싫어요:** I don't want to tease (activity/task).
    - **사람들이 절 놀리는 게 싫어요 (O):** I don't like the idea of people teasing me.
    - **사람들이 절 놀리기(가) 싫어요 (X):** Makes no sense (I don't want to do the activity of people teasing me).
    - Note: ~기(가) 싫다 is a fixed expression for "don't want to."
- **Ji's teaching:** (note: script marker said Slide 12) Let’s tackle one more. *저는 놀리는 게 싫어요* means I don’t like teasing in general; **놀리는 게** refers to the *concept* of teasing. *저는 놀리기가 싫어요* means "I don’t want to tease." This is about **my action**—the task of teasing. We learned **~기 싫다** as a fixed expression for “don’t want to do,” which is why it’s better to learn the form as a whole. Now, what if you want to say, "I don’t like people teasing me"? You add a full clause: *사람들이 저를 놀리다* (People tease me). You must use *저는 사람들이 절 놀리는 게 싫어요* because you are referring to the whole idea of people teasing you. *저는 사람들이 절 놀리기가 싫어요* is wrong because it would mean “I don’t want to *do* the activity of people teasing me,” which makes no sense.

### Slide 15 — When only ~는 거 is acceptable
- **On screen:** Two main cases where ~기 cannot be used.
    1. **When it means "What" (The thing that):** *네가 원하는 걸 모르겠어* (I don't know what you want).
    2. **When it means "That" (The fact that):** *티파니가 준호를 좋아하는 걸 알았어* (I knew that Tiffany likes Joonho); *우리가 서로를 돕는 게 중요해요* (It's important that we help each other).
- **Ji's teaching:** (note: script marker said Slide 13) We just learned that **~는 거** refers to the idea of an action. Naturally, when it works like “what” (the thing that) or “that” (the fact that) in English, you can only use **~는 거**, not **~기**. For example: *네가 원하는 걸 모르겠어* (I don’t know what you want, or the thing that you want). Or: *나는 이미 티파니가 준호를 좋아하는 걸 알았어* (I already knew the fact that Tiffany likes Joonho).

### Slide 16 — ~는 거다 (것이다)
- **On screen:** "More emphatic than ~요 – Verbal emoji."
    - 거 (것) + 이다 (To be).
    - Adds nuance: "So!", "The thing is...", "You see...", "What I'm saying is...".
    - Usage: 1. Imply more to the story (Beginning of a story). 2. Ask/clarify information. 3. Add emotional emphasis (*뭐 하시는 거예요?*).
    - Note: "Why present tense? Similar to how we tell stories in English (So, this guy walks into a bar...)."
- **Ji's teaching:** (note: script marker said Slide 14) You already know **~는 거**, but if you add **이다** (to be), you can add more emotion. Literally it means “It is that~” but it feels like “So!” “The thing is…” “You see…” or “Look.” You can use it when telling a story: *내가 어제 걔한테 전화를 했는데 안 받는 거야. 그래서 문자도 보냈어* (You see, I called him yesterday, but he doesn’t answer. So I texted him). Why present tense? Just like in English: “So, this guy *walks* into a bar…” It grabs attention. You can also use it to clarify: *근데 우리 내일 집에 몇 시에 가는 거예요?* (But wait, what time are we going home tomorrow?). *뭐 하시는 거예요?* is super common. Depending on how you say it, it can sound curious or aggressive. Think of this form like a verbal emoji.

### Slide 17 — ~는 것 같다
- **On screen:** "Expressing opinion (softly)."
    - 같다: To be like. Literally: "It is like the thing that ___."
    - Often translated as "I think," but there is no "thinking" verb (생각하다) involved.
    - Softens tone to avoid being too assertive.
    - Culture note: "Group > Individual Opinion. Korean often avoids strong 'I' statements."
    - Note: Many Koreans pronounce 같아(요) like 같애(요).
    - Note: To modify a reason, use ~는 이유 (not ~는 때문).
- **Ji's teaching:** (note: script marker said Slide 15) **~는 것** plus **같다** (to be like) literally means “it’s like the thing that,” but it’s often translated as “I think.” It’s a very common way to express an opinion softly. This is a great example of how language shows culture. In Korea, people value group over individual opinions, so **~는 것 같다** softens your tone to “It seems like~” rather than saying “*I* think” (내가 생각해요). Koreans even overuse this for factual things. *제 남동생은 이성 친구가 많은 것 같아요* (It seems like my younger brother has many friends of the opposite sex). *엄마가 하는 음식이 좋아서 집에 자주 가는 것 같아* (I guess I go home often because I like Mom’s cooking). *제가 케이팝을 잘 모르는 이유는 티비를 보지 않기 때문인 것 같아요* (I think the reason why I don’t know much about K-pop is because I don’t watch TV). By the way, to modify a reason, always use **이유**, not **때문**.

### Slide 18 — Fixed forms with ~기
- **On screen:** List of patterns: ~기 싫다, ~기 때문에, ~기 전, ~기로 하다, ~기 시작하다.
    - Note: "~는 거 doesn't work here."
    - Advice: "Best to learn them as fixed forms."
- **Ji's teaching:** (note: script marker said Slide 16) There are a lot of fixed **~기** forms, just like *기 싫다* and *기 때문에*. **~는 거** doesn’t work here at all. For now, treat these like English idioms. Why do we say ‘listen *to* music’ and not ‘listen music’? We just know it as one phrase. “Might as well” is impossible to translate literally into Korean, but we know the meaning. It's the same with these **~기** forms. We’re going to learn a couple new ones today, plus their opposites that don’t use **~기**. Don’t think about *why* they work this way; just learn the whole phrase.

### Slide 19 — ~기 전 & ~은/ㄴ 후
- **On screen:** "Before & After."
    - **전 (Before) / 후 (After):** Ordinary nouns, followed by time markers (에, 부터, 까지).
    - **1. Before doing:** Action Verb Stem + 기 전 (*식사하기 전에*).
    - **2. After doing:** Action Verb Stem + 은/ㄴ 후 (*먹은 후에*, *끝난 후*).
    - Note: 은/ㄴ is a past tense modifier (Things that "DID" or "WERE").
- **Ji's teaching:** (note: script marker said Slide 17) **전** means before and **후** means after. They’re ordinary nouns, so you can use them with time markers like **에**, **부터**, or **까지**. *일요일 전에 전화 주세요* (Call me before Sunday). *30분 후에 저녁을 먹을 거예요* (We’ll eat dinner in 30 minutes). To say "before doing," use **Action Verb Stem + 기 전에**. *아빠는 식사하기 전에 항상 기도를 하세요* (Dad always prays before eating). *운동하기 전에 아무것도 먹지 마세요* (Don’t eat anything before working out). To say "after doing," use **은/ㄴ 후에**. If there is a 받침, add **은 후에** (*먹은 후에*); if not, add **ㄴ 후에** (*끝난 후*). **은/ㄴ** is a past tense modifier, describing completed actions. Just remember: **~은/ㄴ 후** means "after something happened."

### Slide 20 — ~기로 하다
- **On screen:** "Making decisions."
    - Action Verb Stem + 기로 하다.
    - Meaning: To set [action] as a choice. Often translated as "decided to" or "supposed to."
    - Usually used in past tense (*만나기로 했어요*).
    - **"Decide not to":** 안 + Verb Stem + 기로 하다 OR Verb Stem + 지 않기로 하다.
    - **Variations:** ~기로 결정하다 (decide), 결심하다/다짐하다 (resolve), 마음(을) 먹다 (make up one's mind), 약속하다 (promise), 동의하다 (agree).
- **Ji's teaching:** (note: script marker said Slide 18) The particle **로** here marks a choice, like choosing options at a restaurant (*스테이크는 미디움으로 할게요*). So **~기로 하다** means to set an action as a choice. That’s why it’s translated as “decide to” or “supposed to.” It’s usually used in the past tense. *우리는 다음 주에 만나기로 했어요* (We decided to meet next week). *친구랑 등산가기로 했는데 다리를 다쳤어요* (I was supposed to go hiking but I hurt my leg). To say "decide not to," you can use **안 하기로 하다** or **하지 않기로 하다**. When used in the present tense, it’s usually suggestive, like “Let’s agree to.” *술은 안 마시기로 해요* (Let’s agree not to drink). You can also use other verbs like **마음을 먹다**. **마음** means mind or heart, but this doesn’t mean to eat a heart; it means to make up one’s mind. *드디어 유학 가기로 마음먹었어요* (I finally made up my mind to go abroad to study).

### Slide 21 — ~기 시작하다 / 그만
- **On screen:** "To start / stop doing."
    - **~기 시작하다:** Action verb stem + 기 시작하다. Usually used in past tense (*배우기 시작했어요*).
    - **그만 + Verb:** "To stop doing something."
    - Note: 그만 is an adverb meaning "up to this point and no more." Used BEFORE the verb.
- **Ji's teaching:** (note: script marker said Slide 19) This is the last **~기** form for today. To say "start doing something," use **Action Verb Stem + 기 시작하다**. *올해부터 한국어를 배우기 시작했어요* (I started learning Korean this year). *날씨가 좋았는데 비가 오기 시작했어요* (It was sunny, but then it started raining). To say "stop doing something," we use the adverb **그만**, which means “up to this point and no more.” You just add **그만** before the verb. *그만 놀고 공부하세요* (Stop playing and study). *저희는 이제 그만 싸우기로 했어요* (We agreed to stop fighting now). *아무도 듣지 않으니까 이제 그만 얘기할게요* (Since no one is listening, I’ll stop talking now).

## Teaching Points

- **Modifiers (Verb → Adjective):**
    - For descriptive verbs in the present tense, add **-은** if the stem ends in a consonant (받침) and **-ㄴ** if it ends in a vowel.
    - For action verbs in the present tense, always add **-는** regardless of the final consonant.
    - **Irregular ㄹ:** Always remove the **ㄹ** before adding the modifier ending (**-ㄴ** for descriptive, **-는** for action).
    - **Irregular ㅂ:** Replace **ㅂ** with **우** and add **-ㄴ** (e.g., 춥다 → 추운).
    - **Irregular ㅎ:** Remove **ㅎ** and add **-ㄴ** (e.g., 어떻다 → 어떤).
    - **있다/없다** always take **-는** (있는/없는).

- **Nominalization (Verb → Noun):**
    - **~음/ㅁ:** Used to create fixed, independent nouns (e.g., 삶, 꿈). Also used in "음슴체" (online/texting slang) to sound factual or cold.
    - **~는 것 (거):** Refers to the general idea, concept, or fact of an action. It is the only choice when translating "what" (the thing that) or "that" (the fact that).
    - **~기:** Refers to a specific task or activity. It is used in lists, instructions, and many fixed grammatical patterns.
    - **Nuance:** **~는 것** is often more subjective/personal, while **~기** is often more objective/task-oriented.

- **Fixed Grammatical Patterns:**
    - **~기 전:** Before doing (Action Verb Stem + 기 전에).
    - **~은/ㄴ 후:** After doing (Action Verb Stem + 은/ㄴ 후에). Uses the past tense modifier.
    - **~기로 하다:** To decide to do / To be supposed to do. Treats the action as a "choice" (**로**).
    - **~기 시작하다:** To start doing.
    - **그만 + Verb:** To stop doing. **그만** is an adverb placed before the verb.
