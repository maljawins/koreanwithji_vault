## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor addresses a common question among language learners: exactly how many vocabulary words are required to achieve fluency or hold a full conversation in Korean. While frequency lists of up to 6,000 words are easily accessible online, the instructor argues that the number needed for functional, fluent-sounding speech is actually much lower. The lesson aims to provide a realistic target for students and emphasizes the importance of word usage over simple memorization.

The learning arc begins with a comparison to English vocabulary statistics, moves into a personal data experiment conducted by the instructor, and concludes with a discussion on the "quality" of vocabulary versus "quantity." By analyzing both his own speech and that of native speakers, the instructor demonstrates that a relatively small core of words handles the vast majority of daily communication.

---

### The 3,000 Word Benchmark
The instructor introduces a common statistic from English language studies: knowing approximately 3,000 words allows a speaker to understand about 95% of an average conversation. He visually demonstrates the math on screen, showing that learning just 10 words a day results in 3,650 words after one year. While some sources suggest that languages like Japanese or Korean might require a higher count—closer to 5,000 or 6,000 words—the instructor uses his own experience to challenge this.

To investigate this further, the instructor performed a linguistic experiment. He transcribed several hours of his own Korean speech and used software to analyze word frequency. He then did the same for a popular Korean streamer to get native speaker data. The results showed that while thousands of unique words were used, the overwhelming majority of words used more than once fell within the 3,000-word range. This confirms that in Korean, as in English, a small bank of high-frequency vocabulary does the "heavy lifting" in daily life.

---

### Core Vocabulary Examples
During the analysis of his own speech, the instructor highlights several high-frequency "filler" and functional words that appeared constantly. These include:
- **거** / thing
- **그냥** / just
- **안** / (negative marker used before verbs)
- **그** / that (also used as a thinking filler like "uh...")
- **근데** / but, by the way

A visual word cloud on screen displays these and other common terms like **그리고 (and)**, **이 (this)**, and **거야 (it is/will be)**. The instructor notes that these words are repeated over and over, allowing a speaker to navigate 95% of a conversation even if they lack the specific "outlier" words for niche topics.

---

### Quality of Usage vs. Quantity of Words
The instructor emphasizes that the total number of words known is less important than how well a student can use them. He points out that a student who knows 20,000 words but cannot string them together will sound less fluent than someone who knows only 1,000 words but uses them with perfect grammar and natural flow. 

The goal for a serious student should still be to reach that 6,000-word mark—the official list provided by the **국립국어원 (National Institute of the Korean Language)**—to achieve true high-level fluency. However, for the purpose of "speaking Korean" and holding conversations, 3,000 unique verbs and nouns (not counting their various conjugations) is the functional "sweet spot." He encourages learners to focus on mastering the core 3,000 words found in comprehensive resources, such as his own *Korean Made Simple* book series, which covers roughly that amount across three volumes.
