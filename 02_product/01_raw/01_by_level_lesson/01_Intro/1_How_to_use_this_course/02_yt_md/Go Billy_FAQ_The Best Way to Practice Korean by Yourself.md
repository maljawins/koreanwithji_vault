### Pros of Practicing Korean by Yourself
The instructor identifies three primary benefits for practicing alone, which he writes on the screen under a "PROS" heading. First, solo practice is ideal for the beginner stage. At this level, a learner cannot yet sustain a real conversation, so speaking with a native speaker often results in receiving feedback or replies that the learner cannot understand. In such cases, practicing with a native is functionally the same as "practicing to a wall." 

Second, solo practice is completely free. Engaging with a language partner often involves costs, such as buying a meal or coffee, or a time-based exchange where you must spend half the session teaching your own language. Third, practicing alone removes the barrier of being shy. Many beginners feel embarrassed or unintelligent when making mistakes in front of others. Practicing alone allows for long gaps between sentences and repeated mistakes without any social pressure or judgment.

---

### Cons of Practicing Korean by Yourself
Despite the benefits, the instructor lists several "CONS" on the whiteboard to highlight the limitations of staying entirely solitary. The most significant drawback is that you cannot learn new material or become truly fluent by only talking to yourself; you are limited to the contents of your textbook or current knowledge. Furthermore, solo practice does not improve listening skills because there is no external input to process.

Pronunciation and naturalness also suffer without a partner. While watching dramas or listening to podcasts can help, a learner needs feedback from a native speaker to know if their pronunciation is accurate and if their intonation matches the specific social situation. The instructor notes that natural conversation skills can only be acquired through natural conversations with others. Without a native speaker to provide corrections, a learner may unknowingly reinforce mistakes or develop unnatural speech patterns.

---

### How to Practice Effectively
The instructor concludes that learners should 100% practice by themselves, but they should do so while also seeking out native interaction. He provides several tips for solo practice. First, start immediately by speaking out loud as soon as you learn the alphabet or basic syllables. Recording your own voice is a powerful tool to compare your speech against a target model. Second, take your time. Unlike real-time conversation, solo practice allows you to pause and think deeply about the best way to construct a sentence.

A unique tip shared by the instructor is to practice thinking in Korean. Even if you cannot form full sentences, you can label the world around you. For example, if you see a dog, you should think `강아지 (puppy)` or `개 (dog)` to get your brain used to retrieving Korean vocabulary. Finally, the instructor recommends finding a "pen pal" through apps like HelloTalk or Tandem, or websites like Conversation Exchange. This bridges the gap between solo study and live conversation, as text-based interaction allows the learner to take their time and use resources while still receiving feedback from a real person.
