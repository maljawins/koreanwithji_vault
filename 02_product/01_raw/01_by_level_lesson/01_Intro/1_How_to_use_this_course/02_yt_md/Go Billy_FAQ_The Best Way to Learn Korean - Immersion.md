## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces the fundamental differences between written and spoken Korean, noting that learners who study exclusively from one type of material may struggle when encountering the other. While written Korean requires strict adherence to grammar and spelling, spoken Korean is more flexible and forgiving. The lesson covers structural changes like particle omission and word order shifts, specific spoken-only grammar forms, vocabulary differences including filler words, and pronunciation nuances that deviate from standard orthography.

---

### Grammar and Structural Differences

One of the most prominent features of spoken Korean is the frequent removal of particles. The instructor notes that topic markers `은/는`, subject markers `이/가`, and object markers `을/를` are often omitted in conversation as long as the meaning remains clear. However, they are essential in written Korean to avoid vagueness, especially in formal contexts like essays or speeches. Learners are cautioned not to skip learning these particles just because they hear natives omitting them, as failing to master them prevents the formation of proper complex sentences later on.

Word order also becomes more fluid in speech. While adverbs typically precede the verb directly in writing—such as `빨리 공부하다 (to study quickly)`—speakers might occasionally place the adverb after the verb if they think of it mid-sentence. This "afterthought" structure is rare in writing because writers have the opportunity to review and correct their sentences.

---

### Spoken-Only Grammar and Connectors

The instructor highlights specific grammar forms that are primarily used in speech. The form `~아/어 가지고` (or the shortened `갖고`) functions similarly to the causal connector `~서`, meaning "because" or "due to." While it can occasionally appear in informal writing, it is a hallmark of casual spoken Korean.

Sentence connectors like `~고요` and `~는데요` are also used differently. While they can connect sentences in both mediums, they are much more common as sentence endings in spoken Korean. This is because they convey specific emotional nuances and tones that are better suited for interpersonal dialogue than for formal books, articles, or journals.

---

### Vocabulary, Slang, and Filler Words

Spoken Korean utilizes a variety of filler words and slang that are absent from formal writing. The word `막` is frequently used as a filler meaning "just" or "like." Other common fillers include `그` and `저`, which function like "um" or "uh" in English.

A specific spoken expression discussed is `~지 뭐`. This combines the `~지` ending with `뭐 (what)`. When `뭐` appears at the start of a sentence, it acts as a filler similar to "well" or "for example." When used at the end as `~하지 뭐`, it functions like a verbal shrug, conveying a sense of "Well, I guess I'll just do it" or "There's nothing else I can do."

---

### Dialects and Standard Korean

The instructor clarifies the relationship between dialects and writing. Even if a person speaks in a regional dialect, they will typically write in Standard Korean, known as `표준어`. He compares this to the relationship between Cantonese and Mandarin, where speakers may use their local tongue for speech but rely on a standardized form for written communication.

---

### Pronunciation and Spelling Deviations

Several words are pronounced differently in speech than they are spelled in standard orthography. For example, the ending `~아요` often sounds like `~애요`, such as `같아요 (to be like)` sounding like `같애요`, or `바라다 (to hope)` becoming `바래요`. Common greetings like `안녕하세요` are often slurred or shortened during rapid natural speech.

A major point of confusion for learners is the conjugation of the verb `이다 (to be)`. While the correct written forms are `예요` after a vowel and `이에요` after a consonant, many Koreans both pronounce and write it as `에요`. This error is so common that some natives might even "correct" a learner's proper usage to the incorrect version. Similarly, the word `효과 (effect)` is technically spelled and pronounced as `효과`, but many Koreans prefer the tensed pronunciation `효꽈`.

---

### Politeness and Word Choice in Writing

Writing for a general audience (books, journals, articles) typically employs the "plain form," characterized by the endings `~는다` or `~ㄴ다`. In this specific written style, the humble first-person pronoun `저` is replaced by the plain pronoun `나`.

The instructor also warns against the word `당신`. While it translates to "you," it often carries negative connotations or sounds rude in spoken Korean, except between spouses where it means "dear." However, it is still used in written Korean when the audience is completely unknown, such as in large-scale advertisements or posters.
