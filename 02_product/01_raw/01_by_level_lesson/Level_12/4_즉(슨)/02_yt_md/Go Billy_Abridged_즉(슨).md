## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces an advanced Korean grammar form, **즉(슨)**, which is rarely covered in standard online resources. This form primarily carries a written, formal, or slightly old-fashioned nuance and is seldom used in casual spoken conversation. The primary function of **즉** is to provide clarification, definition, or emphasis, often translated as "in other words," "that is to say," or "if we are talking about."

The video explores three distinct ways to use this form: as a standalone adverb at the beginning of a sentence or phrase, attached directly to a noun using the **인 즉(슨)** construction, and attached to action verbs in the past tense. Throughout the lesson, the instructor emphasizes that **즉슨** is simply a more emphatic version of **즉**, and for learners struggling with the meaning, it can often be mentally replaced with the topic marker **은/는** to understand its role in framing the subject of discussion.

---

### Basic Usage: 즉 and 즉슨 as Clarifiers

In its most common form, **즉** (or the more emphatic **즉슨**) functions as a connector that signals an explanation or a rephrasing of a previous statement. It is used after a word or phrase, followed by a space. The instructor suggests thinking of it as "if we say [X]" or "if we hear [X], then [Y]." It essentially marks the preceding information as the topic to be further explained or summarized.

The instructor provides several examples to demonstrate this clarifying function. In the first example, a restaurant's long history is used to conclude it must be good:
> **이 식당은 이곳에서 장사를 한 지 20년이 넘었는데 그 말은 즉, 무조건 맛집이라는 거지.** / This restaurant has been in business here for over 20 years, so in other words, it must be a good restaurant.

Another example uses a famous four-character idiom to show how **즉** introduces a modern explanation:
> **백문이 불여일견. 즉, 직접 해보는 게 중요하다는 말이에요.** / Seeing is believing. That is to say, it is important to try it out for yourself.

Finally, the instructor shows how it can be used to summarize a logical argument about skill improvement:
> **아무리 오래 배웠어도 연습을 하지 않으면 실력이 늘지 않아요. 즉, 시간보다 노력이 중요하다는 말이에요.** / No matter how long you've learned, if you don't practice, your skills won't improve. In other words, effort is more important than time.

---

### Noun + 인 즉(슨)

A more specific construction involves attaching the form directly to a noun. This is written as **[Noun]인 즉** or **[Noun]인 즉선** without any spaces. The **인** comes from the verb **이다 (to be)** conjugated into its present tense adjective form. This specific usage is quite old-fashioned and is primarily found in older literature or formal modern writing.

The instructor notes that in this construction, the form functions almost identically to the topic marker **은/는**, but with much stronger emphasis. It signals to the reader, "Let's talk specifically about this noun." Two common phrases where this is still seen today are **그 말인 즉선** (what those words mean is...) and **이유인 즉선** (the reason is...).

Example of clarifying a statement:
> **그 말인 즉선 이곳이 다른 지역보다 안전하다는 것이다.** / What I'm saying is that this place is safer than other regions.

Example of explaining a reason:
> **이유인 즉선 색이 가진 느낌이 다르기 때문입니다.** / The reason is because the feeling the color has is different.

---

### Action Verb + (으)ㄴ 즉(슨)

The rarest and most advanced usage involves attaching the form to an action verb. To do this, the verb is conjugated into the past tense adjective form—**한** for **하다**, **먹은** for **먹다**—and then **즉** or **즉선** is attached without a space. In this context, the meaning shifts slightly to indicate a cause or a reason.

The instructor suggests translating this usage as "since" or "because," similar to the **-(으)니까** ending in Korean. It implies that the action in the first part of the sentence provided the basis or the realization for the second part.

Example of a realization based on an action:
> **설명을 들어본 즉선, 이건 고장이 아니라 설정이 잘못됐다는 겁니다.** / Since I've heard the explanation, [I realize] it's not broken, but was set up incorrectly.

The instructor concludes by reiterating that while these forms are advanced and relatively rare, mastering them allows a student to navigate high-level written Korean and older texts with much greater ease.
