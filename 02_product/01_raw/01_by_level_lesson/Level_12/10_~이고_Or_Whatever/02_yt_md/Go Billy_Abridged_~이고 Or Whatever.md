## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the grammar form ~이고, an advanced-level structure used exclusively in informal, spoken Korean. While it shares some functional similarities with the form ~든지 (whether... or), ~이고 carries a specific dismissive nuance that ~든지 lacks. It is primarily used to express that the speaker is uninterested in, or dismissive of, the nouns being mentioned.

The lesson covers the mechanical construction of the form, its pragmatic usage in conversation, and common idiomatic variations. The instructor emphasizes that because this form is highly informal and carries a "who cares" attitude, it should be avoided in formal writing, business meetings, or proficiency tests like the TOPIK. It is strictly a "friend-to-friend" or casual spoken expression.

---

### Construction and Basic Meaning
The form is created by taking a noun and attaching 이고 if the noun ends in a consonant, or simply 고 if it ends in a vowel. For example, the noun 집 (house) ends in a consonant and becomes 집이고, while 학교 (school) ends in a vowel and becomes 학교고. Unlike ~든지, which can be attached to verb stems, ~이고 is restricted solely to nouns.

Etymologically, the form originates from the copula 이다 (to be) combined with the connector 고 (and). While its literal translation might be "it is [noun] and...", it is almost never used for this literal meaning. Instead, it functions as a marker of dismissal. When a speaker uses this form, they are signaling that they do not care about the noun in question. It is equivalent to saying "or whatever" or "who cares about..." in English.

---

### Using ~이고 in Pairs
The most common way to use this form is by listing two nouns, attaching the marker to both: Noun A + 이고, Noun B + 이고. This structure implies that the speaker is indifferent to both options or finds them both irrelevant to the current situation. The instructor provides an example regarding seasons:

> **여름이고 겨울이고 둘 다 싫지만 겨울이 조금 더 나아요.** / Summer or winter or whatever, I hate both, but winter is a little bit better.

In this sentence, the speaker uses 이고 to dismiss the importance of the seasons before expressing a slight preference. Another example demonstrates social dismissal:

> **지금은 친구고 가족이고 아무도 보고 싶지 않아요.** / Right now, whether it's friends or family or whoever, I don't want to see anyone.

The instructor also notes an idiomatic usage: 밤이고 낮이고. While it literally means "night or day or whatever," it is used as an adverbial phrase meaning "day and night" or "nonstop," often with a complaining nuance.

> **밤이고 낮이고 쉬지 않고 전화가 와요.** / I keep getting phone calls nonstop day and night.

---

### The "Noun + 이고 뭐고" Variation
If a speaker only has one specific noun they wish to dismiss, they can use the fixed expression ~이고 뭐고. In this construction, 뭐 (what) acts as the second noun. This is a very common and emphatic way to say "[Noun] or whatever." The instructor identifies this as his personal favorite way to use the grammar.

Examples of this variation include:
> **운동이고 뭐고 지금은 쉬고 싶어요.** / Exercise or whatever, right now I just want to rest.
> **명절이고 뭐고 저는 돈이나 벌 거예요.** / Holiday or whatever, I'm just going to earn some money.
> **시험이고 뭐고 다 포기하고 싶어요.** / Tests or whatever, I want to give up on everything.
> **다이어트고 뭐고 일단 먹을래요.** / Dieting or whatever, I'm just going to eat first.

---

### The "나발이고" Variation
For even stronger dismissal, speakers can replace 뭐고 with 나발이고. This version is even more informal and sounds significantly more dismissive or even slightly aggressive. It conveys a sense of "I don't give a damn about [Noun]."

The instructor provides a scenario involving a rejected apology to illustrate the intensity:
> **사과고 나발이고 다 필요 없으니까 연락하지 마세요.** / Apologies or whatever, I don't need any of that, so don't contact me.
