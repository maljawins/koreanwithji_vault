## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces two related grammatical forms that describe actions occurring in a repetitive, back-and-forth manner or actions that are on the verge of happening. The lesson begins with a foundational pattern used to create specific compound verbs and then transitions into the main grammar point of the day, which expresses the idea of being "about to" do something or doing something "barely."

The learning arc moves from fixed vocabulary expressions that show alternating opposite actions to a flexible grammar construction that can be applied to various verbs. By understanding the relationship between these two forms, learners can better grasp the nuance of an action that is oscillating between two states—such as happening and not happening.

---

### The Foundational Form: (으)락 (으)락 하다
The first form discussed is a pattern where a verb stem is attached to **으락** (if it ends in a consonant) or **락** (if it ends in a vowel), and this structure is repeated. The instructor notes that students do not need to learn how to produce this form independently because it is not a productive grammar rule used to create new words. Instead, the resulting words are fixed expressions with their own dictionary entries.

This form indicates that two related actions are working against each other in a sequence, similar to the motion of a seesaw. The actions happen one after the other repeatedly. Common examples include:
- **오락가락하다** — derived from **오다 (to come)** and **가다 (to go)**; it means to come and go frequently or to be back and forth.
- **오르락내리락하다** — derived from **오르다 (to go up)** and **내리다 (to go down)**; it means to go up and down frequently.
- **들락날락하다** — derived from **들다 (to enter)** and **나다 (to exit)**; it means to go in and out frequently.

---

### The Main Grammar: ~(을/ㄹ)락 말락 하다
The main focus of the lesson is a variation of the previous form. To conjugate this, you take a verb stem and attach **을** (after a consonant) or **ㄹ** (after a vowel), followed by **락**, a space, and then **말락 하다**. The word **말락** comes from the verb **말다**, which in this context means "to not do." Therefore, the literal sense of the grammar is "doing [verb] and then not doing [verb]."

This construction is most commonly translated as "about to" or "almost." It describes a state where something is right on the edge of happening but hasn't quite happened yet, or it is happening so faintly that it is oscillating between existing and not existing. The instructor points out that it is also very common—perhaps even more common in speech—to use **랑** instead of **락**, resulting in **~(을/ㄹ)랑 말랑 하다**.

A phonetic note is provided: regardless of whether you write **락** or **랑**, the meeting of the final **ㄹ** sound and the **ㅁ** in **말락** often results in a pronunciation that sounds like **랑말락** or **랑말랑**. The instructor also cautions learners not to confuse this grammar with the adjective **말랑말랑하다**, which means "to be soft/squishy" and is entirely unrelated.

---

### Practical Examples and Nuances
The instructor provides several sentences to demonstrate how this form expresses being on the verge of an action or a state of "barely" perceiving something:
- **단추가 떨어질락 말락 해요.** / The button is about to fall off. (It is hanging by a thread, alternating between staying on and falling).
- **달이 구름 때문에 보일락 말락 해요.** / I can barely see the moon because of the clouds. (Literally: The moon is in a state of being seen and not seen).
- **손이 천장에 닿을락 말락 해요.** / My hand is almost touching the ceiling. (It is just barely reaching or not reaching).
- **영희가 들릴락 말락 한 소리로 속삭였어요.** / Young-hee whispered in a voice that I could barely hear. (An audible yet nearly inaudible volume).
- **영화 제목이 기억날락 말락 하네요.** / The movie title is on the tip of my tongue. (I almost remember it, but I don't quite have it).

Finally, the instructor connects the two concepts by showing how a fixed expression like **들락날락하다** can be used in a sentence:
- **들락날락하면 찬바람이 들어와서 추워요.** / If you keep going in and out, the cold wind comes in and it's cold.
