## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the grammar form 십상이다, which is categorized as an intermediate-level expression. While not extremely common in basic daily conversation, it is a valuable tool for learners moving toward fluency as it allows for more nuanced descriptions of probability and consequence. The lesson covers the structural formation of the phrase, its historical etymology, and the specific pragmatic constraints that govern its use in natural Korean.

---

### Structure and Etymology
The grammar is formed by taking a verb stem, attaching the nominalizing suffix 기, and then adding 십상이다. This structure is versatile and can be used with both action verbs and descriptive verbs. The instructor explains that the word 십상 (十常) originates from the longer Hanja expression 십상팔구 (十常八九), which literally translates to "eight or nine times out of ten." Consequently, using 십상이다 is equivalent to saying that something happens eight or nine times out of ten, or that it is "highly likely" or "bound to" happen.

---

### Pragmatic Usage: Known Facts and Negative Outcomes
A critical nuance of 십상이다 is that it is used to describe well-known facts or logical consequences rather than personal opinions about the future. It conveys that "it is a known fact that if X happens, Y is the likely result." Because of this objective quality, it can often be translated as "it's a well-known fact that..." or "of course..."

Furthermore, the instructor emphasizes a strict constraint: 십상이다 is exclusively used for negative outcomes. Using it to describe a positive result sounds highly unnatural to native speakers. For example, the instructor demonstrates an incorrect sentence:

> **매일 한국어를 공부하면 빨리 배우기 십상이에요.** / (Incorrect) If you study Korean every day, you are likely to learn quickly.

Because learning quickly is a positive outcome, 십상이다 cannot be used here. Instead, it must be reserved for undesirable consequences like failing, getting sick, or being tired.

---

### Common Sentence Patterns
The instructor notes that this form is very frequently paired with the conditional -(으)면 (if) form. This mirrors the English logic of "If you do (or don't do) X, then Y is bound to happen." However, the conditional is not a mandatory requirement; the form can stand alone as long as the outcome is negative and statistically likely.

The following examples are provided to illustrate natural usage:

> **공부를 안 하면 시험에 떨어지기 십상이에요.** / If you don't study, you're bound to fail the test.
> **잠을 안 자면 내일 피곤하기 십상이에요.** / If you don't sleep, of course you'll be tired tomorrow.
> **어린이들은 유치원에서 감기에 걸리기 십상이에요.** / Children are likely to catch a cold at preschool.

In the final example, the instructor points out that even without an "if" clause, the sentence works because "catching a cold" is a well-known negative possibility for children in that environment.
