## Lesson Overview

This lesson focuses on refining advanced grammatical structures and providing detailed feedback on previous assignments. The session begins with a review of the student's self-study notes, addressing nuances in particles like 마다, the short quoting form ~대요, and the comparative structure ~에 비해서. Ji emphasizes the importance of natural phrasing, such as using 께, 한테, or 에게 instead of the more formal 을/를 위해 in casual contexts, and introduces the "the more... the more..." structure using 을수록.

The core of the lesson is dedicated to two major functional categories in Korean: "While" expressions and "Purpose/Intention" expressions. Ji breaks down the various ways to express "while" (~면서, ~는 동안, ~은 채로, ~다가), highlighting critical distinctions such as whether the subjects of the two clauses must be the same and whether the expression describes an ongoing action or a resulting state. The lesson also explores the nuances of ~다가 보니까 (realization) versus ~다가 보면 (natural outcome).

Finally, the lesson covers expressions of purpose and intention, specifically comparing (으)러 and (으)려(고). Ji explains the constraints of these forms, such as (으)러's requirement for motion verbs and its compatibility with commands, versus (으)려(고)'s broader application and its "about to" meaning when used with inanimate subjects. The session concludes with a guide to combining (으)려 with various connectors and a pronunciation tip regarding the Seoul/Gyeonggi dialectal variant 을라고.

### Slide 11 — While
- **On screen:** Two functions of "While": Duration and Contrast. Lists four Korean versions: ~면서, ~는 동안(에), ~은/ㄴ 채(로), and ~다가.
- **Ji's teaching:** Now, we’re going to learn how to say “while” in Korean. In English, "While" is very versatile with its two main functions being to indicate duration or contrast depending on the context. In English, we use the same word "while" for both, but in Korean, we use different connectors depending on the specific idea we want to convey. Today we're going to cover these four different Korean forms that can translate to "while".

### Slide 12 — ~(으)면서 #1
- **On screen:** Conjugation rules (먹으면서 vs 가면서). Explanation: [(으)면 If/When] + [서 Continuity]. Examples showing simultaneous action and contrast. A cartoon character says "너밖에 없는 거 알면서~" (While you know you're all I have).
- **Ji's teaching:** The first form we're going to look at is (으)면서. This form is 받침-sensitive: if the verb stem has a final consonant, you add 으면서 (먹으면서); if it ends in a vowel, you just add 면서 (가면서). You can think of 면서 as combining the "if or when" meaning of ~면 with the "continuity" meaning of ~서. So, it can be translated as "While" or "At the same time." 집에 가면서 친구랑 통화를 했어요 (I had a phone call with my friend while going home). 면서 can also show contrast. When it does, you'll often see 도 optionally added, like 세련되면서도 비싸지 않은 (stylish but not expensive). 도 emphasizes it like "even at the same time." There's also a super common phrase: 알면서. It's like saying, "You know that, don't you?" in a friendly way. 너밖에 없는 거 알면서... (You know you're all I've got...). It implies "you should know how I feel about you."

### Slide 13 — ~(으)면서 #2
- **On screen:** Usage with Action Verbs (A.V) and Descriptive Verbs (D.V). **Rule:** The subject for both clauses should be the same. Irregular verb conjugations (걸으면서, 알면서, 더우면서).
- **Ji's teaching:** Here are a couple more things to know about (으)면서. It can come after both Action Verbs and Descriptive Verbs. For example, "I exercise while listening to music" or "I like people who are funny but not shallow." However, the subject for both clauses must be the same. This is a crucial rule. In English, you can say, "I exercise while *he* listens to music," but in Korean, you cannot use (으)면서 for that. If you want to use two different subjects, you need to use 동안. For irregulars: ㄷ irregulars like 걷다 become 걸으면서; ㄹ 받침 verbs like 알다 just add 면서 to become 알면서; and ㅂ irregulars like 덥다 become 더우면서.

### Slide 14 — 동안(에)
- **On screen:** 동안 meaning "During / For time." Examples: 한동안 (for a while), 오랫동안 (for a long time). Usage after time nouns like 여름동안 (during summer).
- **Ji's teaching:** Alright, let's move on to 동안(에). "For a while" in Korean is 한동안, and "For a long time" is 오랫동안. Unlike 면서, 동안 cannot show contrast; it only shows duration. You can optionally attach 에 after 동안 but you can drop it most of the time. It can be used after time nouns where it works like a particle meaning "during" or "for," such as 여름 동안 (during the summer) or 다섯 시간 동안 (for five hours).

### Slide 15 — ~는 동안(에)
- **On screen:** A.V.S + 는 동안(에). Note: 있다 & 없다 are the only descriptive verbs allowed. **Rule:** You CAN use different subjects. Comparison: 는 동안 vs 던 동안.
- **Ji's teaching:** After a verb, 동안 is treated like a noun so you need to use a modifier: 는 동안. 90% of the time, people use 는, not 던. Note that in Korean, the tense of the clause before the connector doesn't usually need to match the main clause's tense, unlike in English. You can only use action verbs with ~는 동안(에), not descriptive verbs, with the exceptions of 있다 and 없다. 집에 있는 동안 한국어를 공부했어요 (I studied Korean while I was at home). Most importantly, you CAN use different subjects with 동안 unlike 면서. It's more natural to use 면서 when the subjects are the same, but if you need to describe two different subjects doing actions simultaneously, you have to use 동안. 여자친구가 쉬는 동안 저는 산책을 했어요 (While my girlfriend was resting, I went for a walk).

### Slide 16 — For a period of time
- **On screen:** 어린 시절 (childhood). 시절 is retrospective time. 어린 시절 동안 (During my childhood).
- **Ji's teaching:** When talking about a period of time in the past, we use 시절. So, 어린 시절 동안 means "During my childhood." You can also say 어린 시절에 or 어릴 때.

### Slide 17 — ㄴ/은 채(로)
- **On screen:** 채: State - Bound noun. Comparison: 면서 (Action happening / 고 있다) vs 채로 (While in the state / 아/어 있다). Examples: 누운 채로 (while lying down), 피곤한 채로 (while tired).
- **Ji's teaching:** Next is 은/ㄴ 채(로). 채 is a bound noun meaning "state." This form can also be translated as "while," but the key difference is the same as the difference between ~고 있다 and 아/어 있다. 면서 and 동안 are like ~고 있다 (action is happening), while 은/ㄴ 채(로) is like 아/어 있다 (in the state of a completed action). For example, you shouldn't say 누우면서 잠들었어요 because that implies you fell asleep *as* you were actively lowering your body. Instead, say 누운 채로 잠들었어요 (I fell asleep while lying down). It can also be used with descriptive verbs: 피곤한 채로 운전을 했어요 (I drove while tired). It emphasizes that you were in that state throughout the action.

### Slide 18 — ~다(가)
- **On screen:** A.V.S + 다가. Diagram shows Action A being interrupted by Action B. **Rule:** Subjects must match. Examples: 일하다(가) 말고 (while working [and stopping]), 이랬다가 저랬다가 (back and forth/capricious).
- **Ji's teaching:** You’ve seen ~다가 before. When it comes after a present stem, it's used when Action A is interrupted by Action B. Koreans often drop the 가. Common expressions include 하다가 말다 (to do but then stop) and 이랬다가 저랬다가 (to be like this then like that), which describes someone inconsistent. 왔다 갔다 means "back and forth." Think of ~다가 as "But then," "While," or "As" combined. While 면서 and 동안 usually indicate simultaneous actions, 다가 shows something new happening in the middle of an ongoing action. Just like with 면서, the subject of Action A and Action B must be the same.

### Slide 19 — Past Stem + 다(가)
- **On screen:** Diagram shows Action A completed before Action B. Two usages: 1. Action A completed then Action B (갔다가 올게). 2. Action B happens unexpectedly as a result of Action A.
- **Ji's teaching:** When 다가 comes after a past stem, there are two main usages. First, Action A was completed before Action B happened. 했다가 has a stronger "and then" meaning. 나 편의점에 갔다(가) 올게 means "I'll complete going to the store, and then I'll come back." Second, Action B happens unexpectedly as a result of Action A. 새벽 네 시에 일어났다가 오후에 졸려졌어요 (I woke up at 4 AM, but then I became sleepy in the afternoon). Here, waking up early led to the unexpected result of being sleepy later.

### Slide 20 — ~다(가) 보니(까) #1
- **On screen:** While + Why? 니까 works like "As" in English. Examples: Sequential action (들어오니까 - As I entered) and Twist of events (오니까 - As I came home [I discovered...]).
- **Ji's teaching:** We know 니까 means “because,” but it also has an advanced usage similar to "as" in English. It can express either a sequential action or an unexpected twist of events. 부엌에 들어오니까 맛있는 냄새가 나기 시작했어요 (As I entered the kitchen, a delicious smell started emerging). Or a twist: 집에 오니까 고양이가 소파에 오줌을 싸 놨네요 (As I came home, I discovered the cat had peed on the sofa). Notice the 니까 clause is in the present tense.

### Slide 21 — ~다(가) 보니(까) #2
- **On screen:** While + As. 다가 (Interrupted) + 보니까 (As one looks figuratively). Meaning: Now that, Since, After. **Rule:** Action B is Present/Past, NOT Future.
- **Ji's teaching:** The 니까 in 다(가) 보니(까) has that "as" meaning. It means while you’re doing A, you stop (because of 다가), then you look, and as you look, Action B happens. It translates to "Now that," "Since," or "After." Action B often turns out to be something you've come to realize. Therefore, Action B typically works with present or past tense, but not future tense. 살다 보니 이렇게 좋은 일이 생기네요 (Now that I've lived for so long, something this good happens). The keyword here is realization.

### Slide 22 — ~다(가) 보면
- **On screen:** While + If. Meaning: After action A, B will naturally happen. **Rule:** Action B is Present/Future/겠다, NOT Past.
- **Ji's teaching:** The keyword for 다(가) 보면 is "natural outcome." It means while doing A, you stop and look, and IF you continue, B will happen naturally. Action B tends to be in present, future, or ~겠다 form. You cannot use past tense here. 길을 따라서 가다 보면 오른쪽에 지하철역이 보일 거예요 (As you go along the road, you’ll see the subway station on the right side). So, 다(가) 보면 is about a predictable consequence, whereas 다(가) 보니(까) is about a realization.

### Slide 23 — (으)러 & (으)려(고)
- **On screen:** Purpose & Intention. Table compares (으)러 (motion verbs only, commands/suggestions OK, people/animals only) vs (으)려(고) (any action verb, no commands, anything as subject).
- **Ji's teaching:** Let's learn about (으)러 and (으)려(고). Both are 받침-sensitive, but if the stem ends in ㄹ, you just attach 러 or 려고 (살러, 살려고). Koreans often drop the 고. (으)러 must be followed by a motion verb like 가다 or 오다. You can't say 사업을 시작하러 준비하고 있어요 (X) because 준비하다 isn't a motion verb; use 시작하려고 instead. (으)러 works with commands or suggestions (놀러 와 - Come hang out), but (으)려고 doesn't. (으)러 is for people/animals, while (으)려고 can be for anything. 려고 has a stronger sense of "intending to" or "in order to." Also, 려고 했다 means "was going to." 가려고 했는데 못 갔어요 (I was going to go, but I couldn't).

### Slide 24 — (으)려(고) When the subject is a ‘thing’
- **On screen:** "Things can't have intentions." Meaning: "About to." Examples: 꽃이 피려고 (The flower is about to bloom), 해가 지려는 (when the sun was about to set).
- **Ji's teaching:** Since inanimate things can't have intentions, when they are the subject of (으)려고, it means "about to." 꽃이 피려고 하고 있어요 (The flower is about to bloom). It's like it's on the verge of happening.

### Slide 25~27 — (으)려 + Connectors
- **On screen:** Table of combinations: 려는, 려는데, 려던, 려다가, 려면, 려니까. Examples: 사냥하려는 호랑이 (tiger trying to hunt), 보내려는데 (intending to send but...), 나가려던 참에 (just as I was about to leave), 살려면 (if you want to live), 가려다가 (was going to go but then...), 하려니까 (just as I'm about to... [negative effect]).
- **Ji's teaching:** You can combine 려 with connectors you already know. 려던 참에 means "just at the moment I was about to do something." 참 is a bound noun for "moment." 려니까 combines intention with the "as" meaning of 니까. It means: as you're intending to do Action A, Action B suddenly happens and has a negative effect on your intent. 버스에서 자려니까 아기가 울기 시작했어요 (As I was about to sleep on the bus, a baby started crying). The baby crying negatively affected the intention to sleep. 떨리다 (to be shaken/nervous) is the passive of 떨다 (to shake something).

### Slide 28 — (으)려 Pronunciation
- **On screen:** Standard vs Dialect table. Dialect uses 을/ㄹ라고 (e.g., 할라고, 먹을라고). Map shows Seoul/Gyeonggi area. Tip for ㄹ pronunciation: tongue position like "Butter."
- **Ji's teaching:** If you find 려 hard to pronounce, you can say 을라고. This is from the Seoul/Gyeonggi dialect, but it's used nationwide. For standard pronunciation, ㄹ is neither "R" nor "L"; it's closer to the double "T" in "Butter." Combine that tongue position with "여" to say 려.

## Teaching Points

- **"While" Nuances:**
    - **~(으)면서:** Used for simultaneous actions or contrast. The subject of both clauses **must** be the same.
    - **~는 동안(에):** Used for duration. The subjects **can** be different. It only works with action verbs (plus 있다/없다).
    - **~은/ㄴ 채(로):** Describes being in a certain state while performing another action (similar to the resultative state 아/어 있다).
    - **~다가:** Indicates an interruption. Action A is stopped or interrupted by Action B. The subjects must be the same.
- **Realization vs. Outcome:**
    - **~다(가) 보니(까):** Focuses on a **realization** or discovery made after doing an action. It cannot be used with the future tense.
    - **~다(가) 보면:** Focuses on a **natural or predictable outcome** if an action continues. It cannot be used with the past tense.
- **Purpose and Intention:**
    - **~(으)러:** Specifically for use with motion verbs (가다, 오다). It is compatible with commands and suggestions.
    - **~(으)려(고):** Expresses a stronger intention ("in order to"). It can be used with any action verb but is not compatible with commands. When the subject is an inanimate object, it means "about to."
- **Pronunciation:** The ㄹ sound is a flap, similar to the "tt" in the American English pronunciation of "butter." In casual speech or dialects, (으)려고 is often pronounced as 을/ㄹ라고.
