## Lesson Overview
This lesson focuses on expanding the student's ability to express complex conditions, abilities, and obligations in Korean. A significant portion of the lesson is dedicated to the versatile verb 되다, which serves as the foundation for expressing concepts that English handles through modal verbs like "should," "may," and "must." The lesson also covers the nuances of "can" and "can't," contrasting different ways to negate ability based on whether the limitation is situational, a lack of skill, or an objective impossibility.

Furthermore, the lesson clarifies the distinctions between various connectors used for contrast (~지만 vs. ~데) and time/condition (ㄹ/을 때 vs. 면/으면). By the end of the session, the student should understand how to use particles to add specific nuances to these structures—such as emphasis, hesitation, or surprise—and how to express hopes and desires using the ~면 좋겠다 structure.

### Slide 10 — Can & Can’t (ㄹ/을 수 있다/없다)
- **On screen:** Grammar structure: V.S + ㄹ/을 수 있다 (없다). 
    - 수 is defined as a noun meaning "Ability," "Choice," "Means," or "Way."
    - ㄹ/을 수 있다: "There is a way to..."
    - ㄹ/을 수 없다: "There is no way to..." (Stronger nuance of impossibility/out of control).
- **Ji's teaching:** We use the grammar Verb Stem + ㄹ/을 수 있다 or 없다 to say "can" and "can't." The word 수 is actually a noun meaning "ability," "way," "means," or "possibility." So when you say 수 있다, you’re literally saying "There is a way to do it" or "I have the ability to do it." Conversely, 수 없다 means "There’s no way" or "It’s not possible." This has a stronger nuance than just being busy; it implies the situation is out of your control. For example, 그 날 파티에 오래 있을 수 없었어요 means you couldn't stay, perhaps because something came up—it wasn't an option. Similarly, 제 친구는 올 수 없을 거예요 means your friend can't come due to a limitation, not just because she doesn't want to.

### Slide 11 — 이러다 / 저러다 / 그러다 + ㄹ 수 있다 (없다)
- **On screen:** Combining 이/저/그 with -러다.
    - 이럴 수 있어 / 저럴 수 있어 / 그럴 수 있어: "That can happen," "It's possible," "That makes sense."
    - 이럴 수가 / 저럴 수가 / 그럴 수가: Used without 없어 to express shock, disbelief, or "No way!"
    - Visual notes: A note distinguishes 이러다 (Action verb) from 이렇다 (Descriptive verb).
- **Ji's teaching:** These are super common expressions for reacting to situations with empathy or shock. You know 이, 저, and 그. When combined with -러다, we get 이러다 (to act/say like this), 그러다 (to act/say like that), and 저러다. These are action verbs. Adding -(으)ㄹ 수 있다 creates 이럴 수 있어, 저럴 수 있어, or 그럴 수 있어. Especially 그럴 수 있어 is very common to show empathy, like saying "I get it" or "That's understandable." For example, if someone says they woke up too late, you can say 그럴 수 있어. For the negative, you'll often hear 이럴 수가... or 그럴 수가... without the 없어 at the end. This delivers a full emotional punch of "No way!" or "I can't believe it," often used in dramatic moments.

### Slide 12 — V.S + ㄹ/을 수(marker) 있다 (없다)
- **On screen:** A table showing how particles after 수 change the nuance:
    - 할 수가 있다/없다: More emphatic emotion.
    - 할 수는 있다: "I can do it, but..." (hesitant/contrast).
    - 할 수는 없다: "I can't do it [at least not this]."
    - 할 수도 있다: "I can also do this" or "I just might."
    - 할 수도 없다: "I can't even do this."
    - 할 수밖에 없다: "I have no choice but to do it."
- **Ji's teaching:** We can add more meaning to (으)ㄹ 수 있다/없다 using particles like 가, 는, 도, and 밖에. 할 수가 있다/없다 adds more emotion; 할 수가 없어! sounds like "There is just no way!" The particle 는 shows contrast; 할 수는 있어요 implies "I can do it, but..." (maybe I'm hesitant). 할 수도 있다 means "It's also possible" or "I might," while 할 수도 없어요 is more desperate, meaning "I can't even do this." Finally, 할 수밖에 없다 is very natural. If you think of 수 as "choice," it literally means "There isn't any choice but to do this," or "I have no choice."

### Slide 13 — Two more ways to say “Can’t” using 못
- **On screen:** 
    1. 못 + Verb: Functions like 안. Works with Action verbs (Pure Korean and 3-syllable Sino-Korean 하다 verbs). Can also mean doing something poorly (요리를 못해).
    2. V.S + 지 못하다: Slightly more formal. Works with ANY verbs, including descriptive verbs (완벽하지 못해요).
- **Ji's teaching:** There are two other very common ways to say "can't." First is the adverb 못, placed right before the verb like 안. It works only with action verbs, including pure Korean verbs like 가다 and 3-syllable Sino-Korean verbs like 변하다. For example, 나는 못 갈 거야 means "I won't be able to go." It can also mean you are bad at something, like 얘는 요리를 못해 (She can't cook well). The second way is Verb Stem + 지 못하다. This is more formal and, importantly, can be used with descriptive verbs. 완벽하지 못해요 means "It's not perfect," implying an effort was made but the result couldn't be reached.

### Slide 14 — Comparison: 할 수 없다 vs 못하다 vs 하지 못하다
- **On screen:** A detailed comparison table:
    - 할 수 없다: Objective impossibility, factual/logical.
    - 못 + Verb: Personal limitation/circumstance, most casual/versatile.
    - V.S + 지 못하다: Personal limitation, slightly more formal.
    - Visual notes: A highlighted box notes that 못 + Verb is the most versatile form covering all meanings of "cannot."

### Slide 15 — V.S + 지 (를/는/도) 못하다
- **On screen:** Adding markers after 지 for nuance.
    - 가지를 못해요: Emphatic, physical/emotional stuckness ("The car just won't move").
    - 가지는 못해요: Contrast ("I can't go, but [I'll call]").
    - 가지도 못해요: "Can't even go" (helplessness).
    - 어떻게 하지도 못해요: "I can't even do anything."
- **Ji's teaching:** While 못 + verb is straightforward, using -지 못하다 allows you to add particles like 를, 는, or 도 for richer nuance. 가지를 못해요 uses the object particle for emphasis, like saying a car is physically blocked and just won't move. 가지는 못해요 implies contrast; if you can't go home this weekend, you're hinting that you'll do something else instead, like calling. 가지도 못해요 means "I can't even go," showing helplessness. 어떻게 하지도 못해요 means "I can't even do anything," where 어떻게 하다 is used like "do something/anything."

### Slide 16 — Showing contrast ~지만
- **On screen:** Verb stem + 지만.
    - Comes from 하지만 (however).
    - Connects two sentences with clear contrast.
    - 받침 doesn't matter.
    - Examples: 피곤하지만 (tired but), 오지만 (raining but), 친절하지만 (kind but), 바쁘지만 (busy but).
- **Ji's teaching:** 지만 comes from 하지만. While 하지만 is used at the start of a sentence, ~지만 attaches to a verb stem to connect two contrasting ideas in one sentence. It is closer to "however" and emphasizes a clear contrast. It’s simple because you don't need to worry about 받침. For example, 저는 피곤하지만 갈 수 있어요 (I’m tired, but I can go) or 비가 오지만 우산이 없어요 (It’s raining, but I don’t have an umbrella).

### Slide 17 — Showing contrast ~데
- **On screen:** ~데 comes from 그런데 (but).
    - Shows softer, less direct contrast; can mean "but," "and," or "yet."
    - Conjugation Table:
        - Action: V.S. + 는데 (가는데, 믿는데). Irregular ㄹ: remove ㄹ (사는데).
        - Descriptive: 받침 O + 은데 (작은데); 받침 X + ㄴ데 (예쁜데). Irregular ㅂ: ㅂ → 우 + ㄴ데 (쉬운데).
        - Exceptions: 있다/없다 always use 있는데/없는데.
- **Ji's teaching:** ~데 comes from 그런데 (often shortened to 근데). Unlike the direct contrast of ~지만, ~데 is softer and provides background or setup. It can mean "but," "and," or "yet." Conjugation is trickier: action verbs always take ~는데 (even with ㄹ irregulars, you just remove the ㄹ, like 살다 to 사는데). Descriptive verbs depend on the 받침: use ~은데 if there is one (작은데) and ~ㄴ데 if there isn't (예쁜데). For ㅂ irregulars like 쉽다, it becomes 쉬운데. Remember that 있다 and 없다 always become 있는데 and 없는데.

### Slide 18 — ~데 Examples (Connector)
- **On screen:** Examples of ~데 as a connector:
    - Contrast: 작은데 맛있어요 (Small but delicious).
    - Background/Flow: 친구를 만났는데 같이 밥을 먹었어요 (I met a friend and [then] we ate together).
    - Linking qualities: 넓은데 조용해요 (Spacious and quiet).

### Slide 19 — ~데(요) Ending (Context marker)
- **On screen:** Ending a sentence with ~데(요) to imply contrast/context.
    - Nuances: 1) Indirect disagreement (비가 오는데요? - It's raining, though), 2) Surprise (맛있는데요? - It's good, though!), 3) Hesitation (바쁜데... - I'm busy, but...), 4) Curiosity (누군데? - Who is it, though?).
- **Ji's teaching:** You can end a sentence with ~데요 to imply contrast without stating it directly, similar to "though" in English. It has four main purposes. First, to politely disagree; if someone suggests meeting at the park, saying 비가 오는데요? gently points out the rain as a reason to meet elsewhere. Second, to express surprise; 맛있는데요! means "It’s actually good!" (perhaps contrary to expectations). Third, to show hesitation; 바쁜데... leaves the sentence trailing off to show you're busy without a hard rejection. Fourth, to ask with curiosity, like 누군데? (Who is it, though?), which is softer than a direct demand.

### Slide 20 — ~데(요) Examples (Ending)
- **On screen:** Dialogue examples:
    - A: How is this bag? B: It's pretty, but a little expensive, though (비싼데요).
    - A: Mom made this cookie. B: It's delicious! (맛있는데요!).
    - A: Can you talk? B: I'm busy but... (바쁜데...).
    - A: Who is that? B: He's my friend, though (친구인데요).
    - A: Are you a member? B: I came for the first time, though (왔는데요).

### Slide 21 — ~지만 VS ~데 Comparison
- **On screen:** A summary table comparing Origin, Tone, Meaning/Nuance, Sentence Position, and Translation.
    - ~지만: Strong, direct, "however," connector only.
    - ~데: Soft, indirect, "but/and/yet/while," connector or ending.

### Slide 22 — When (Verb stem + ㄹ / 을 때)
- **On screen:** 
    - Usage: 받침 O → 을 때 (받을 때); Vowel → ㄹ 때 (갈 때).
    - Irregulars: ㄹ (살 때), ㅂ (더울 때).
    - Meaning: 때 means "moment" or "time." "At the time when."
- **Ji's teaching:** To say "when," we use ㄹ/을 때. If the verb has a 받침, add 을 때 (받을 때). If it ends in a vowel, add ㄹ 때 (갈 때). For ㄹ irregulars like 살다, just add 때 (살 때). For ㅂ irregulars like 덥다, it becomes 더울 때. 때 literally means "moment," so think of this as "at the time when." It refers to specific, definitive situations. For example, 집에 갈 때 전화해 means "Call me at the time you go home."

### Slide 23 — ㄹ/을 때 More Examples
- **On screen:** List of examples including: 배고플 때 (when hungry), 시간 있을 때 (when I have time), 졸릴 때 (when sleepy), 음악을 들을 때 (when listening to music), 사진 찍을 때 (when taking a photo).
- **Ji's teaching:** Notice that the 때 clause usually comes first in the sentence. For example, 음악을 들을 때 집중이 잘 돼요 (I can focus well when I listen to music).

### Slide 24 — If & When (Verb stem + 면 / 으면)
- **On screen:** 
    - Usage: 받침 O → 으면 (닫으면); Vowel → 면 (오면).
    - Irregulars: ㄹ (알면), ㅂ (더우면).
    - Meaning: Can mean both "If" and "When." Used for hypothetical situations.
    - Optional: Add 만약(에) to emphasize "if."
- **Ji's teaching:** 면 / 으면 can mean both "if" and "when." Use 으면 after a 받침 (닫으면) and 면 after a vowel (오면). For ㄹ verbs like 알다, just add 면 (알면). For ㅂ irregulars, it becomes 더우면. Unlike 때, which is for definite moments, 면 is for possibilities—things that may or may not happen. You can add 만약(에) at the beginning to make it clearly hypothetical. In some dialects, you might hear it pronounced as "믄." 도착하면 연락 주세요 is a case where "when" is more appropriate because the arrival is expected.

### Slide 25 — 면 / 으면 More Examples
- **On screen:** Examples including: 피곤하면 (if tired), 시간 있으면 (if you have time), 창문을 열면 (when you open the window), 비 오면 (if it rains), 한국 가면 (if/when you go to Korea).
- **Ji's teaching:** Some of these feel more like "if" (hypothetical), like 시간 있으면 같이 영화 보자 (If you have time, let's watch a movie). Others feel like "when" (factual result), like 창문을 열면 바람이 들어와요 (When you open the window, wind comes in).

### Slide 26 — (으)면 vs (으)ㄹ 때 Comparison
- **On screen:** A table distinguishing the two:
    - ㄹ/을 때: Definite "When," specific time/moment.
    - 면 / 으면: Hypothetical "If" or "When," conditional/uncertain.

### Slide 27 — 면 / 으면 + 좋겠다 = “I hope…”
- **On screen:** 
    - Stem + (으)면 좋겠다: "It would be good if..." → "I hope..."
    - Past stem + 으면 좋겠다: Adds slight emphasis/emotion ("If it WERE like this").
    - Example: 빨리 여자친구가 생겼으면 좋겠어요 (I hope I get a girlfriend soon).
    - Visual note: 생기다 = To come into being/appear.
- **Ji's teaching:** This structure is used to express hope or desire. Literally, it means "It would be good if..." For example, 잘 지내면 좋겠어 means "I hope you do well." Using the past tense stem, like 생겼으면 좋겠어요, adds a bit more emotion, similar to the English "If it *were* like this." In Korean, we don't say "get a girlfriend"; we say "a girlfriend appears" (생기다).

### Slide 28 — 되다 Meanings
- **On screen:** Four core meanings:
    1. To become (건축가가 되고 싶어요).
    2. To be done / Passive of 하다 (집중이 잘 돼요; 됐어요 - "It's done/Forget it").
    3. To work / function (컴퓨터가 잘 안 돼요).
    4. To be okay / acceptable (안 돼 - "Not okay"; 가도 돼요? - "May I go?").
- **Ji's teaching:** 되다 is as important as 하다. It has four main categories of meaning. First, "to become," like wanting to become an architect. Second, it's the passive of 하다, meaning "to be done." You'll hear 됐어요 a lot; depending on tone, it can mean "Forget it" or "No thanks." Third, it means "to work" or "function," like a computer not working. Finally, it means "to be okay." 안 돼 means "You can't" or "It's not okay," which is different from just saying "no."

### Slide 33 — Connectors + 되다
- **On screen:** Korean uses 되다 after connectors to express "should," "may," or "have to."
    - Table of 6 forms: 1) ~면 되다 (You could/can just), 2) ~면 안 되다 (Shouldn't), 3) ~도 되다 (May), 4) Negative + 도 되다 (Don't have to), 5) ~야 되다 (Have to), 6) Negative + 야 되다 (Should NOT - strong).
- **Ji's teaching:** Korean doesn't have modal helping verbs like English. Instead, we use 되다 after connectors, where it means "to be okay" or "acceptable." This is a major difference between the two languages. We will focus on the connectors 면, 도, and 야.

### Slide 34 — Verb Stem + 면 / 으면 되다
- **On screen:** 
    - 면 / 으면 되다 = To be okay if → [Could / Can just / Just have to]. Example: 뭐 하면 돼요? (What do I do now?).
    - 면 / 으면 안 되다 = Not okay if → [Shouldn’t]. Example: 만지시면 안 돼요 (You shouldn't touch that).
- **Ji's teaching:** Combined with 되다, 면 means "to be okay if." 뭐 하면 돼요? literally asks "What is okay if I do now?" but means "What should I do?" Conversely, 면 안 되다 means "not okay if," which translates to "shouldn't."

### Slide 35 — Verb Stem + 아/어/여도 + 되다
- **On screen:** 
    - 아/어/여 + 도 = "even if/when/though."
    - 아/어/여도 되다 = "may" (positive) or "don't have to" (negative). Used for permission.
    - Adverbs for emphasis: 아무리 (no matter how much), 꼭/굳이 (necessarily).
    - Examples: 만져봐도 돼요? (May I touch it?); 안 입어도 돼요 (You don't have to wear it).
- **Ji's teaching:** 도 means "even." So 아/어/여도 되다 means it's okay "even if" you do something, which translates to "may." It's used for asking permission, like 그림을 만져봐도 돼요? (May I touch the painting?). In the negative, it means "don't have to." You can add adverbs like 아무리 for extreme situations or 꼭/굳이 for emphasis, like saying you don't *necessarily* have to wear a swimsuit.

### Slide 36 — Verb Stem + 아/어/여야 + 되다 / 하다
- **On screen:** 
    - 야 = "only if." Example: 매워야 맛있어요 (Tasty only if spicy).
    - ~야 되다 = "must," "have to," "need to." Literally: "acceptable only if."
    - ~야 하다 = same as ~야 되다, but slightly more formal/less common in speech.
    - Examples: 모아야 돼요 (need to gather), 출근해야 됩니다 (have to go to work).
- **Ji's teaching:** The suffix 야 means "only if." When combined with 되다, it means something is acceptable *only if* a condition is met, which translates to "must" or "have to." For example, 내일 아침에 출근해야 됩니다 (I have to go to work tomorrow). ~야 하다 is the same but more formal and less common in spoken Korean.

## Teaching Points

- **The Noun 수 (Ability/Way):** Understanding that 수 is a noun meaning "way" or "ability" helps clarify why ㄹ/을 수 있다/없다 literally means "there is/isn't a way." This explains the strong nuance of impossibility in the negative form.
- **Negation Nuances (못 vs. 수 없다):** 
    - **못** is used for personal inability or lack of skill (e.g., "I'm bad at cooking" or "I can't go because I'm busy").
    - **수 없다** is used for objective impossibility or situations out of one's control (e.g., "The road is blocked, so I can't go").
    - **지 못하다** is the formal version of 못 and is the only one of these three that can negate descriptive verbs (adjectives).
- **Contrast Connectors (~지만 vs. ~데):** 
    - **~지만** is a strong, direct "but/however." 
    - **~데** is softer and often provides background information ("...and/so..."). It is also highly versatile as a sentence ending (~데요) to show hesitation, surprise, or polite disagreement.
- **Time vs. Condition (때 vs. 면):** 
    - **때** refers to a definite, specific moment in time. 
    - **면** refers to a hypothetical condition (if) or a general possibility (when).
- **The Power of 되다:** In Korean, modal concepts like "should," "may," and "must" are built by combining connectors with 되다 (to be okay/acceptable).
    - **면 되다** = "It's okay if..." (You can just...)
    - **도 되다** = "It's okay even if..." (You may...)
    - **야 되다** = "It's okay ONLY if..." (You must...)
