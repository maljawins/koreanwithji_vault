## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor explains that while the English word "should" is a single, versatile modal verb, Korean does not have a direct one-to-one grammatical equivalent. Instead, Korean speakers use various descriptive constructions to convey the feeling of obligation, recommendation, or preference. The lesson covers several ways to express "should" and "should not," ranging from beginner-friendly phrases to more nuanced intermediate structures.

The learning arc begins with the concept of "preferable actions" using nominalization, moves into conditional "if-then" structures that evaluate the goodness or acceptability of an action, and concludes with specific past-tense forms used for expressing regret or giving advice about what someone "should have" done. Throughout the video, the instructor emphasizes the literal meanings of these phrases to help students understand the underlying logic of Korean expression.

---

### Expressing Preference with ~는 것이 낫다
The first method introduced involves saying that an action is "preferable" or "better." To form this, you take an action verb stem and attach the nominalizing suffix **~는 것** followed by the subject marker **이**. This transforms the verb into a noun phrase meaning "the act of [doing]." For example, **가다 (to go)** becomes **가는 것이 (the act of going)**. If a verb stem ends in **ㄹ**, such as **놀다 (to play)**, the **ㄹ** is dropped before adding the suffix, resulting in **노는 것**.

This nominalized phrase is then paired with the verb **낫다**, which literally means "to be preferable" or "to be better." In a polite sentence, this conjugates to **나아요**. Therefore, **가는 것이 나아요** literally means "The act of going is preferable," which translates naturally as "You should go." To express "should not," you simply make the action verb negative. For instance, **안 가는 것이 나아요** means "Not going is preferable" or "You should not go." Another example provided is **그 사람을 안 믿는 것이 나아요**, meaning "It is preferable not to trust that person" or "You should not trust that person."

---

### Giving Advice with ~면 좋다 and ~면 안 좋다
A very common textbook method for expressing "should" is using the conditional **~(으)면 (if)** combined with **좋다 (to be good)**. This construction literally means "If you do [verb], it is good." The instructor suggests that this is equivalent to the English sentiment "I think you should..." because it sounds like a recommendation or an opinion rather than a strict requirement.

Examples of this form include:
- **집 빨리 청소하면 좋아요.** / It is good if you clean the house quickly. (You should clean the house right away.)
- **수업에 집중하면 좋아요.** / It is good if you focus on class. (You should focus on class.)

To say "should not," you use **~면 안 좋다**, meaning "If you do [verb], it is not good." For example, **밤에 라면을 먹으면 안 좋아요** translates to "It's not good if you eat ramen at night," or "I don't think you should eat ramen at night."

---

### Expressing Hope or Wishes with ~면 좋겠다
The instructor notes that adding the suffix **~겠다** to the **~면 좋다** form changes the meaning from a general recommendation to a personal hope or wish. **~면 좋겠다** literally means "It would be good if..." This can be used in the present or past tense. **비가 오면 좋겠어요** and **비가 왔으면 좋겠어요** both mean "I wish it would rain" or "I hope it rains." The instructor explains that using the past tense (**왔으면**) makes the hope feel slightly more emphatic or stronger, but both are common.

---

### Acceptability and Commands with ~면 되다 and ~면 안 되다
While **~면 좋다** evaluates whether an action is "good," **~면 되다** evaluates whether an action is "acceptable" or "okay." **되다** literally means "to work," "to function," or "to be acceptable." Using **지금 가면 돼요** suggests that going now is the acceptable course of action, often implying that if you don't go now, there might be a problem or you might miss your turn.

The negative version, **~면 안 되다**, is extremely common for giving commands or stating prohibitions. It literally means "If you do [verb], it is not acceptable."
- **뛰면 안 돼요.** / If you run, it's not okay. (No running / You should not run.)
- **그거 먹으면 안 돼.** / If you eat that, it's not okay. (You shouldn't eat that.)

The instructor highlights the military motto **하면 된다**, which literally means "If you do it, it works," conveying a "can-do" spirit or the idea that "you should just do it."

---

### Regret and Advice for the Past: ~지 그랬다 and ~을 걸 그랬다
To express "should have" (regret or advice about the past), Korean uses different forms depending on who the subject is.

**For the Second Person ("You should have"):**
Use the verb stem + **~지 그랬다**. This is used to tell someone else what they should have done. It is informal but not necessarily rude.
- **같이 가지 그랬어.** / You should have gone with us. (Literally: Why didn't you go together?)
- **더 일찍 말하지 그랬어.** / You should have said that earlier.
- **더 시키지 그랬어요.** / You should have ordered more.

**For the First Person ("I should have"):**
Use the verb stem + **~(으)을 걸 (그랬다)**. The word **그랬다** is optional when speaking about yourself.
- **나도 갈 걸.** / I should have gone too.
- **할 걸 그랬어요.** / I should have done it.

To say "I should not have," you use the negative **~지 말다** base. For example, **물어보지 말 걸** means "I should not have asked." The instructor cautions that while **~을 걸** is primarily for the first person, it can occasionally be used for the second person if followed by **그랬다**, but **~지 그랬어** is much more common and recommended for "you."
