### Overview

The lesson focuses on two primary verb endings: **~야 되다** and **~야 하다**. While both convey a similar core meaning, the instructor highlights a nuance in their usage and intensity, providing a clear framework for when to choose one over the other.

The learning arc begins with the structural formation of these endings, followed by a detailed breakdown of conjugation across different politeness levels. ---

### Two Ways to Say "Have to"
There are two main structures used to express that one must or has to do something. The first is attaching **~야 되다** to a verb stem, and the second is attaching **~야 하다**. The instructor explains that using **되다 (to become)** is significantly more common in daily conversation. In contrast, using **하다 (to do)** feels slightly less common and carries a much stronger, more authoritative tone. Because of this increased intensity, the **~야 하다** structure is often more accurately translated as "must" rather than just "have to."

---

### Conjugation Rules for 되다 and 하다
Both auxiliary verbs follow standard conjugation patterns across different speech levels. For the more common **되다**, the formal polite forms are **됩니다** (statement) and **됩니까?** (question). The casual polite form is **돼요**, while dropping the **요** results in the informal/slang form **돼**. Additionally, the form **된다** is frequently encountered in written print or when quoting someone.

The conjugation for **하다** follows a nearly identical pattern. The formal polite forms are **합니다** and **합니까?**. The casual polite form is **해요**, and the informal version is simply **해**. Just like its counterpart, the form **한다** is the standard for written materials and quotations. The instructor notes that for any verb already ending in **하다**, the construction is simplified by changing that **하다** to **해야** before adding the final conjugated form of **되다** or **하다**.

---

### Practical Examples and Sentence Construction
The instructor provides several examples to illustrate these rules in context. To say "I have to eat" or "I need to eat," one would say:
> **밥을 먹어야 됩니다.** / I have to eat. (Formal)

To express a stronger sense of "must," such as going to school, the sentence becomes:
> **지금 학교에 가야 합니다.** / I must go to school now.

The lesson demonstrates the versatility of this grammar by applying it to various verbs like cooking, swimming, working, and traveling. For instance, expressing a strong necessity to travel is shown as:
> **저는 유럽을 여행해야 합니다.** / I must travel Europe.

To ask a question about obligation, the instructor introduces the adverb **꼭**, which means "surely," "certainly," or "at all costs." It is used here to mean "really" or "definitely":
> **꼭 일을 해야 됩니까?** / Do you really have to work?

Further examples include:
> **만들어야 합니다.** / I must make (it).
> **친구에게 편지를 써야 돼요.** / I need to write a letter to my friend.
> **저는 한국말을 더 배워야 합니다.** / I must learn more Korean.
