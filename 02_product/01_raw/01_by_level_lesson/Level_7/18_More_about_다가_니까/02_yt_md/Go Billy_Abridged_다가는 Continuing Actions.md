### Overview
The instructor introduces the grammar form 다가는, which is used to express a warning about a potential negative outcome if a current action or state continues. While it shares an etymological root with the simpler form 다가, its pragmatic function is distinct: it focuses on the speaker's opinion that a bad result is inevitable if the subject "keeps doing" what they are doing. The lesson covers construction, common sentence endings, and the specific nuances that separate it from other conditional forms like (으)면.

The learning arc begins with basic conjugation and speech variations, moves into the core conceptual meaning of "warning," and then details specific grammatical restrictions regarding tense. The instructor provides numerous practical examples ranging from health warnings to social consequences, and concludes by explaining a less common variation using the past tense stem for purely hypothetical warnings.

### Construction and Speech Variations
To form this grammar point, one simply takes a verb stem and attaches 다가는. In casual spoken Korean, this is frequently shortened to 다간. For example, the verb 하다 (to do) becomes 하다가는 or 하다간. This form can be used with both action verbs and descriptive verbs. The instructor notes that while 다가는 and the simpler 다가 (which indicates an interruption) come from the same original root, they are used very differently in modern Korean.

---

### The Core Meaning: Warning of Negative Consequences
The primary function of 다가는 is to express the speaker's opinion that something negative will happen if a specific action or state continues. It is essentially a tool for issuing a warning. The logic follows: "If you keep doing [Verb], then [Negative Result] will happen." Unlike a neutral "if" statement, 다가는 implies that the action is already occurring or is very likely to occur, and the speaker is projecting a bad outcome based on that continuation.

---

### Grammatical Restrictions and Common Endings
There is a strict tense restriction when using 다가는: the sentence must end in the present or future tense. Using the past tense in the concluding clause is grammatically incorrect because one cannot "keep doing" something and have it result in a completed past event. Common endings that follow 다가는 include:
- **~ㄹ/을 것이다** (Future certainty: "Something will happen")
- **~ㄹ/을지도 모르다** (Possibility: "Something might happen")
- **~ㄴ/은/ㄹ 것 같다** (Opinion/Likelihood: "I think something will happen")
- **~겠어요** (Stronger future supposition: "It's going to happen")

---

### Comparison with (으)면
While both 다가는 and (으)면 can translate to "if" in English, they are not interchangeable. (으)면 is a general hypothetical that can be used for both positive and negative outcomes and does not necessarily imply the action is currently happening. In contrast, 다가는 is specifically for warnings and negative results. Furthermore, 다가는 assumes the action is currently in progress; the "if" part refers to the *continuation* of that action, whereas (으)면 is a broader hypothetical about whether the action happens at all.

---

### Emphasis with Adverbs
The instructor highlights three adverbs often used to strengthen the warning: 이렇게 (like this), 그렇게 (like that), and 저렇게 (like that over there). These are used to emphasize the current manner in which the action is being performed. For instance, saying "If you keep doing it *like this* (이렇게)..." makes the warning more immediate and specific to the listener's current behavior.

---

### Usage with the Past Tense Stem
A less common variation involves attaching 다가는 to the past tense stem (e.g., 했다가는). When used this way, the meaning shifts from "if you keep doing" to a more purely hypothetical "if you were to do." It still carries a warning of a negative result, but it describes a situation that hasn't started yet. The instructor notes that for this specific hypothetical meaning, native speakers are more likely to use the standard (으)면 form, but 했다가는 remains a valid option for emphasizing a warning.

---

### Practical Examples
The instructor provides several sentences to demonstrate the form in context:
- **카라멜을 매일 먹다가는 이가 다 망가질 거예요.** / If you keep eating caramel every day, your teeth are going to be ruined.
- **이렇게 자다가는 목디스크가 생기겠어요.** / If you keep sleeping like this, you're going to get neck problems (a cervical disc herniation).
- **이렇게 계속 바쁘다가는 남자친구 얼굴도 잊어버리겠어요.** / If I keep being busy like this, I'm going to even forget my boyfriend's face.
- **우물쭈물하다가는 다 놓치게 될지도 몰라요.** / If you keep hesitating, you might end up missing out on everything.

- **이런 날씨에 밖에서 놀다가는 감기에 걸릴지도 몰라요.** / If you keep playing outside in this kind of weather, you might catch a cold.
