**Slide 2: Topics**

Alright, so today we’re going to learn how to say “because” in Korean—or more accurately, things that *feel* similar to “because” in English. As you probably know by now, not everything translates word-for-word, so we’ll focus on how Korean actually expresses reasons or causes in natural ways.

We have 4 forms to cover today. And each one has its own style and use.

First, we have **아/어 + 서**. You actually used this really well in one of your sentences last time!

This form is *super* common in daily conversation and has three major uses. So, we’re going to about them in detail.

Next, we’ll look at **아/어 가지고**, or the shortened form **갖고**. It’s super similar to ~서. So, once you understand ~서, this form will come naturally.

Then we’ll move on to **(으)니까** and **때문에**, which are the closest to the English meaning of “because.”

We’ll go through each one, step by step—how to make it, when to use it, and what makes it unique.

Let’s get started**.**

**Slide 3: Cause Comes First in Korean**

So, here’s one important thing to understand when it comes to expressing reasons in Korean:

In Korean, the cause always comes first—before the effect.

And I don’t even think I have to explain why that is, because this is just a universal fact.

But in English, it’s the opposite.
We usually say the effect first, and then give the reason.
Like: *“I was late because I missed the bus.”*

In Korean, it’s flipped:
*“Because I missed the bus, I was late.”*

So keep this in mind:
**“Because A comma then B.”** → Cause, then Effect.

And this rule applies to every form we’re going to learn today. With each of them, the cause always comes before the effect.

But—sometimes—you want to explain the reason *afterward,* especially if it flows better that way in the conversation.
In that case, you can start your next sentence with **왜냐하면**, which literally means, *“If you ask me why…”*

Here’s an example:
**저희 공부 진짜 열심히 했어요. 왜냐하면 다음 주에 시험이 있거든요.**
“We studied really hard. Because—well, if you ask me why—it’s because we have a test next week.”

And **거든요** at the end is a **context marker.**

It’s like saying, “Hey, Here’s the background or the reason for what I just said.”

It’s kind of like saying, *“We have a test next week, you know.”*

Alright, with that in mind, let’s move into the first and most common form: **아/어 + 서.**

**Slide 4: What is 서?**

This is one of the most essential connectors in Korean, and it’s one that a lot of learners misunderstand at first. So I really want you to get this: **~서 is NOT the same as "because" or “so.”**

Now, Let’s start with this word: **그래서, which** DOES translate to “so.” But it actually comes from **그렇다 + 서.** But it’s just ONE of the usages of 서.

A lot of learners just memorize ~서 as “because,” or “so,” and yes, sometimes it works like that — That’s just **one** of the ways 서 is used — not the full picture.

A lot of learners just learn 그래서 first, then memorize ~서 as “because” or “so.” It works like that sometimes, but that’s not what 서 actually means.

**서 is actually an abstract concept and there’s no direct translation in English.**

Instead, I want you to think of it THIS way: It shows a kind of **flow** or **continuity** between two events, states, or actions. There’s some kind of connection between A and B — that’s what 서 expresses.

Here’s another way to help you get this: Think about the particles **에** and **에서**.

If you say: **집에 있어요 – “I’m at home,”
that’s just a state — nothing is happening. Just stillness.**

But if you say:
**집에서 운동해요** – “I work out at home,”
Now there’s **action**, **flow**, movement.

And that **서** in **에서** is doing exactly that — it shows that something is **happening** in that space.

And this is what the form ~서 does, too. It connects one thing to another.

And now with that in mind, ~서 as a sentence connector can be used in **three main uses:**

1. **Cause and Effect** – Like “I was tired, so I went home.”
2. **Sequential Actions** – Like “I went to the store and bought milk.”
3. **Means or Method** – Like “I made it using glue.”

We’ll go through each of these one by one. There’s also a bonus usage that we’ll touch on later.

So again, don’t just memorize ~서 as “because.” Or “so” The key word I want you to remember is **continuity.** That’s the heart of ~서.

Alright, but let’s look at how to make the ~서 form first.

**Slide 5: How to make the ~서 form.**

The way you make this form is simple:
**Verb stem + 아 / 어 + 서**
So for example:

* 하다 → **해서**
* 보다 → **봐서**
* 먹다 → **먹어서**

So, 운동을 해서 배가 고팠어요. I worked out, so I was hungry.

Quick side note — what’s this 가 doing in **배가 고프다?
배** means “stomach,” and **고프다** is the descriptive verb for “to be hungry.”
So **가** is just marking **배** as the subject — literally, “The stomach is hungry.”

Now, let’s talk about **tense** it can take.

🟢 It can be in the **present tense** like 해**서**, **먹어서** and 봐서

🟢 It can also be in the **future tense**, we use **ㄹ/을 거라서**:
For example: **할 거라서**

**Now, you might be wondering — what is that 라 in 거라서?
It only shows up with 이다 or 아니다, and it comes from the full form ㄹ/을 것이**다.

I’ll explain this a bit more on the next slide.

🔴 But you **cannot** use the past tense directly with ~서.
So don’t say: **했어서** ❌

Though you might hear it sometimes. — since around 2010, some Koreans have started saying things like **했어서** to put emphasis on the past. But according to standard grammar rules, this is incorrect. still incorrect.

So to keep things clean and natural, stick with the **present** or **future** forms when using ~서.

Slide 6: 이다 / 아니다 + 서

Now let’s talk about how to use ~서 with 이다 and 아니다.

There are two ways to do it:

* 이라서 / 아니라서
* 이어서 / 아니어서

✅ Both versions are grammatically correct. But in daily conversation, you’ll hear (이)라서 a lot more often.

So when do you use 이라서 and when do you use 라서? It depends on whether the word ends in a 받침 — a final consonant.

🟢 If the word ends in a consonant (받침), you use 이라서
예:
지금 봄이라서 날씨가 건조해요.
“It’s spring now, so the weather is dry.”

🟢 If the word ends in a vowel, you drop the 이 and just use 라서
예:
내일 파티에 갈 거라서 기대돼요.
(= 갈 것이라서)
“I’m going to the party tomorrow, so I’m excited.”

Just a quick note — 기대 means “expectation,” or looking forward to something.

Now, here’s something important:
With 라서, the 서 part can be dropped in casual or shortened speech.
But you can’t drop 서 with 이어서 or 여서 — it always needs to be there.

So what about 이어서 and 여서?

🟢 Same logic:

* If the word ends in a consonant: use 이어서
  예:
  이건 펜이어서 지우개로 못 지워요.
  “This is a pen, so you can’t erase it with an eraser.”
* If the word ends in a vowel: use 여서
  예:
  지루한 영화여서 잠들었어요.
  “It was a boring movie, so I fell asleep.”

So to wrap it up:

* Both versions are fine.
* (이)라서 is more commonly used in speech.
* And only 라서 can drop the 서 — 이어서/여서 can’t.

Alright, now let’s take a closer look at the first usage of ~서: Cause & Effect.

**Slide 7: ~서 Usage #1 — Cause & Effect**

**This is the most standard and common use of ~서 — where A causes B.**

예: 집까지 걸어가서 다리가 아팠어요.
“I walked home, so my legs hurt.”

음식이 너무 매워서 못 먹겠어요.
“The food is too spicy, so I don’t think I can eat it.”

But there’s one important restriction here:

You can’t use ~서 with suggestions or commands — like
“Let’s do it because…” or “Do this because…”

For that, you need to use a different form: ~니까.
(We’ll cover that in the next lesson.)

So why not ~서?

Because a cause can’t “cause” a suggestion or command. Suggestions and commands are a conscious decision — not a natural result. They come from someone’s intent, not automatic flow. So it sounds awkward in these examples.

**Let’s compare:**

**❌ 더워서 바다에 가자.
✅ 더우니까 바다에 가자.
→ “Because it’s hot, let’s go to the beach.”**

**❌ 비가 와서 이 우산을 가져가세요.
✅ 비가 오니까 이 우산을 가져가세요.
→ “Because it’s raining, take this umbrella.”**

**Slide 9: ~서 Usage #2 — Sequential Actions**

Now let’s move on to the second major use of ~서.

It’s used to connect **two actions that happen one after another**, where the **first action naturally leads into the second**.

Let’s look at some examples:

**버스 정류장까지 걸어가서 버스를 탔어요.**
“I walked to the bus stop and then took the bus.”
👉 "At the bus stop" is implied here.

**가게에 가서 우유를 샀어요.**
“I went to the store and then bought milk.”
👉 "At the store" is implied.

In both cases, the first action flows directly into the next like a **chain reaction**.

Now let’s think about this in English:
**“Go do it!”** — You’re not just saying “go” and “do it” as separate things.
You mean: Go somewhere, and once you’re there, do it.

Same in Korean with: **가서 해!**
It means: “Go (to a place), and then do it *there*.”
So again, **the two actions are linked**.

This brings us to the key difference between **~고** and **~서**.

Here’s a sentence with **~고**:

❌ **친구를 만나고 카페에 갔어요.**
This sounds like: You met your friend, that ended, and then you went to the café — maybe by yourself.

But with **~서**:

✅ **친구를 만나서 카페에 갔어요.**
Now it means: You met your friend, and **together**, you went to the café.
There’s a **natural sequence and flow** between the two actions.

So just remember:

* **~고** just lists A and B
* **~서** shows flow and sequence. One leads into the next.

**Slide 11: ~서 Usage #3 Means & Method**

Alright, this is the third major usage of ~서 — The first action shows *how* the second action happened.

We’re talking about method, tool, or means. So, basically: *“By doing A, you achieve B.”*
Let’s look at a few examples:

고기를 소스에 찍어서 먹었어요.
“I dipped the meat in sauce and ate it.”
→ The method of eating was dipping it in sauce.

앱을 사용해서 주문했어요.
“I ordered it using the app.”
→ The app was the tool or method I used to order.

나는 걸어서 학교에 가요.
“I go to school by walking.”
→ Walking is the method I use to get to school.

벽에 그림을 걸어서 집을 꾸몄어요.
“I decorated the house by hanging a painting on the wall.”
→ Hanging the painting was the way I decorated.

Quick sidenote: notice the different verb stems for these two verbs:

* 걷다 (to walk) → 걸어서 → irregular ㄷ
* 걸다 (to hang) → 걸어서 → regular

Totally different verbs, but they just happen to look similar once you add ~서.

**Slide 13: 📝 3 usage of ~서 Summary**

Let’s wrap up with a quick summary of the three major uses of **~서**.

🔹 **1. Cause → Effect**
One event naturally causes the next to happen.
예:
**저는 한국 사람이라서 한국말을 잘 해요.**
“I’m Korean, so I speak Korean well.”

🔹 **2. Sequential Actions**
Two connected actions happening one after another.
예:
**친구를 만나서 점심을 먹었어요.**
“I met a friend and then had lunch.”
(You ate together — it’s part of the same flow.)

🔹 **3. Means / Method**
Describes **how** something was done.
예:
**앱을 사용해서 음식을 주문했어요.**
“I ordered food by using the app.”

So the key idea to remember is continuity: so you can visualize **~서** as a long arrow

Each use shows a **continuous flow** — one thing leads right into the next.

Alright — now that you’ve seen all three, we’re ready to compare this with **~니까** next!

**Slide 14: 있어서 — Special Usage:**

Now, here’s the special usage of the 서 form I talked about earlier. And it’s **있어서**.

**1. First — the normal usage:**

**있다** means *to exist* or *to have*, so naturally:

* **새는 날개가 있어서 날 수 있어요.**
  “Birds have wings, so they can fly.”
* **돈이 있어서 여행을 갔어요.**
  “I had money, so I went on a trip.”

These are typical *cause-and-effect* sentences. Having something (A) allows you to do something (B).

**2. But there’s a special usage with 에게 / 한테 / 께 있어서**

This changes the meaning completely.

🔹 It’s not about *existence* anymore — it’s about **perspective**.
🔹 Think: *To me* / *In their eyes* / *As far as [someone] is concerned*
And in this case, **서** is often dropped in speech.

Let’s look at an example:

* **내게 있어(서) 너는 소중한 친구야.**
  “To me, you’re a precious friend.”
  *(내게 = to me — short for 나에게)*

Another example:

* **그 사람한테 있어(서) SNS가 전부예요.**
  “To her, social media is everything.”
  *(SNS = Social Media — Konglish for Social Networking Service)*

⚡ So, in these cases, **있어서** doesn’t mean “It exists, so \_\_\_.”
Instead, it shows **value** or **importance** from someone’s personal perspective.

Think of it like this: **A에게 있어서 B** = *To A, B holds meaning / value*

**Slide 15: 아/어 가지고 (갖고) Similar to ~서**

Now let’s take a look at another connector:
**아/어 가지고** — often shortened to **갖고** when speaking.

This form is super common in everyday Korean, and it’s really similar to **~서**.

**🔹 Even the way you make it is similar to 서.**

You just take the verb stem and attach: **아/어 + 가지고**

Examples:

* **먹다 → 먹어가지고**
* **사다 → 사가지고**

This comes from the verb **가지다**, which means “to have” or “to hold.” So literally, **가지고** means “have and then…”,

**✅ What does it express?**

Just like ~서, it shows:

* **One action leads into the next**
* **Used to mean**: “so,” “and then,” or sometimes “with”

It’s almost the same idea as 서

You’ll see it used in **two main ways**:

**1️⃣ Cause & Effect**

* **길이 막혀가지고 늦었어요.**
  “The road was jammed, so I was late.”

**2️⃣ Sequential Actions**

* **돈을 찾아가지고 밥을 먹었어요.**
  “I withdrew money and then ate.”

Again, this is just like ~서 — just more casual or spoken in tone.

**BUT**

* ❌ **가지고** sounds awkward when used with greetings or emotions
  You wouldn’t say:
  **만나가지고 반갑습니다 ❌**
  Instead, use:
  **만나서 반갑습니다 ✅**

✅ And for “means or method,” **가지고** is only used with nouns:

* + **카드 갖고 계산했어요.**
    “I paid with the card.”

So overall:

* **가지고 = ~서**, but more casual
* **갖고** is the shortened, spoken version
* Use it for cause-effect or sequences, not emotions or greetings

**Slide 16: Verb Stem + (으)니까**

**Now let’s move on to ~(으)니까**

**🔹 So what is 니까?**

니까 does **mean “because.” So naturally, it** puts more **emphasis** on the reason than **~서.**

And if you look at this word, **그러니까** which is super common. 그러니까 comes from **그렇다** (to be so) + **니까**
= And it can be translated as “That’s why” or “therefore” in some cases.

**🔹 Other points to keep in mind:**

* ~니까 is **slightly informal**, but not rude ▶ Totally fine in most spoken situations
* So, if you want to sound more formal you can
  ➤ Drop 까: **하니까 → 하니**
  ➤ Or use **때문에**, which we will learn next.

**So, the key difference between 서 and 니까.**

* **~서** shows a natural connection or flow between actions.
  ▶ Think: A causes B naturally
* **~니까** is all about the reason. So you can use this form even when the two events are not connected.
  ▶ And you’re specifically pointing out the *why*.

They can both be “translated” as “because,” but ~니까 feels closer to it than 서.

**Slide 17: Key features of ~니까**

Alright — now let’s look at some important things to know about ~니까.

🔹 First — You CAN use ~니까 with suggestions and commands.

This is a big difference from ~서. Remember?
You couldn’t say “Let’s do it because…” with ~서.
But with ~니까, it works.

Let’s see:

배고프니까 빨리 먹자.
“Because we’re hungry, let’s hurry up and eat.”

Totally natural!

Another one:

지금 갈 거니까 조금만 기다려.
“Because I will go now, wait just a little.”
(Meaning: I’m on my way, so hang in there.)

So whenever you want to give a suggestion or command *because of* a reason, ~니까 is the one to use.

**🔹 ~니까 can be used in past tense unlike the 서 form.**

Let’s look at a dialogue:

A: **어제 전화했는데 왜 안 받았어?**
“I called you yesterday — why didn’t you pick up?”

B: **일하고 있었으니까요.**
“Because I was working.”

**If you were to use 있었어서 it would be wrong but 있었으니까 is perfectly fine.**

🔹 But ~니까 CAN’T be used for emotions. This is a key restriction.

❌ You can’t say:

만나니까 반갑습니다.
“Because I meet you, I’m glad.” That just sounds weird — even in English.

✅ Instead, use:

만나서 반갑습니다.
“Nice to meet you.”
This feels natural — just a simple connection.

So for emotions like *thank you*, *glad*, *sorry*, etc., stick with ~서.

**Slide 18: Key features of ~니까**

**So how do you make this form? It’s simple**

**🔹 Step 1: Just attach ~(으)니까 or 니까 to the verb stem.**

* If the verb ends in a **consonant (받침)** → add **으니까**  ike **먹으니까**
  “Because I eat” / “Because I ate” (context decides)
* If the verb ends in a **vowel** → use **니까 like 가니까**
  “Because I go” / “Because I went”

**🔸 Irregular verbs — just a couple to remember.**

1️⃣ **ㄹ-ending verbs** → Drop the ㄹ, then add ~니까

살다 → **사니까** “Because I live”

2️⃣ **ㅂ-ending descriptive verbs** → ㅂ changes to **우**, then add ~니까

쉽다 → **쉬우니까** “Because it’s easy”

**🔹 What about the verb 이다 (to be)?**

Same rule applies, but slightly different:

* If it follows a consonant → **이니까**

**물이니까** → “Because it’s water”

* If it follows a vowel → **니까**

**친구니까** → “Because (he/she) is a friend”

**🔹 And you can also apply the same rule with the past tense and the future tense like this.:**

* **Past tense**:

하다 → **했으니까** “Because I did”

* **Future tense**:

하다 → **할 거니까** “Because I will do (it)”

So you can use ~니까 in the present, past, or even future — very flexible!

**Slide 22: What is 때문?**

**Let’s talk about 때문.**

**때문 is actually a noun — it literally means *“the cause”* or *“the reason.”*
So when you say something 때문에, you’re literally saying *“because of \_\_\_.”***

**This is the most formal way to say “because.” You’ll often hear it in news, writing, or serious conversations.**

**But—because it directly points to the reason, it can sometimes sound a little blunt or even accusatory. So, has a slightly negative connotation. So, if you want to talk about a positive reason, you can use 덕분 instead. For example:
시험에 합격했어요. 다 선생님 덕분이에요.
→ “I passed the exam. It’s all thanks to you, teacher.”**

**But note that 덕분 can only be used with nouns, not verbs. So just keep that in mind.**

**And also, you can’t use 때문에 with commands or suggestions. It just sounds awkward
So this one:
❌ 감기 때문에 병원에 가세요. → Sounds off.
✅ 감기에 걸렸으니까 병원에 가세요.**

**Slide 23: 때문 Usage #1 — 때문 + 이다 = It is because:**

**Let’s look at how to say “It’s because \_\_\_” using 때문이다.**

**There are two main patterns:**

**🔹1. Noun + 때문이다
This means: “It is because of [noun].”**

**Example:
요즘 모든 게 너무 비싸요. 이게 다 정치인들 때문이에요.
→ These days, everything is too expensive. It’s all because of the politicians.**

**사실 그건 은행 때문이에요.
→ Actually, that’s because of the banks.**

**So when you want to clearly blame or explain the reason behind something using a noun, this is the form to use.**

**🔹2. Verb Stem + 기 때문이다
This means: “It is because [someone] did \_\_\_.”**

**To do this, you first add 기 after the verb stem to turn the verb into a noun using ~기.
This is called nominalization. You’ve seen it in phrases like ~기 싫다 (“I don’t want to \_\_\_”).**

**Examples:
학교에 갈 수가 없었어요.
→ I couldn’t go to school.
늦게 일어났기 때문이에요.
→ It’s because I woke up late.**

**Or:
가족과 함께 부산에 갈 거기 때문이에요.
→ It’s because I’ll be going to Busan with my family.**

**🟡Bonus Tip:
You *can* use 기 때문에 with past and future tenses.
But you can’t say 기 덕분 ❌
Remember: 덕분 only works with nouns, never with verbs.**

**Let’s now look at the next common use of 때문에.**

**Slide 24: 때문 Usage #2 때문에 = Because**

**Let’s look at 때문에 — this time, you’re following it with a sentence.**

**🔹 1. Noun + 때문에 + [sentence]
This means: “Because of [noun]…”**

**Examples:
비 때문에 수업이 취소됐어요.
→ Because of the rain, the class was canceled.**

**부모님 덕분에 대학교에 갈 수 있었어요.
→ Thanks to my parents, I was able to go to college.**

**⚠️ Quick nuance tip:
• 때문에 can sound slightly negative.
• 덕분에 is positive — it’s used to express thanks or a good result.**

**🔹 2. Verb Stem + 기 때문에 + [sentence]
This means “because [someone] did something” or “because [something] happened.”**

**Examples:
• 친구가 늦게 올 거기 때문에 영화를 먼저 봤어요.
→ Because my friend will come late, we watched the movie first.**

**• 어제 밖에 오래 있어서 감기에 걸렸기 때문에 학교에 가지 못 했어요.
→ Because I was outside for a long time, I caught a cold, so I couldn’t go to school.**

**• 남편이 요리사이기 때문에 요리를 잘해서 사람들이 항상 부탁해요.
→ Because my husband is a chef, he’s good at cooking, so people always ask him (for his food).**

**🟡 Did you notice how I used both ~서 and 기 때문에 in the same sentence?
This actually happens all the time in Korean.**

**Sometimes, the first reason leads to the second reason, and that second reason becomes the cause for the final action.
In those cases, it sounds much more natural and smoother to use ~서 for one of the two reasons instead of repeating 기 때문에 twice.**

**This kind of combination makes your sentence less repetitive.**

**Slide 27 – Summary**

**Now let’s quickly go over everything we’ve learned using this summary table.
You’ll see each form, what it’s used for, what kinds of sentence types it works with, and a quick example to jog your memory.**

**🔹 ~서
We started with the ~서 form. I explained it as a big arrow — showing a natural flow or continuity.
It’s one of the most flexible forms with three main uses: cause & effect, sequential actions, and means/method.**

**Here are 4 key things to remember:**

1. **You can’t use the past stem like 했어서 or 갔어서 — it’s grammatically incorrect.**
2. **This is the only form that can be used to express the reason for your emotions.
   → *도와주셔서 감사합니다.* (Thank you for helping me.)**
3. **When used for cause and effect, don’t use it in commands or suggestions.**
4. **With 이다 / 아니다, it changes to *이라서* or *아니라서*, and in some cases, *서* can be dropped.**

**🔹 We also looked at 에게/한테/께 있어서, a special case of ~서.
It’s used to express the value or importance of something to someone.
→ *나에게 있어서 가족이 가장 중요해.***

**🔹 ~가지고
This one is very similar to ~서 but only used in spoken Korean.
It can show cause and effect or sequential actions, but not emotions.
If you're using it for means/method, it only works with nouns, like:
→ *음식 갖고 장난치지 마.* (Don’t play with your food.)**

**🔹 ~니까
This form emphasizes reason and can be used in commands or suggestions.**

**But—⚠️—you can’t use it to express emotions.**

**Example: *피곤하니까 일찍 자.***

**It’s slightly informal, but very common in everyday conversation.**

**🔹 ~때문이다 / ~기 때문에
This one is the most formal. It often shows up in writing, news, and academic settings.**

**You can use it with both nouns and verbs, but not with suggestions or emotions.**

**It’s also more direct—so sometimes it has a slightly negative or a blaming tone.**

**If you want to express a positive reason, use *덕분에* instead — especially when thanking someone.**

* **Example: *눈 때문에 미팅이 취소됐습니다.***
