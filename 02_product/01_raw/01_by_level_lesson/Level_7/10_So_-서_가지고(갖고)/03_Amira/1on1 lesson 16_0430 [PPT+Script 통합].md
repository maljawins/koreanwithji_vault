## Lesson Overview
This lesson focuses on the various ways to express "because" or provide reasons in Korean. While English often uses the word "because" as a catch-all, Korean utilizes several different forms—~서, ~가지고, ~(으)니까, and ~때문에—each carrying its own specific nuance, level of formality, and grammatical restrictions. The primary objective is to help the student move beyond word-for-word translation and understand the "feel" and natural flow of these connectors in real-world conversation.

The lesson begins by establishing the fundamental logic of Korean sentence structure: the cause always precedes the effect. From there, it dives deep into the most common connector, ~서, explaining it as a concept of "continuity" rather than a simple translation of "so." The lesson then compares this to the more reason-focused ~(으)니까 and the formal, sometimes blunt, ~때문에. By the end of the session, the student will understand how to choose the correct form based on whether they are giving a command, expressing an emotion, or speaking in a formal setting.

### Slide 3 — Cause Comes First in Korean
- **On screen:** A large green arrow pointing from left to right labeled "CAUSE → EFFECT." Text explains that Korean is the opposite of English. A yellow highlighted box introduces 왜냐하면 (If you ask me why) for explaining the cause later. Example sentence: 저희 공부 진짜 열심히 했어요. 왜냐하면 다음 주에 시험이 있거든요. (We studied really hard. Because we have an exam next week). Note on 거든(요) as a context marker.
- **Ji's teaching:** So, here’s one important thing to understand when it comes to expressing reasons in Korean: In Korean, the cause always comes first—before the effect. And I don’t even think I have to explain why that is, because this is just a universal fact. But in English, it’s the opposite. We usually say the effect first, and then give the reason. Like: “I was late because I missed the bus.” In Korean, it’s flipped: “Because I missed the bus, I was late.” So keep this in mind: “Because A, then B.” Cause, then Effect. This rule applies to every form we’re going to learn today. With each of them, the cause always comes before the effect. But—sometimes—you want to explain the reason afterward, especially if it flows better that way in the conversation. In that case, you can start your next sentence with 왜냐하면, which literally means, “If you ask me why…” Here’s an example: 저희 공부 진짜 열심히 했어요. 왜냐하면 다음 주에 시험이 있거든요. “We studied really hard. Because—well, if you ask me why—it’s because we have a test next week.” And 거든요 at the end is a context marker. It’s like saying, “Hey, here’s the background or the reason for what I just said.” It’s kind of like saying, “We have a test next week, you know.” Alright, with that in mind, let’s move into the first and most common form: 아/어 + 서.

### Slide 4 — What is 서?
- **On screen:** Large text stating "~서 Is NOT 'Because'." A diagram shows "A [서] B" with a green arrow representing "Continuity, Flow, Connection." A comparison box for location markers: 집에 있어요 (State/no continuity) vs. 집에서 운동해요 (Action continues at home). List of 3 core functions: 1. Cause & Effect, 2. Sequential Actions, 3. Means & Method. Note on 그래서 (So) being 그렇다 + 서.
- **Ji's teaching:** This is one of the most essential connectors in Korean, and it’s one that a lot of learners misunderstand at first. So I really want you to get this: ~서 is NOT the same as "because" or “so.” Now, let’s start with this word: 그래서, which DOES translate to “so.” But it actually comes from 그렇다 + 서. That’s just ONE of the usages of 서. A lot of learners just learn 그래서 first, then memorize ~서 as “because” or “so.” It works like that sometimes, but that’s not what 서 actually means. 서 is actually an abstract concept and there’s no direct translation in English. Instead, I want you to think of it THIS way: It shows a kind of flow or continuity between two events, states, or actions. There’s some kind of connection between A and B—that’s what 서 expresses. Here’s another way to help you get this: Think about the particles 에 and 에서. If you say: 집에 있어요 – “I’m at home,” that’s just a state—nothing is happening. Just stillness. But if you say: 집에서 운동해요 – “I work out at home,” now there’s action, flow, movement. And that 서 in 에서 is doing exactly that—it shows that something is happening in that space. And this is what the form ~서 does, too. It connects one thing to another. And now with that in mind, ~서 as a sentence connector can be used in three main uses: 1. Cause and Effect, 2. Sequential Actions, and 3. Means or Method. We’ll go through each of these one by one. There’s also a bonus usage that we’ll touch on later. So again, don’t just memorize ~서 as “because” or “so.” The key word I want you to remember is continuity. That’s the heart of ~서.

### Slide 5 — How to make the ~서 form
- **On screen:** Formula: [Stem] + 아 / 어 / 여 + 서. Examples: 봐서, 먹어서, 해서. A tree diagram shows the "Stem" as the trunk, "아/어/여" as the leaf, and "서" as the connector. Tense usage rules: Present (해서) and Future (할 거라서) are marked with green checkmarks; Past (했어서) is marked with a red "X". A note explains that 배고프다 is often used as 배가 고프다 where 가 marks the stomach as the subject.
- **Ji's teaching:** The way you make this form is simple: Verb stem + 아 / 어 + 서. So for example: 하다 becomes 해서, 보다 becomes 봐서, and 먹다 becomes 먹어서. So, 운동을 해서 배가 고팠어요 (I worked out, so I was hungry). Quick side note—what’s this 가 doing in 배가 고프다? 배 means “stomach,” and 고프다 is the descriptive verb for “to be hungry.” So 가 is just marking 배 as the subject—literally, “The stomach is hungry.” Now, let’s talk about the tense it can take. It can be in the present tense like 해서, 먹어서, and 봐서. It can also be in the future tense; we use ㄹ/을 거라서. For example: 할 거라서. Now, you might be wondering—what is that 라 in 거라서? It only shows up with 이다 or 아니다, and it comes from the full form ㄹ/을 것이다. I’ll explain this a bit more on the next slide. But you cannot use the past tense directly with ~서. So don’t say: 했어서. Though you might hear it sometimes—since around 2010, some Koreans have started saying things like 했어서 to put emphasis on the past. But according to standard grammar rules, this is still incorrect. So to keep things clean and natural, stick with the present or future forms when using ~서.

### Slide 6 — 이다 / 아니다 + 서
- **On screen:** Rules for "To be" and "To not be."
    - 이라(서) vs 라(서): After 받침 use 이라(서) (e.g., 봄이라서); After vowel use 라(서) (e.g., 갈 거라서).
    - 이어서 vs 여서: After 받침 use 이어서 (e.g., 펜이어서); After vowel use 여서 (e.g., 영화여서).
    - Note: (이)라서 is much more common. 서 can be dropped after 라.
- **Ji's teaching:** Now let’s talk about how to use ~서 with 이다 and 아니다. There are two ways to do it: 이라서/아니라서 or 이어서/아니어서. Both versions are grammatically correct. But in daily conversation, you’ll hear (이)라서 a lot more often. So when do you use 이라서 and when do you use 라서? It depends on whether the word ends in a 받침—a final consonant. If the word ends in a consonant, you use 이라서. For example: 지금 봄이라서 날씨가 건조해요 (It’s spring now, so the weather is dry). If the word ends in a vowel, you drop the 이 and just use 라서. For example: 내일 파티에 갈 거라서 기대돼요 (I’m going to the party tomorrow, so I’m excited). This is the same as 갈 것이라서. Just a quick note—기대 means “expectation,” or looking forward to something. Now, here’s something important: With 라서, the 서 part can be dropped in casual or shortened speech. But you can’t drop 서 with 이어서 or 여서—it always needs to be there. So what about 이어서 and 여서? Same logic: If the word ends in a consonant, use 이어서 (e.g., 이건 펜이어서 지우개로 못 지워요). If the word ends in a vowel, use 여서 (e.g., 지루한 영화여서 잠들었어요). So to wrap it up: Both versions are fine, but (이)라서 is more commonly used in speech, and only 라서 can drop the 서.

### Slide 7 — ~서 Usage #1: Cause & Effect
- **On screen:** Explains ~서 as a "natural flow or consequence." Examples: 걸어가서 (walked, so...), 매워서 (spicy, so...). A large "IMPORTANT!" box states it cannot be used for suggestions or commands (~자, ~세요). Instead, use ~니까. A "Why NOT?" box explains that suggestions are conscious decisions, not automatic results.
- **Ji's teaching:** This is the most standard and common use of ~서—where A causes B. For example: 집까지 걸어가서 다리가 아팠어요 (I walked home, so my legs hurt). Or: 음식이 너무 매워서 못 먹겠어요 (The food is too spicy, so I don’t think I can eat it). But there’s one important restriction here: You can’t use ~서 with suggestions or commands—like “Let’s do it because…” or “Do this because…” For that, you need to use a different form: ~니까. So why not ~서? Because a cause can’t “cause” a suggestion or command. Suggestions and commands are a conscious decision—not a natural result. They come from someone’s intent, not automatic flow. So it sounds awkward in these examples. Let’s compare: You shouldn't say 더워서 바다에 가자; instead, say 더우니까 바다에 가자 (Because it’s hot, let’s go to the beach). Similarly, don't say 비가 와서 이 우산을 가져가세요; say 비가 오니까 이 우산을 가져가세요 (Because it’s raining, take this umbrella).

### Slide 9 — ~서 Usage #2: Sequential Actions
- **On screen:** Explains connecting two closely related actions where the first leads naturally to the second. Examples: 걸어가서 버스를 탔어요 (Walked to the stop and then took the bus); 가서 우유를 샀어요 (Went to the store and then bought milk). A comparison box shows ~고 vs ~서: ~고 just lists actions, while ~서 implies they are directly connected. Example: 친구를 만나서 카페에 갔어요 (Met a friend and went to the cafe together).
- **Ji's teaching:** Now let’s move on to the second major use of ~서. It’s used to connect two actions that happen one after another, where the first action naturally leads into the second. Let’s look at some examples: 버스 정류장까지 걸어가서 버스를 탔어요 (I walked to the bus stop and then took the bus). "At the bus stop" is implied here. Or: 가게에 가서 우유를 샀어요 (I went to the store and then bought milk). "At the store" is implied. In both cases, the first action flows directly into the next like a chain reaction. Now let’s think about this in English: “Go do it!”—You’re not just saying “go” and “do it” as separate things. You mean: Go somewhere, and once you’re there, do it. Same in Korean with: 가서 해! It means: “Go to a place, and then do it there.” So again, the two actions are linked. This brings us to the key difference between ~고 and ~서. If you say 친구를 만나고 카페에 갔어요, it sounds like you met your friend, that ended, and then you went to the café—maybe by yourself. But with ~서, as in 친구를 만나서 카페에 갔어요, it means you met your friend, and together, you went to the café. There’s a natural sequence and flow. So just remember: ~고 just lists A and B, while ~서 shows flow and sequence.

### Slide 11 — ~서 Usage #3: Means & Method
- **On screen:** Explains ~서 as showing the method or tool (By doing A, you achieve B). Examples: 찍어서 먹었어요 (Dipped and ate); 사용해서 주문했어요 (Ordered using the app); 걸어서 학교에 가요 (Go to school by walking). A note highlights the difference between 걷다 (to walk, irregular ㄷ -> 걸어서) and 걸다 (to hang, regular -> 걸어서).
- **Ji's teaching:** Alright, this is the third major usage of ~서—the first action shows how the second action happened. We’re talking about method, tool, or means. So, basically: “By doing A, you achieve B.” Let’s look at a few examples: 고기를 소스에 찍어서 먹었어요 (I dipped the meat in sauce and ate it). The method of eating was dipping it in sauce. 앱을 사용해서 주문했어요 (I ordered it using the app). The app was the tool or method I used to order. 나는 걸어서 학교에 가요 (I go to school by walking). Walking is the method I use to get to school. 벽에 그림을 걸어서 집을 꾸몄어요 (I decorated the house by hanging a painting on the wall). Hanging the painting was the way I decorated. Quick sidenote: notice the different verb stems for these two verbs: 걷다 (to walk) becomes 걸어서 (irregular ㄷ), while 걸다 (to hang) also becomes 걸어서 (regular). Totally different verbs, but they just happen to look similar once you add ~서.

### Slide 13 — 3 Usages of ~서 Summary
- **On screen:** A summary of the three uses with a "long arrow" visualization.
    1. Cause → Effect (Natural consequence).
    2. Sequential Actions (Connected order).
    3. Means / Method (How it's achieved).
- **Ji's teaching:** Let’s wrap up with a quick summary of the three major uses of ~서. First, Cause → Effect: One event naturally causes the next to happen. For example: 저는 한국 사람이라서 한국말을 잘 해요 (I’m Korean, so I speak Korean well). Second, Sequential Actions: Two connected actions happening one after another. For example: 친구를 만나서 점심을 먹었어요 (I met a friend and then had lunch). You ate together—it’s part of the same flow. Third, Means / Method: Describes how something was done. For example: 앱을 사용해서 음식을 주문했어요 (I ordered food by using the app). So the key idea to remember is continuity: you can visualize ~서 as a long arrow. Each use shows a continuous flow—one thing leads right into the next.

### Slide 14 — 있어서: Special Usage
- **On screen:**
    1. Normal usage: 새는 날개가 있어서 날 수 있어요 (Birds have wings, so they can fly).
    2. Special Usage: Person 에게 / 한테 / 께 있어서. Meaning: "In someone's perspective" or "As far as concerned." Example: 내게 있어서 너는 소중한 친구야 (To me, you are a precious friend). Note: 서 can be dropped here.
- **Ji's teaching:** Now, here’s the special usage of the 서 form I talked about earlier, and it’s 있어서. First—the normal usage: 있다 means to exist or to have, so naturally: 새는 날개가 있어서 날 수 있어요 (Birds have wings, so they can fly) or 돈이 있어서 여행을 갔어요 (I had money, so I went on a trip). These are typical cause-and-effect sentences. Having something (A) allows you to do something (B). But there’s a special usage with 에게 / 한테 / 께 있어서. This changes the meaning completely. It’s not about existence anymore—it’s about perspective. Think: "To me," "In their eyes," or "As far as [someone] is concerned." And in this case, 서 is often dropped in speech. Let’s look at an example: 내게 있어(서) 너는 소중한 친구야 (To me, you’re a precious friend). 내게 is short for 나에게. Another example: 그 사람한테 있어(서) SNS가 전부예요 (To her, social media is everything). SNS is Konglish for Social Media. So, in these cases, 있어서 doesn’t mean “It exists, so ___.” Instead, it shows value or importance from someone’s personal perspective. Think of it like this: A에게 있어서 B means "To A, B holds meaning or value."

### Slide 15 — 아/어 가지고 (갖고): Similar to ~서
- **On screen:** Formula: Verb stem + 아/어 + 가지고. Examples: 먹어가지고, 사가지고. Explains that 가지고 comes from 가지다 (to have/hold) + 고 (and). It can replace ~서 in Cause & Effect and Sequential Actions.
- **Ji's teaching:** Now let’s take a look at another connector: 아/어 가지고—often shortened to 갖고 when speaking. This form is super common in everyday Korean, and it’s really similar to ~서. Even the way you make it is similar to 서. You just take the verb stem and attach 아/어 + 가지고. For example: 먹다 becomes 먹어가지고, and 사다 becomes 사가지고. This comes from the verb 가지다, which means “to have” or “to hold.” So literally, 가지고 means “have and then…” What does it express? Just like ~서, it shows one action leads into the next. It's used to mean “so,” “and then,” or sometimes “with.” It’s almost the same idea as 서. You’ll see it used in two main ways: Cause & Effect (e.g., 길이 막혀가지고 늦었어요 - The road was jammed, so I was late) and Sequential Actions (e.g., 돈을 찾아가지고 밥을 먹었어요 - I withdrew money and then ate). Again, this is just like ~서—just more casual or spoken in tone. BUT, 가지고 sounds awkward when used with greetings or emotions. You wouldn’t say 만나가지고 반갑습니다; instead, use 만나서 반갑습니다. And for “means or method,” 가지고 is only used with nouns, like 카드 갖고 계산했어요 (I paid with the card). So overall: 가지고 equals ~서 but is more casual; 갖고 is the shortened version; use it for cause-effect or sequences, not emotions or greetings.

### Slide 16 — Verb Stem + (으)니까
- **On screen:** Explains that ~니까 emphasizes the reason more strongly than ~서 and is closer to "because" in English. Note on 그러니까 (That's why). Mentions it is slightly informal. A "Don't overthink it!" box explains that the difference between 서 and 니까 is debated even by linguists.
- **Ji's teaching:** Now let’s move on to ~(으)니까. So what is 니까? 니까 does mean “because.” So naturally, it puts more emphasis on the reason than ~서. And if you look at this word, 그러니까, which is super common, it comes from 그렇다 (to be so) + 니까. It can be translated as “That’s why” or “therefore” in some cases. Other points to keep in mind: ~니까 is slightly informal, but not rude, so it's totally fine in most spoken situations. If you want to sound more formal you can drop 까 (하니까 becomes 하니) or use 때문에, which we will learn next. So, the key difference between 서 and 니까: ~서 shows a natural connection or flow between actions (Think: A causes B naturally), while ~니까 is all about the reason. So you can use this form even when the two events are not connected. You’re specifically pointing out the why. They can both be “translated” as “because,” but ~니까 feels closer to it than 서. And I encourage you not to stress too much about the difference because even professional linguists don’t fully agree with each other on this topic. I actually tried researching this myself—in both Korean and English—and honestly, every explanation seemed to contradict one another. None of them made sense. They just didn’t match my intuition as a native speaker. So, after spending hours digging through articles, posts, and papers, I came to the conclusion that this topic is still heavily debated. That’s why I encourage you to focus on how each one feels through examples and practice. And don’t worry about mixing them up because Koreans will understand you.

### Slide 17 — Key features of ~니까
- **On screen:**
    - CAN be used with suggestions & commands (e.g., 배고프니까 빨리 먹자).
    - CAN be used with past tenses (e.g., 일하고 있었으니까요).
    - CAN'T be used to express emotions (e.g., 만나니까 반갑습니다 is wrong; use 만나서 반갑습니다).
- **Ji's teaching:** Alright—now let’s look at some important things to know about ~니까. First—You CAN use ~니까 with suggestions and commands. This is a big difference from ~서. Remember? You couldn’t say “Let’s do it because…” with ~서. But with ~니까, it works. Let’s see: 배고프니까 빨리 먹자 (Because we’re hungry, let’s hurry up and eat). Totally natural! Another one: 지금 갈 거니까 조금만 기다려 (Because I will go now, wait just a little). This means "I’m on my way, so hang in there." So whenever you want to give a suggestion or command because of a reason, ~니까 is the one to use. Also, ~니까 can be used in past tense unlike the 서 form. Let’s look at a dialogue: A asks 어제 전화했는데 왜 안 받았어? (I called you yesterday—why didn’t you pick up?) and B answers 일하고 있었으니까요 (Because I was working). If you were to use 있었어서 it would be wrong, but 있었으니까 is perfectly fine. But ~니까 CAN’T be used for emotions. This is a key restriction. You can’t say 만나니까 반갑습니다 (Because I meet you, I’m glad). That just sounds weird—even in English. Instead, use 만나서 반갑습니다 (Nice to meet you). This feels natural—just a simple connection. So for emotions like thank you, glad, sorry, etc., stick with ~서.

### Slide 18 — How to make the ~니까 form
- **On screen:** Formula: Stem + (으)니까.
    - After 받침: 으니까 (e.g., 먹으니까).
    - After Vowel: 니까 (e.g., 가니까).
    - Irregulars: ㄹ-ending (Drop ㄹ, add 니까: 살다 -> 사니까); ㅂ-ending (Change ㅂ to 우, add 니까: 쉽다 -> 쉬우니까).
    - Past/Future: 했으니까, 할 거니까.
- **Ji's teaching:** So how do you make this form? It’s simple. Step 1: Just attach ~(으)니까 or 니까 to the verb stem. If the verb ends in a consonant (받침), add 으니까 like 먹으니까 (Because I eat / Because I ate—context decides). If the verb ends in a vowel, use 니까 like 가니까 (Because I go / Because I went). For irregular verbs, there are just a couple to remember. First, ㄹ-ending verbs: Drop the ㄹ, then add ~니까 (살다 becomes 사니까). Second, ㅂ-ending descriptive verbs: ㅂ changes to 우, then add ~니까 (쉽다 becomes 쉬우니까). What about the verb 이다 (to be)? Same rule applies, but slightly different: If it follows a consonant, use 이니까 (물이니까 - Because it’s water); if it follows a vowel, use 니까 (친구니까 - Because he/she is a friend). And you can also apply the same rule with the past tense and the future tense like this: for the past tense, 하다 becomes 했으니까 (Because I did); for the future tense, 하다 becomes 할 거니까 (Because I will do it). So you can use ~니까 in the present, past, or even future—very flexible!

### Slide 22 — What is 때문?
- **On screen:** Explains 때문 is a noun meaning "the cause" or "the reason." It is the most formal form. Note on 탓 (blame/negative) vs. 덕분 (thanks to/positive). Restriction: Cannot be used with suggestions or commands.
- **Ji's teaching:** Let’s talk about 때문. 때문 is actually a noun—it literally means “the cause” or “the reason.” So when you say something 때문에, you’re literally saying “because of ___.” This is the most formal way to say “because.” You’ll often hear it in news, writing, or serious conversations. But—because it directly points to the reason, it can sometimes sound a little blunt or even accusatory. So, it has a slightly negative connotation. If you want to talk about a positive reason, you can use 덕분 instead. For example: 시험에 합격했어요. 다 선생님 덕분이에요 (I passed the exam. It’s all thanks to you, teacher). But note that 덕분 can only be used with nouns, not verbs. So just keep that in mind. And also, you can’t use 때문에 with commands or suggestions. It just sounds awkward. So this one: 감기 때문에 병원에 가세요 sounds off. Instead, say 감기에 걸렸으니까 병원에 가세요.

### Slide 23 — 때문 Usage #1: 때문 + 이다 = It is because
- **On screen:**
    1. Noun + 때문이다 (e.g., 정치인들 때문이에요 - It's because of the politicians).
    2. Verb stem + 기 때문이다 (Nominalization). Example: 늦게 일어났기 때문이에요 (It's because I woke up late).
    - Note: 기 때문 can be used with past/future. ~기 덕분 is wrong.
- **Ji's teaching:** Let’s look at how to say “It’s because ___” using 때문이다. There are two main patterns. First, Noun + 때문이다. This means: “It is because of [noun].” Example: 요즘 모든 게 너무 비싸요. 이게 다 정치인들 때문이에요 (These days, everything is too expensive. It’s all because of the politicians). Or: 사실 그건 은행 때문이에요 (Actually, that’s because of the banks). So when you want to clearly blame or explain the reason behind something using a noun, this is the form to use. Second, Verb Stem + 기 때문이다. This means: “It is because [someone] did ___.” To do this, you first add 기 after the verb stem to turn the verb into a noun using ~기. This is called nominalization. You’ve seen it in phrases like ~기 싫다 (“I don’t want to ___”). Examples: 학교에 갈 수가 없었어요. 늦게 일어났기 때문이에요 (I couldn’t go to school. It’s because I woke up late). Or: 가족과 함께 부산에 갈 거기 때문이에요 (It’s because I’ll be going to Busan with my family). Bonus Tip: You can use 기 때문에 with past and future tenses. But you can’t say 기 덕분. Remember: 덕분 only works with nouns, never with verbs.

### Slide 25 — 때문 Usage #2: 때문에 = Because
- **On screen:** (Note: script marker said Slide 24).
    1. Noun + 때문에 + [sentence] (e.g., 비 때문에 수업이 취소됐어요).
    2. Verb stem + 기 때문에 + [sentence] (e.g., 친구가 늦게 올 거기 때문에...).
    - Note: When stating more than 2 reasons, use ~서 for one of them to sound more natural and avoid repetition.
- **Ji's teaching:** Let’s look at 때문에—this time, you’re following it with a sentence. First, Noun + 때문에 + [sentence]. This means: “Because of [noun]…” Examples: 비 때문에 수업이 취소됐어요 (Because of the rain, the class was canceled). Or: 부모님 덕분에 대학교에 갈 수 있었어요 (Thanks to my parents, I was able to go to college). Quick nuance tip: 때문에 can sound slightly negative, while 덕분에 is positive—it’s used to express thanks or a good result. Second, Verb Stem + 기 때문에 + [sentence]. This means “because [someone] did something” or “because [something] happened.” Examples: 친구가 늦게 올 거기 때문에 영화를 먼저 봤어요 (Because my friend will come late, we watched the movie first). Or: 어제 밖에 오래 있어서 감기에 걸렸기 때문에 학교에 가지 못 했어요 (Because I was outside for a long time, I caught a cold, so I couldn’t go to school). Or: 남편이 요리사이기 때문에 요리를 잘해서 사람들이 항상 부탁해요 (Because my husband is a chef, he’s good at cooking, so people always ask him for his food). Did you notice how I used both ~서 and 기 때문에 in the same sentence? This actually happens all the time in Korean. Sometimes, the first reason leads to the second reason, and that second reason becomes the cause for the final action. In those cases, it sounds much more natural and smoother to use ~서 for one of the two reasons instead of repeating 기 때문에 twice. This kind of combination makes your sentence less repetitive.

## Teaching Points

- **Sentence Logic:** In Korean, the cause always comes before the effect ("Because A, B"). To explain a reason afterward, use 왜냐하면 (If you ask me why) at the start of the next sentence.
- **~서 (Continuity/Flow):**
    - Represents a natural flow or "long arrow" between two ideas.
    - **Three Uses:** Cause & Effect, Sequential Actions (closely related), and Means/Method.
    - **Restrictions:** Cannot be used with past tense stems (❌ 했어서) or in commands/suggestions.
    - **Special Note:** This is the correct form for expressing reasons for emotions (e.g., "Thank you for...").
    - **Special Usage:** 에게 있어서 means "In [someone's] perspective" or "To [someone]."
- **~가지고 / 갖고:**
    - A casual, spoken-only version of ~서.
    - Follows the same logic as ~서 but is more informal.
    - For "Means/Method," it is only used with nouns.
- **~(으)니까 (Reason Focus):**
    - Emphasizes the reason itself rather than the flow.
    - **Flexibility:** Can be used with past tense (✅ 했으니까) and in commands/suggestions.
    - **Restriction:** Cannot be used to express emotions.
- **~때문에 / 기 때문에 (Formal/Blunt):**
    - The most formal way to express "because."
    - **Nominalization:** Verbs must be turned into nouns using ~기 before adding 때문에.
    - **Nuance:** Can sound blunt or accusatory. Use 덕분에 (thanks to) for positive reasons.
    - **Restriction:** Cannot be used with commands, suggestions, or emotions.
- **Combining Forms:** In complex sentences with multiple reasons, it is natural to mix forms (e.g., using ~서 for the first reason and ~기 때문에 for the second) to avoid repetitive phrasing.
