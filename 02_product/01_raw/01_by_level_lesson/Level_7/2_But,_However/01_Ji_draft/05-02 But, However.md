**Intro**

이번 레슨에서는 한국어로 “But”을 얘기해보겠다. There are many ways to express contrast in Korean, one of them being the use of the topic marker.

And today, we’re going to learn the 2 most basic ones.

**~지만**

First is 지만. Now, it is often translated as “but”. But you see, ‘but’ in English is very versatile word. I mean, there are times when ‘but’ and ‘and’ are completely interchangeable. Let’s look at this sentence.

▶ *I’ve been working for 12 hours, but/and I’m still at the office.*

Either ‘but’ or ‘and’ would completely make sense.

However, this version of ‘but’ doesn’t really work with 지만.

So, it’s actually better to think of 지만 as 'however’ or ‘although’ where you clearly highlight the contrast between the two sentences.

Now, here’s a word you’re probably familiar with - 하지만. But you can only use 하지만 at the beginning of a new sentence. It’s not a sentence connector.

I also want to bring up “그러나”. 그러나 is definitely very rare in spoken Korean and you can think of it as the formal, written version of 하지만. It’s sort of like the equivalent of “Nevertheless”.

We also have 그렇지만 or 그치만 (shortened), can only be used in the beginning of a new sentence as well. This is a combination of 그렇다 to be so (like that) and 지만. So, it has a similar nuance to “Even so.”

So, the way you use 지만 as the sentence connector all you need to do just take the **verb stem** and add **~지만**. There’s **no need to worry about 받침.**

"Let’s go over some examples together.

1. **저는 피곤하지만 갈 수 있어요.**
   → *I’m tired. However, I can go.*
2. 그 사람은 친절하지만 좀 말이 많아요.
   → *That person is kind, but talks a lot.*
3. 오늘은 바쁘지만 내일은 한가해요.
   → *I’m busy today, but I’m free tomorrow.*
   This sentence shows a contrast in time or schedule—busy today, free tomorrow."

All of these sentences clearly contrast two ideas:

**ㄴ/은데 & 는데**

Now, we have ~데.

You probably heard this word ‘근데’ countless times. But it’s actually a shortened version of 그런데. You’ll hear this version a lot more in casual conversation.

Unlike ~지만, which directly shows a clear contrast, ~데 feels softer, more subtle, and can sometimes even feel like “and” or “yet,” depending on the context. Earlier in the lesson, I mentioned how ‘but’ is a very versatile word. It’s similar to how you can say but/and/yet in English.

Let’s use the same example sentence from before.

In this example:
▶ *I’ve been working for 12 hours, but/and/yet I’m still at the office.*
Now this is what ~데 feels like.

Think of ~데 as showing a background, setup, or a mild contrast between two ideas.

Conjugating ~데 is a bit trickier than the ~지만 form.
Here’s what you need to do:

First, you need to identify what type of verb you’re dealing with.

If it’s an action verb, then it’s pretty simple—regardless of whether the verb stem ends in a consonant or not, you just add ~는데.

For example, 가다 becomes 가는데, and 믿다 becomes 믿는데.

But when you’re working with descriptive verbs, you attach ㄴ/은데 and it’s 받침-sensitive.

After a 받침, you add ~은데. For example, 작다 has a 받침, so it becomes 작은데.

After a vowel, you add ~ㄴ데. So 예쁘다 becomes 예쁜데.

But there are a few exceptions with this rule.

First, with action verbs that end in ㄹ, namely ㄹ irregulars, you remove the ㄹ and then add ~는데. For example, 살다 turns into 사는데.

And for descriptive verbs that end in ㅂ. In this case, you replace the ㅂ with 우, and then add ~운데. So 쉽다 becomes 쉬운데.

Lastly, we have two verbs that don’t follow the usual descriptive verb pattern—있다 and 없다. These are always conjugated as 있는데 and 없는데, no matter what. Just memorize these as fixed forms.

So again—action verbs are usually simple, descriptive verbs depend on 받침, and then we’ve got some irregulars and a couple of exceptions to keep in mind.

Once you get used to spotting the verb type, this form becomes a lot easier to use naturally.

**~데 examples (Connector)**

Now, we have more example sentences here. We’re going to look at a couple of them here

First, 이 식당은 작은데 음식이 정말 맛있어요.
→ The restaurant is small, but the food is really good.
Here, we’re showing contrast. You’d expect a small place to have average food, but the food is surprisingly good. So 작은데 is giving us that unexpected twist.

친구를 만났는데 같이 밥을 먹었어요.
→ *I met a friend, and we ate together.*
Here, ~는데 is just connecting two actions. It’s not contrast—it’s more like a natural flow from one event to the next. I met a friend → and then we ate.

Last, 방이 넓은데 조용해요.
→ *The room is spacious and quiet.*
This is another example where ~데 works almost like “and.” There’s no contrast here. You’re simply linking two positive qualities about the room.

**~데(요) Ending (Context marker)**

Just like ~고요, we can end a sentence with ~데요, but the nuance is a little different. ~데요 is often used to imply contrast, surprise, hesitation, or curiosity—without saying it directly.

You can think of it like the English word “though” at the end of a sentence. It’s not as direct as saying “but,” but it still gives a hint of contrast or a shift in thought.

This ending has 4 main purposes.

1. To politely disagree or suggest a different perspective
Imagine someone says:
오늘 공원에서 만나시죠. → *Let’s meet at the park today.*
And you respond:
비가 오는데요? → *It’s raining, though.*
You’re not directly saying no, but you’re gently pointing out the weather, implying we should probably meet somewhere else.

2. Let’s say someone tells you:
그 카레 아빠가 만들었어. → *Dad made the curry.*
And you say: 맛있는데요?
→ *It’s good, though!*
You’re surprised—in a good way. It’s better than you thought. That ~데요 shows your reaction without needing to explain further.

3. To show hesitation or trail off
Someone asks:
토요일에 시간 있으세요? → *Do you have time this Saturday?*
And you say: 토요일에는 바쁜데… → *I’m busy Saturday, but…*
You’re not rejecting them outright—you’re leaving it open. It’s not like you don’t want to go but it’s just that you’re busy, or maybe you’re still thinking about it.

4. To ask with curiosity
Let’s say your boyfriend hangs up a phone call, and you want to know who it was.

You can ask: 누군데? → *Who was it, though?*
That ~데 adds a light, casual feeling of curiosity. You’re not demanding an answer—you’re just asking for more info in a natural way.

~데요 can be used to politely disagree, show surprise, express hesitation, or ask with curiosity.

It’s all about tone, context, and delivery. You’ll hear it a lot, and once you get used to it, you’ll find it super useful in conversations.

**~데(요) examples (Ending)**

**A:** 이 가방 어때요?
→ *How is this bag?* *(Do you like the bag?)*
**B:** 예쁜데, 너무 비싼데요.
→ *It’s pretty, but it’s a little expensive, though.*
You’re giving your honest opinion, but softening it. It’s not a hard “no, I don’t want it.” But you’re just pointing out.

**A:** 이 쿠키 엄마가 만들었어.
→ *Mom made this cookie.*
**B:** 이거 맛있는데요!
→ *It’s delicious!*
You’re surprised in a good way. Maybe you didn’t expect it to taste that good —and ~데요 captures that reaction.

**A:** 통화 가능하세요? → *Is this a good time to talk?*
**B:** 지금 바쁜데… → *I’m busy, but…*
You’re not flat-out rejecting the call. You’re giving a soft, hesitant answer. That trailing tone leaves space for follow-up.

**A:** 저 사람 누구예요? 왜 따라와요? → *Who is that person? Why is he following us?*
**B:** 제 친구인데요. → *He’s my friend, though.*
You’re gently correcting the assumption—without sounding defensive.

**A:** 회원이신가요? → *Are you a member?*
**B:** 오늘 처음 왔는데요. → *I came today for the first time, though.* *(I’m new, though.)*
Here, you’re not just saying “no.” You’re giving context and being polite about it.

**~지만 VS ~데**

Alright, let’s compare **~지만** and **~(으)ㄴ/는데.**

~지만 comes from 하지만 and has a similar nuance to “however,” so it has a stronger, clearer sense of contrast.
~데, on the other hand, comes from 그런데, which is softer. The contrast it shows feels more indirect or implied. So, depending on the context, ~데 can mean “and,” “but,” “yet,” or even “while.”

Also, ~지만 can only be used to connect sentences, whereas ~데 can be used both as a sentence connector and as a sentence ending.
