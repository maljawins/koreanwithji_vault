## Teaching Points

### Overview

In this lesson, the instructor introduces how to express the concept of "but" or "however" in Korean. The lesson is divided into two primary methods: using a conjunction at the beginning of a new sentence and using a specific verb ending to connect two contrasting clauses within a single sentence. The instructor provides several example sentences to demonstrate these forms in action and concludes with a conversation practice that highlights the subtle differences between time-related vocabulary often used in these contexts.

---

### Starting a Sentence with 하지만

The most straightforward way to say "but" or "however" at the beginning of a sentence is by using the word **하지만**. This is used to add contrasting information to a statement made in a previous sentence. The instructor demonstrates this with three distinct examples. First, he contrasts earning money with being busy:

> **저는 돈을 많이 벌어요. 하지만 항상 바빠요.** / I make (earn) a lot of money. But I'm always busy.

In this example, the instructor introduces the vocabulary word **항상 (always)**. The second example contrasts a general liking for cats with a stronger preference for monkeys, introducing the word **원숭이 (monkey)**:

> **저는 고양이를 좋아해요. 하지만 원숭이를 더 좋아해요.** / I like cats. But I like monkeys more.

The final example shows a contrast between nationality and personal taste:

> **저는 한국 사람이에요. 하지만 김치를 싫어해요.** / I am Korean. But I dislike kimchi.

---

### Connecting Sentences with the Verb Ending -지만

To continue a sentence without ending it, the instructor introduces the verb ending **-지만**. This is attached directly to a verb stem to mean "but." The construction is simply **Verb Stem + 지만**. The instructor revisits the previous examples to show how they can be combined into single, fluid sentences.

> **저는 돈을 많이 벌지만 항상 바빠요.** / I earn a lot of money, but I'm always busy.
> **저는 고양이를 좋아하지만 원숭이를 더 좋아해요.** / I like cats, but I like monkeys more.

A critical grammatical note is provided for the verb **이다 (to be)**. When using **-지만** with **이다**, the form changes based on whether the preceding noun ends in a consonant or a vowel. If it ends in a consonant, it becomes **이지만**; if it ends in a vowel, it becomes **지만**. This is illustrated using the Korean nationality example:

> **저는 한국 사람이지만 김치를 싫어해요.** / I'm Korean, but I dislike kimchi.

Because **사람 (person)** ends in the consonant **ㅁ**, the ending becomes **이지만**.

---

### Conversation Practice and Vocabulary Nuance

The lesson includes a dialogue between two characters, **재석** and **나래**, to show these forms in a natural context.

> **재석: 저는 이제 공원에 가요.** / Now I'm going to the park.
> **나래: 저도 가고 싶어요. 하지만 지금은 조금 바빠요.** / I want to go too. But right now, I'm a bit busy.
> **재석: 흠... 저는 지금 가고 싶지만 조금 기다릴 수 있어요.** / Hmm... I want to go now, but I can wait a little.
> **나래: 오! 고마워요!** / Oh! Thank you!

During this practice, the instructor clarifies the difference between **지금** and **이제**. While both can be translated as "now" in English, **지금** refers specifically to the current moment ("right now"). In contrast, **이제** refers to "from now" or a change in state regarding what is about to happen. Therefore, **저는 이제 공원에 가요** implies that from this point forward, the speaker is heading to the park.

---

### Additional Expressions: 그렇지만 and 그런데

The instructor concludes with two additional ways to say "but" at the start of a sentence. The first is **그렇지만**, which comes from the verb **그렇다 (to be so)** combined with the **-지만** ending. Literally meaning "it is so, but...", it functions similarly to **하지만** but carries a slightly stronger sense of contrast.

The second expression is **그런데**, which also stems from **그렇다** but uses the ending **-데**. While it also means "but" or "however," it shows less contrast than **하지만**. The instructor notes that **그런데** is much more common in casual or informal conversations.
