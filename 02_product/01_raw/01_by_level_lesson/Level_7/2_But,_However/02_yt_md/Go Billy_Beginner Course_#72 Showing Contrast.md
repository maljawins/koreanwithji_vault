## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces the concept of showing contrast in Korean sentences. While learners may already be familiar with the form **지만 (but)**, this lesson focuses on the **는데/은데/ㄴ데** form. This versatile ending also translates to "but" or sometimes "and," depending on the context. It is used to connect two halves of a sentence to show that there is something different or contrasting between them. However, the instructor notes that this form generally indicates a softer contrast than **지만**. Beyond simple contrast, it is frequently used when the speaker is providing background information or trying to explain a situation.

---

### Conjugating with Action Verbs

The conjugation for this form depends on whether the verb is an action verb or a descriptive verb. For action verbs, the process is straightforward: take the verb stem and attach **는데**. This can be used to connect two clauses or, by adding **요**, as a polite sentence ending. For example, **좋아하다 (to like)** becomes **좋아하는데**, and **먹다 (to eat)** becomes **먹는데**.

There are specific rules for irregular stems. Action verb stems ending in the consonant **ㄹ** must remove the **ㄹ** before attaching **는데**. Thus, **살다 (to live)** transforms into **사는데**. Additionally, the verbs **있다 (to exist/to have)** and **없다 (to not exist/to not have)** always use the action verb suffix, becoming **있는데** and **없는데** respectively.

---

### Conjugating with Descriptive Verbs

For descriptive verbs (adjectives), the conjugation depends on the final sound of the verb stem. If the stem ends in a consonant, attach **은데**. For example, **좋다 (to be good)** becomes **좋은데**. If the stem ends in a vowel, attach a **ㄴ** (niun) to the bottom of the stem followed by **데**. For example, **친절하다 (to be kind)** becomes **친절한데**.

Similar to action verbs, descriptive verbs have irregular patterns. For stems ending in **ㄹ**, the **ㄹ** is removed and replaced with **ㄴ데**, so **길다 (to be long)** becomes **긴데**. For stems ending in **ㅂ**, the **ㅂ** is removed and replaced with **운**, followed by **데**. An example of this is **맵다 (to be spicy)**, which becomes **매운데**.

---

### Usage and Examples of Contrast

The instructor provides several examples to demonstrate how this form functions as "but" in natural conversation. It often sets up a contrast where the second clause is unexpected or provides a different direction than the first.

- **저는 미식 축구를 좋아하는데 축구를 더 좋아해요.** / I like American football, but I like soccer more.
- **비가 오고 있는데 우산이 없어요.** / It's raining, but I don't have an umbrella.
- **아침에 눈이 왔는데 지금은 안 추워요.** / It snowed in the morning, but now it's not cold.
- **등산을 하는 게 재미있는데 비싸요.** / Hiking is fun, but expensive.
- **병원에 갔는데 의사를 만날 수 없었어요.** / I went to the hospital, but I couldn't meet the doctor.
- **바쁜데 만날 수 있어요?** / You're busy, but can you meet?
- **김치는 매운데 맛있어요.** / Kimchi is spicy, but delicious (or "spicy and delicious").

---

### Explaining and Background Information

A major distinction between **는데** and **지만** is that **는데** is used much more often when the speaker is trying to explain something or provide background context. In English, this usage might be prefaced with "You see," "Well," or "So." When used at the end of a sentence with **요**, it often implies that there is more to be said or invites a response from the listener.

The instructor highlights that the future tense **것이다** form can combine with this ending. **것이다** becomes **것인데**, or more commonly in speech, **건데**.

- **오늘 시간이 없는데요.** / (You see/Well) I don't have any time today.
- **내일 영화를 보러 갈 건데요.** / I'll go see a movie tomorrow (providing background).
- **포도가 맛있는데 너무 배불러요.** / The grapes are delicious, but I'm too full.
- **제 방에서 정말 큰 거미를 봤는데 좀 잡아주세요.** / I saw a really big spider in my room, so please catch it.

---

### Note on the Topic Marker

The instructor concludes with a reminder that while specific grammar forms like **는데** and **지만** show contrast, the most basic way to show contrast in Korean is through the topic marker **은/는**. By simply using the topic marker on the subjects being compared, a speaker can create a sense of contrast without any additional complex grammar.
