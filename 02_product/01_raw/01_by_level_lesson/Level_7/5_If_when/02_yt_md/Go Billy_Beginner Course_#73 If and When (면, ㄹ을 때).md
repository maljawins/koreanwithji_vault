## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces two essential grammar forms used to express the concepts of "if" and "when" in Korean. While English often uses these words distinctly, Korean grammar overlaps in some areas while remaining strictly separate in others. The lesson focuses on the conditional marker (으)면 and the temporal marker (으)ㄹ 때, explaining their conjugation rules, usage conditions, and the nuances that distinguish a hypothetical situation from a specific point in time.

The learning arc begins with the versatile (으)면 form, which covers both "if" and "when" for uncertain events. It then moves to (으)ㄹ 때, which specifically denotes the time when an action occurs. Finally, the instructor provides a comparative analysis to help learners choose the correct form based on whether a situation is hypothetical or a confirmed occurrence in the past, present, or future.

---

### The Conditional Marker: (으)면
The first form taught is (으)면, which is used to indicate that something might or might not happen. Depending on the context of the sentence, it can translate to either "if" or "when." To conjugate this form, one must first identify the verb stem. If the verb stem ends in a consonant, the suffix 으면 is attached. If the verb stem ends in a vowel, only 면 is attached. This form can be used in the middle of a sentence to connect clauses or at the end of a sentence by adding the polite ending 요.

The instructor provides several conjugation examples to illustrate the rules:
- **하다 (to do)** becomes **하면** (if/when you do)
- **먹다 (to eat)** becomes **먹으면** (if/when you eat)

Specific irregular rules are also highlighted. For verb stems ending in the consonant ㄹ, such as **살다 (to live)**, you simply attach 면 to get **살면**. For verb stems ending in the consonant ㅂ, the ㅂ is removed and replaced with 우 before adding 면. For example, **맵다 (to be spicy)** becomes **매우면**.

The instructor presents several example sentences on screen to demonstrate how (으)면 functions as both "if" and "when":
> **추우면 눈이 와요.** / It snows if it's cold. (Or: It snows when it's cold.)
> **사전을 사면 얘기해 주세요.** / Please tell me if you buy a dictionary. (Or: ...when you buy a dictionary.)
> **제가 모르면 힌트를 주세요.** / Give me a hint if or when I don't know.
> **쿠키를 좋아하면 제가 더 만들어 줄 수 있어요.** / If you like the cookies, I can make more for you.
> **저를 도와주시면 돈을 드릴 수 있어요.** / I can give you money if you help me.
> **아까 창문을 닫아서 덥지만 열면 시원해요.** / It's hot because you closed the window a little while ago, but if you open it, it is cool.

---

### The Temporal Marker: (으)ㄹ 때
The second form is (으)ㄹ 때, which is used exclusively to mean "when" in the sense of "at the time that" something happens. Unlike (으)면, this form cannot mean "if" because it refers to the actual time of an occurrence. The conjugation follows a similar pattern: attach 을 때 to verb stems ending in a consonant and ㄹ 때 to verb stems ending in a vowel.

Conjugation examples shown include:
- **하다 (to do)** becomes **할 때** (when/at the time I do)
- **먹다 (to eat)** becomes **먹을 때** (when/at the time I eat)

For ㄹ irregulars like **살다 (to live)**, you simply attach 때 to get **살 때**. For ㅂ irregulars like **덥다 (to be hot)**, the ㅂ is removed and replaced with 우 before adding ㄹ 때, resulting in **더울 때**.

The instructor provides examples of (으)ㄹ 때 in practice:
> **심심할 때 영화를 보세요.** / Watch a movie when you're bored. (Literally: At the time when you're bored.)
> **배가 고플 때 이걸 먹어요.** / Eat this when you are hungry.
> **우리 이따가 만날 때 빌려 주세요.** / Lend it to me when we meet a little bit later.
> **비가 올 때 밖에서 놀 수 없어요.** / I can't hang out outside when it rains.
> **초아 씨가 춤을 출 때 가장 예뻐요.** / Choa is the prettiest when she dances.
> **제가 어렸을 때 선생님이 되고 싶었어요.** / I wanted to be a teacher when I was young.

The final example demonstrates that (으)ㄹ 때 can be used with past tense stems (었/았/였) to refer to a specific time in the past.

---

### Choosing Between (으)면 and (으)ㄹ 때
The instructor emphasizes the critical difference: (으)면 is for hypothetical situations (might or might not happen), while (으)ㄹ 때 is for the actual time of an event (past, present, or future).

If a situation is purely hypothetical, using (으)ㄹ 때 sounds awkward. For example, in the sentence "If you really like that guy, learn Korean," the instructor explains that **좋아하면** is correct because it is a hypothesis. Using **좋아할 때** (when you like him) would imply a guaranteed timeline for those feelings, which doesn't fit the context.

Conversely, (으)면 cannot be used for things that have already happened. In the sentence "When we met last year, we went to an amusement park," the instructor notes that **만났을 때** is the only correct choice. Using **만났으면** would incorrectly imply "if we met last year," which is impossible since the meeting already occurred.

To strengthen the "if" meaning of (으)면, the words **만약** or **만약에** can be added to the beginning of the sentence. This adds emphasis to the hypothetical nature of the statement.

### Conversation Practice
The lesson concludes with a dialogue between two characters, 이린 and 슬기, incorporating the new forms.

> **이린: 내년에 졸업하면 뭐 할 거예요?** / When or if you graduate next year, what will you do?
> **슬기: 글쎄요... 이린 씨는요?** / Well, I'm not sure. How about you, 이린?
> **이린: 저는 식당을 열고 싶어요.** / I want to open a restaurant.
> **슬기: 그러면 요리를 배울 거예요?** / In that case, will you learn cooking?
> **이린: 네, 이미 배우기 시작했어요.** / Yes, I already started learning.
> **슬기: 돈은 있어요? 식당이 비싼데요?** / Do you have money? A restaurant is expensive.
> **이린: 네, 제가 요리를 잘 하면 부모님이 도와주실 거예요.** / Yes, if I cook well, my parents will help me.
> **슬기: 그럼 만약에 식당을 열면, 저도 거기서 일하고 싶어요.** / Well then, if you open a restaurant, I also want to work there.

The instructor points out the use of **만약에** in the final sentence to emphasize the "if" condition of opening the restaurant.
