## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces the grammar form ~답시고 and its noun-based variant ~(이)랍시고, which are used to express a specific type of contrast rooted in disappointment, sarcasm, or mockery. The core function of this form is to describe a situation where someone (either the speaker or a third party) intends or claims to do something positive, but the actual result is negative, insufficient, or even makes the situation worse. It is essentially a "quoting form" mixed with a heavy dose of attitude.

The lesson arc begins with the mechanical construction of the form using the plain form of verbs and adjectives. It then moves into the pragmatic nuances of using it with third parties to show sarcasm, and finally explores how to use it in the first person for modesty or self-deprecation. Throughout the video, the instructor emphasizes that as soon as a listener hears this form, they are primed to expect a disappointing or failed outcome in the second half of the sentence.

---

### Construction and Mechanics

To create this form, the speaker first takes the plain form (narrative form) of a verb or adjective and attaches ~ㅂ시고. For action verbs, this means using the ~ㄴ/는다고 quoting base. For example, the verb 하다 (to do) becomes 한다 in the plain form, resulting in 한답시고. The verb 먹다 (to eat) becomes 먹는다 in the plain form, resulting in 먹는답시고. While this can be used in various tenses, it is most commonly used in the present tense to describe the intent behind an action.

For descriptive verbs (adjectives), the plain form is identical to the dictionary form. Therefore, 좋다 (to be good) simply becomes 좋답시고. There is a specific exception for the copula 이다 (to be). In a quoting context, 이다 transforms into 이라고. Following this pattern, the grammar form becomes 이랍시고 after a consonant or 랍시고 after a vowel. This mechanical foundation allows the speaker to "quote" an intention or a description before contrasting it with a disappointing reality.

---

### Expressing Sarcasm and Disappointment with Third Parties

When used to describe the actions of a third person, ~답시고 carries a strong sense of sarcasm or mockery. It implies that the subject's stated intention was either pretentious or a total failure. The instructor explains that the sentence structure usually follows a pattern: the subject intends to do "A" (something positive), but the actual result is "B" (something negative or disappointing).

The instructor provides an example involving a character named Jeremy:
> **제레미가 한국어 공부를 한답시고 게임을 시작했다가 게임 중독이 되었대요.** / Jeremy said he was going to study Korean, but then he started playing games and I heard he became addicted to them.

In this case, using 한답시고 instead of a neutral form like 한다고 해서 adds the speaker's judgment. It suggests that Jeremy's "intent" to study was laughable or poorly executed. The instructor notes a critical grammatical rule: when talking about a third person's feelings or intentions, you must use a quoting form at the end of the sentence (like ~대요) because you cannot claim to know their internal state directly. Another example illustrates a failed errand:
> **영수는 책을 산답시고 서점에 갔는데 장난감 구경만 했대.** / Young-su went to the bookstore saying he'd buy books, but I heard he only looked at toys.

The listener knows immediately upon hearing 산답시고 that Young-su is going to "mess up" or fail his original goal.

---

### Using Descriptive Verbs for Negative Results

When used with descriptive verbs, the form functions similarly to a quoting form where someone "calls" or "thinks" something is a certain way, leading to a negative outcome. It often translates to "saying/thinking it is [Adjective], [Negative Result]."

The instructor demonstrates this with the noun-based 이랍시고:
> **아이가 선물이랍시고 막대사탕을 줬어요.** / The child gave me a lollipop, calling it a "present."

The sarcasm here is gentle; the lollipop is technically a gift, but the speaker uses 선물이랍시고 to highlight the gap between a "real" present and a simple lollipop. A more frustrated example involves a poor purchase:
> **작은 신발을 예쁘답시고 샀더니 신지는 못하고 장식품이 되었어요.** / I bought these small shoes thinking they were pretty, but I can't even wear them and they just became decorations.

The speaker is mocking their own past logic (thinking the shoes were pretty) because that logic led to the current disappointing reality of owning unwearable shoes.

---

### First-Person Usage: Modesty and Self-Deprecation

When the speaker uses ~답시고 to describe their own actions, the tone shifts from mockery of others to modesty or self-deprecation. It is a way to downplay one's own efforts, essentially saying, "Well, I tried to do this, but..." This is often used when presenting a gift or a favor to lower expectations.

A common pattern in the first person is to repeat the verb or use a similar verb after the ~답시고 clause to emphasize the effort made despite the potential for failure.
> **파는 것과 비슷하게 만든답시고 만들어 봤는데 맛이 있을지 모르겠어요.** / I tried to make it like the store-bought ones, but I don't know if it'll taste good.

By using 만든답시고, the speaker is being modest, suggesting that their attempt to mimic professional quality might have fallen short. Other examples of self-deprecation include:
> **첫 버스를 탄답시고 일찍 나왔는데 버스를 놓쳐서 한참 기다렸어요.** / I left early trying to catch the first bus, but I missed it and had to wait a long time.
> **절약한답시고 직접 페인트를 칠했는데 너무 이상하게 됐어요.** / I painted it myself trying to save money, but it turned out really strange.
> **모기를 잡는답시고 약을 잔뜩 뿌렸더니 머리가 아파요.** / I sprayed a ton of spray trying to catch mosquitoes, and now I have a headache.

In each case, the speaker highlights their "positive" intention (catching mosquitoes, saving money) and contrasts it with the "stupid" or "disappointing" result (a headache, a weird paint job), effectively making fun of their own failure.
