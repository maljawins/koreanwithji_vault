**While**

Now, we’re going to learn how to say “while” in Korean.

In English, "While" is very versatile with its two main functions being to indicate duration or contrast depending on the context.

1. Duration

- I read a book while waiting for the bus.

2. Contrast

- While I like cats, I’m allergic.

in English, we use the same word "while" for both, but in Korean, we use different connectors depending on the specific idea we want to convey.

And today we're going to cover these four different Korean forms that can translate to "while".

**(으)면서 #1**

The first form we're going to look at is **(으)면서**.

This form is **받침-sensitive**:

* If the verb stem has a final consonant (받침), you add 으면서. For example, 먹다 (to eat) becomes 먹으면서.
* If the verb stem ends in a vowel (no 받침), you just add 면서. For example, 하다 (to do) becomes 하면서.

For **Irregular Verbs**, here's how they work with (으)면서:

* ㄷ irregular verbs (like 걷다 - to walk) → ㄹ + 으면서 → 걸으면서.
* ㄹ batchim verbs (like 알다 - to know) → just add 면서 → 알면서.
* ㅂ irregular verbs (like 덥다 - to be hot) → 우 + 면서 → 더우면서.

**면서의 뜻**

Now, you can think of 면서 as combining the "if or when" meaning of ~면 with the "continuity" meaning of ~서.

So, it can be translated as **"While"** or **"At the same time."**

집에 가면서 친구랑 통화를 했어요. (I had a phone call with my friend while going home.) - This means the two actions happened simultaneously.

디자인이 세련되면서(도) 비싸지 않은 신발을 찾고 있어요. (I'm looking for shoes that are stylish but also not expensive at the same time.)

**2. Contrast:** 면서 can also show contrast, similar to the English "while."

When it does, you'll often see (도) optionally added after 면서, like in the example above.

도 emphasizes 면서 like "even at the same time," which can be interpreted as "but at the same time."

* 실수한 거 알고 계시면서 왜 사과를 안 하세요? (Why don't you apologize while (when) you know you made a mistake?)
* 저는 돈이 많이 없었으면서도 항상 남을 도와줬습니다. (While I didn't have a lot of money, I always helped others.)

There's also a super common phrase: **알면서**. "while knowing”, with the second part of the sentence just.. implied.

It's like saying, "You know that, don't you?" or "You should know that," but in a friendly way.

너밖에 없는 거 알면서... (You know you're all I've got...). It implies "you should know how I feel about you."

**(으)면서 #2**

Here are a couple more things to know about (으)면서

**It can come after both Action Verbs and Descriptive Verbs.**

* 저는 음악을 들으면서 운동을 해요. (I exercise while listening to music.)
* 저는 재미있으면서 가볍지 않은 사람을 좋아해요. (I like people who are funny but not shallow at the same time.)

**The subject for both clauses must be the same.** This is a crucial rule for (으)면서.

* In English, you can say, "I exercise while *he* listens to music."
* But in Korean, you **cannot** say 얘가 음악을 들으면서 제가 운동을 해요. (X)
* The subject of both the first and second part of the sentence must be identical when using (으)면서.
* If you want to use two different subjects, you need to use 동안, which we'll learn next!

**동안(에)**

Alright, let's move on to **동안(에)**

“For a while” in Korean is “한동안”. And “For a long time” is “오랫동안”.

**음식이 많아서 한동안 마트에 안 가도 돼요.**

**- There’s a lot of food so I don’t have to go to the mart for a while**

**너무 오랫동안 자면 개운하게 일어날 수 없어요.**

**- If you sleep for too long, you can’t wake up refreshingly**

So, unlike “면서”, “동안” cannot show contrast. It can only show duration.

you can optionally attach 에 after 동안 but you can drop it most of the time.

**It can be used after time nouns.** Here, 동안 works like a particle meaning "during" or "for."

* 여름 동안 어디로 여행가고 싶으세요? (Where do you want to travel during the summer?)
* 오늘은 다섯 시간 동안 영화만 봤어요. (Today, all I did was watch movies for five hours.)

**~ 는 동안(에)**

2. After a verb, 동안 is treated like a noun so you need use a modifier. Hence, **는** 동안. 90% of the time, people use 는, not 던.

So, I recommend sticking with 는 동안 rather than 던 동안

Note that in Korean, when two sentences are connected like this, the tense of the clause before the connector doesn't usually need to match the main clause's tense unlike in English, where you often have to match tenses, like "While I *was* at home, I *studied* Korean."

**3. You can only use action verbs with ~는 동안(에)**, not descriptive verbs with the exceptions of 있다 and 없다.

* **자는 동안(에) 전화가 와서 깼어요**

**– While I was sleeping, I got a phone call so I woke up.**

* **집에 있는 동안 한국어를 공부했어요**

**- I studied Korean while I was at home**

4. You CAN use different subjects with 동안 unlike 면서. This is a very important distinction.

It's more natural to use 면서 when the subjects are the same but if you need to describe two different subjects doing actions simultaneously, you *have* to use 동안

* **여자친구가 쉬는 동안 저는 산책을 했어요**

**- While my girlfriend was resting, I went for a walk**

**V.S + 은/ㄴ 채(로)**

다음 form은 은/ㄴ ~채(로) 이다.

채 is a bound noun meaning "state." Again, a bound noun is a noun that's ‘bound’ to a grammar form. And you need to use 은/ㄴ before 채 to modify it.

This form can also be translated as "while" in English, but it's different from (으)면서 and 동안. The key difference is exactly the same as the difference between ~고 있다 and 아/어 있다.

* ~면서 and 동안 are like ~고 있다 – they describe **"while doing the action"** or an action that is happening.
* ~은/ㄴ 채(로) is like 아/어 있다 – it describes **"while in a certain state"** or "in the state of a completed action."

And ~은/ㄴ 채(로) can be used with both **Action Verbs and Descriptive Verbs**.

**A.V.S + 은/ㄴ 채(로)**

저는 누우면서 잠들었어요. This one means I fell asleep AS I was lying myself down. No one’s capable of falling asleep while actively lying down unless you’re fainting so you should instead say:

저는 누운 채로 잠들었어요. This one means I fell asleep while lying down.

**D.V.S + 은/ㄴ 채(로)**

피곤한 채로 운전을 했어요 - I drove (while) tired.

In English, you would just say “I drove tired” without “while” but in Korean, we use 은 채 to describe the state you were in while driving. It emphasizes that you were in a tired state *throughout* the driving

**~다가**

Alright you’ve seen ~다가 before during your last listening practice, so you probably have a rough idea what it means.

When ~다가 comes after a **present stem**, it's used when **Action A, which you were originally doing, is interrupted by Action B.**

Koreans often drop the 가 in 다가. So, sometimes it might just look like the dictionary form of a verb, which can be confusing. When that happens, just assume the 가 is omitted.

Let's look at some common expressions with ~다가:

* **하다(가) 말다**: This means "to do something but then stop doing it." For example, 공부하다가 말았어요. (I was studying but then stopped.)

일하다(가) 말고 어디 가세요? (Where are you going while working? [and not finishing?])

* **이랬다가 저랬다가**: This literally means "to be like this but then like that." It's used to describe someone or something that's inconsistent or goes back and forth.

이랬다가 저랬다가 하지 말고 원하는 걸 말해. (Don't be so capricious and just say what you want.)

* **왔다 갔다**: This means "back and forth."

So, it’ll help to think of ~다가 as "But then," and "While," or "As." Combined.

While ~면서 and 동안 usually indicate actions happening simultaneously, 다가 shows something new happening *in the middle* of an ongoing action.

You can think of them as opposite concepts in that sense.

집에 가다가 우연히 친구를 만났어요. (I met a friend while going home.) This implies you were on your way home, and then the meeting happened, interrupting your journey.

And, just like with (으)면서, the **subject of Action A and Action B must be the same** when using ~다가.

**Past stem + ~다가**

When 다가 comes after a past stem, there are two main usages.

1. **Action A was completed before Action B happened.**
   * 했다가 (past stem + 다가) has a stronger **"and then"** meaning. It indicates that the first action was fully completed before the next one began.
   * Example: 나 편의점에 갔다(가) 올게. (I'm going to the convenience store and then come back.) This means: "I'll complete going there, and then I'll come back."
2. **Action B happens unexpectedly as a result of Action A.**
   * 새벽 네 시에 일어났다가 오후에 졸려졌어요. (I woke up at 4 AM, but then I became sleepy in the afternoon.)
   * Here, waking up early led to an unexpected result of being sleepy later.

**~다(가) 보니(까) #1**

We know that 니까 means “because” but you might be wondering how “while” and “why” can come together.

Actually, to understand ~다(가) 보니(까), you first need to know an advanced usage of 니까. 니까 doesn't *only* mean "because." In this case, its meaning is very similar to **"as"** in English.

It can express either a **sequential action** or an **unexpected twist of events**, just like "as" in English.

* **Sequential action example:** 부엌에 들어오니까 맛있는 냄새가 나기 시작했어요. (As I entered the kitchen, a delicious smell started emerging / it started to smell nice.) The entering led to the smell.
* **Unexpected twist example:** 집에 오니까 고양이가 소파에 오줌을 싸 놨네요. (As I came home, I discovered the cat had peed on the sofa.) Coming home led to an unexpected discovery.

Notice that 니까 clause is in the **present tense** just like I mentioned earlier.

**~다(가) 보니(까) #2**

So, the 니까 in 다(가) 보니(까) has that "as" meaning.

What it means is while you’re doing A, you stop, because that’s what 다가 does, then you look and AS you look, action B happens.

Depending on the usage, it can be translated as **"Now that," "Since,"** or **"After."**

And because of this meaning, Action B often turns out to be something you've **come to realize** as a result of Action A. Therefore, Action B typically works with **present tense or past tense**, but **not future tense**

예문 2개

* 살다 보니 이렇게 좋은 일이 생기네요. (Now that I've lived [for so long], something this good happens.) This implies a realization that good things are happening as a result of living.
* 열심히 노력하다 보니 이 자리까지 올 수 있었습니다. (After having worked hard, I was able to succeed to this position.) This shows a realization of success as a result of continuous effort.

Remember, the keyword for this form is **realization**, because we have a similar form, 다(가) 보면, which has a different nuance.

**~다(가) 보면**

Now, the keyword for '다가 보면' is natural outcome.

What it means is while doing A, you stop and you look. And IF you continue to look, B will happen naturally as a result of A. So, Action B tends to be in either **present, future tense, or ~겠다 presumptive** form. You **cannot use past tense** with ~다(가) 보면.

* **길을 따라서 가다 보면 오른쪽에 지하철역이 보일 거예요**

**- As you go along the road, you’ll see the subway station on the right side.**

* **꾸준히 공부하다 보면 이해할 수 있을 거예요**

**- After studying consistently, you’ll be able to understand**

So, ~다(가) 보면 is about a predictable or natural consequence if an action continues, whereas ~다(가) 보니(까) is about a realization that occurs as a result of an action.

**(으)러 & (으)려고 - Purpose & Intention**

Let's learn about two connectors that express purpose and intention : ~(으)러 and ~(으)려고.

Both forms can only be used with **action verbs** and are **받침-sensitive**

But if the verb stem ends in ㄹ, you can just attach 러 or 려고. (e.g., 살다 (to live) → 살러, 살려고)

And Koreans often drop 고 in 려고, so you'll frequently hear just 려.

**differences between these two forms**:

1. **Verb Type:**
   * After (으)러, you should always use some kind of **motion verb** like 가다 (to go) or 오다 (to come).
   * After (으)려고, you can use **any verb**.
   * Example: 사업을 시작하러 준비하고 있어요. (X) This is wrong because 준비하다 is not a motion verb.
   * Instead: 사업을 시작하려고 준비하고 있어요. (O) (I’m getting ready to start a business.)

**2. (으)러 can be used with the imperative or propositive sentences. But (으)려고 can’t**

**Sentence Type:**

* (으)러 can be used with **imperative (commands) or propositive (suggestions) sentences**.
* (으)려고 **cannot** be used with imperative or propositive sentences.
* Example: 놀러 와. (Come hang out.) This is correct.
* 놀려고 와. (X) This is incorrect.

**Subject Type:**

* For (으)러, the subject can **only be people or animals**.
* For (으)려고, the subject can be **anything** (people, animals, or inanimate things).

And there’s also a difference in nuance.

I mentioned in the last lesson that if you see 려, you should immediately think of "intention."

So, 려고 has a stronger feeling of purpose or intention. It'll help to think of (으)려고 like "intending to."

 으)러 is less decisive and is used more simply like **"to,"** meaning it enables or allows the verb to happen. It's more casual.

 (으)려고 has a stronger sense of purpose. You can think of it as **"in order to,"** and sometimes even **"about to"** or the English "going to" (with intention)

A common expression is 놀러와 / 놀러오세요.

놀다 means "to play" in the sense of "to have fun."

So, 놀러와 has a more casual feeling like "Hey, come over, let’s hang out."

No one ever says 놀려고 와 (X), because "Come in order to have fun" sounds weird.

Also, when you attach a **past tense verb** after (으)려고, it forms the meaning of **"was going to"**.

* **가려고 했는데 못 갔어요 ▶ I was going to go, but I couldn’t**

**3. The subject (으)려고 can be inanimate things while (으)러 can only have people or animals as the subject.**

* It means: **AS you're intending to do Action A, Action B suddenly happens and has a negative effect on Action A, your initial intent.**

** 버스에서 자려니까 아기가 울기 시작했어요. (As I was about to sleep on the bus, a baby started crying.) The baby crying negatively affected the intention to sleep.**

** 비행기표를 예매하려니까 갑자기 매진이 됐어요. (As I was about to book the plane ticket, it suddenly sold out.) The selling out negatively affected the intention to book.**

**을/ㄹ라고**

Now, if you find 려 a bit too hard to pronounce, you could alternatively say 을라고 instead.

This pronunciation originated from the Seoul/Gyeonggi dialect, but everyone uses it nationwide, including myself without even thinking.

But, if you want to speak standard Korean perfectly, try this:

 ㄹ is neither an "R" nor an "L." It's actually closer to the sound of the double "T" in "Butter." Say "Butter" out loud. Do you feel your tongue position is exactly in the middle of "R" and an "L" sound?

 Remember that tongue position. Now, say "여" like in "Young."

 And then try to combine that ㄹ tongue position with "여" to say **"려."**
