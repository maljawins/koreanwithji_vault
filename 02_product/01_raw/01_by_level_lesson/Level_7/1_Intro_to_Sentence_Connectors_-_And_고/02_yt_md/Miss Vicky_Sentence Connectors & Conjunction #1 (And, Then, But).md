## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor provides a comprehensive deep dive into Korean sentence connectors and conjunctions, focusing on those that mean "and," "then," and "but." The lesson begins by clarifying the technical distinction between a sentence connector and a conjunction. A sentence connector is an independent word (like 그리고) used to link two separate sentences. A conjunction is formed when that connector is attached directly to the verb or adjective of the first sentence, transforming two sentences into one cohesive unit. This process involves dropping the sentence-ending punctuation and modifying the verb/adjective stem according to specific rules.

The learning arc follows a structured path: first, the instructor covers the additive connector 그리고 (and/then), then the contrastive connectors 하지만 and 그렇지만 (but), and finally the more complex 그런데 and 근데 (but/by the way). For each, she demonstrates how to use them as standalone words and how to conjugate them into conjunctions across present, past, and future tenses. The instructor emphasizes that while conjunctions make speech more efficient and natural, they should be used in moderation to avoid "run-on" sentences that never seem to end.

---

### The Additive Connector: 그리고 (And, Then)

The word 그리고 serves three primary functions: as a noun connector, a verb connector, and a sentence connector. While nouns are typically connected using particles like 와/과, 랑, or 하고, 그리고 can be used when listing multiple items, such as 영어, 한국어, 그리고 러시아어 (English, Korean, and Russian). When used to connect verbs or sentences, 그리고 transforms into the conjunction suffix ~고.

The conjugation rule for ~고 is the most straightforward among all connectors. To form the conjunction, one simply takes the stem of the verb or adjective and attaches 고. In the present tense, the base stem is used: 먹다 (to eat) becomes 먹고. For example, "I watch a movie and my brother reads a book" becomes 저는 영화를 보고 오빠는 책을 읽어요. This rule applies equally to adjectives: 지수는 귀엽고 착해요 (Jisoo is cute and kind).

In the past tense, the instructor notes a specific nuance: while one can use the past stem (e.g., 먹었고), it is very common and often preferred to use the present/base stem (먹고) as long as the final verb of the sentence is conjugated in the past tense. The listener understands the entire sequence happened in the past based on the final verb. For the future tense, the connector is attached to the future stem ~ㄹ/을 거, resulting in ~ㄹ/을 거고.

Beyond meaning "and," 그리고 (and its conjunction form ~고) frequently means "and then," indicating a sequence of actions. The instructor provides examples like 저녁을 먹고 설거지를 했어요 (I ate dinner and then did the dishes). A specialized use of ~고 also appears in song lyrics to mean "while" or "at the same time," such as 손 잡고 갈게 (I will go while holding your hands).

---

### Contrastive Connectors: 하지만 and 그렇지만 (But)

하지만 and 그렇지만 are interchangeable connectors used to show contrast. They both share the same conjunction suffix: ~지만. The rule for the present tense is identical to the "and" connector: attach ~지만 directly to the base stem. For example, "I am good at Korean but bad at English" is 저는 한국어를 잘하지만 영어는 못해요.

A critical difference between ~고 and ~지만 occurs in the past tense. Unlike the "and" connector, where the first verb can remain in the present form, the "but" connector requires the first verb to be explicitly conjugated in the past tense if the action is finished. The instructor demonstrates this with 사랑했지만 (loved but), noting that matching the tenses is necessary for clarity in contrastive sentences.

The instructor also introduces the adjective 착하다 (to be nice/kind) and the slang term 노잼 (no fun/lame), which is a combination of "No" and 재미 (fun). These are used in contrastive examples like "The movie is interesting but long" (재미있지만 길어요).

---

### The Versatile Connector: 그런데 and 근데 (But, By the Way)

그런데 (and its shortened form 근데) is the most common connector in spoken Korean but also the most grammatically complex. Unlike the previous connectors, it follows different rules for adjectives and verbs in the present tense. For adjectives, the stem takes ~ㄴ/은 데 (ㄴ if no batchim, 은 if there is a batchim). For verbs, the stem always takes ~는데 regardless of batchim.

The instructor highlights a "fixed rule" for words ending in 있다 or 없다 (like 재미있다 or 맛있다). Even though these function as adjectives, they are treated as verbs for this specific grammar rule, meaning they always take ~는데 (e.g., 재미있는데). For the past tense, the complexity disappears as both adjectives and verbs follow the same rule: attach ~는데 to the past stem (e.g., 좋았는데, 공부했는데).

Beyond its role as a "but" connector, 근데 is frequently used as a sentence starter to mean "By the way." It is used to change the topic, ask a favor out of the blue, or break a silence. The instructor distinguishes this from 아참, which is used specifically when you suddenly remember something you had forgotten.

---

### Formal Writing Connector: 그러나 (But)

The connector 그러나 is strictly reserved for formal writing, such as textbooks, newspaper articles, or documentaries. It is rarely used in speech. Its conjunction form is ~나 (for present base stems) or ~으나 (for past stems). The instructor provides high-level examples involving the habits of animals (herbivores vs. carnivores) and political news to illustrate the serious, non-conversational tone this connector carries.

---

### Summary of Formality Levels

The instructor concludes by ranking the connectors by their level of "seriousness" or formality:
1.  **그러나:** Most serious, used almost exclusively in writing.
2.  **하지만 / 그렇지만:** Formal and polite, common in both writing and speech.
3.  **그런데 / 근데:** Least serious, most natural and common in daily conversation.

There is actually one more meaning that is still used in lots of lyrics. 고, when it's a conjunction, can also mean "at the same time." 니 손 잡고 갈게. I will go while holding your hands. 놀라지 말고 들어. Don't be surprised and listen at the same time. 가방을 들고 뛰었어요. I ran while holding my bag.

Okay, let's move on to 그렇지만 and 하지만. "But." As you can see, the commonality of these two sentence connectors is 지만. They both have the same conjunction form and the same meaning. You just put the base stem and then connect it with 지만. 저는 한국어를 잘해요. 하지만 영어는 못해요. 잘하다 is the base form, right? 잘하지만. 저는 한국어를 잘하지만 영어는 못해요. 엄마는 차를 마셔요. 그렇지만 아빠는 안 마셔요. 마시지만. 엄마는 차를 마시지만 아빠는 안 마셔요. 저는 TV를 보고 있어요 하지만 동생이 방해해요. 보고 있지만. 동생 means younger sibling. 방해하다 means to interrupt or disturb.

They can also be combined with an adjective following the same rule. 지수는 예쁘지만 저는 안 예뻐요. Jisoo is pretty but I am not. 떡볶이는 맛있지만 매워요. Tteokbokki is delicious but spicy. 맛있다 means delicious. 맛 means taste and 있다 means to be there. So 맛있다 means "there is taste." 맛없다 means "there isn't taste." 한국어는 재미있지만 어려워요. Korean is interesting but hard. 노잼 is a slang word. "No" plus 재미. It means no fun or lame.

When it's past tense, you put past stem and 지만. Unlike 그리고, for 지만 you have to make it in past form. You have to. 데니는 샌디를 사랑했지만 용기가 없었어요. Danny loved Sandy but had no courage. 용기 is courage. 점심은 먹었지만 저녁은 안 먹었어요. I ate lunch but didn't eat dinner. 한국은 예전에 가난했지만 지금은 많이 발전했어요. Korea used to be poor but now it has developed a lot. 가난하다 means to be poor. 발전하다 means to develop.

Future tense: I will meet Jisoo but not now. 지수를 만날 거지만 지금은 아니에요. Jeff will not come but it's okay. 제프는 안 올 거지만 괜찮아요.

Finally, we're moving on to the third one. 그런데 and 근데. This is the hardest one because the conjunction rule is quite different. 그런데 and 근데 is the only sentence connector that has different rules for adjectives and verbs in the present tense. For adjectives, you put base stem plus ㄴ or 은 plus 데. 걔는 예뻐 근데 성격이 안 좋아. 걔는 예쁜데 성격이 안 좋아. She is pretty but her personality is not good. 성격 means personality. 걔는 성격이 안 좋은데 예뻐. Her personality is not good but she is pretty.

For verbs, it's different. You put 는데 regardless of batchim. 저는 한국어를 공부해요 근데 아직 잘 못해요. 공부하는데. 아직 means yet or still. 저는 영화를 보는데 언니가 방해해요. I'm watching a movie but my sister is interrupting. Now, 재미있다 and 맛있다. Every word that contains 있다, we treat it like a verb in these rules. So even if 재미있다 is an adjective, it follows the verb rule. 재미있는데. 맛있는데.

저는 집에 가고 싶어요 근데 아직 셋이에요. 싶은데. 싶다 is treated like an adjective. I want to go home but it's still three o'clock. 저는 배가 고픈데 집에 음식이 없어요. I'm hungry but there's no food at home. 저는 여행을 좋아하는데 돈이 없어요. I like traveling but I don't have money. 저는 한국어가 좋은데 너무 어려워요. I like Korean but it's too hard. 좋다 is initially an adjective meaning "to be good," so it follows the adjective rule: 좋은데.

Past tense gets easier because both adjective and verb follow the same rule: 는데. 제프는 예전에 성격이 좋았는데 요즘에는 별로예요. Jeff's personality used to be good but recently it's not that good. 별로 means "not that much" or "not that good." 어제 사람이 많았는데 오늘은 적네요. There were many people yesterday but today there are less. 적다 means to be small in quantity.

열심히 공부했는데 시험을 망쳤어요. I studied hard but ruined the test. 망치다 means to ruin or spoil. 실패하다 means to fail in general (like a business), not necessarily a test. 저는 안 갈 건데 엄마는 갈 거예요. I won't go but mom will. 저는 많이 먹는데 지수는 조금 먹어요. I eat a lot but Jisoo eats a little.

근데 can also mean "By the way." This is a sentence starter. 근데, 저 부탁 하나 해도 돼요? By the way, can I ask you a favor? 근데, 나 핸드폰 좀 빌려줄 수 있어? By the way, can I borrow your phone? 근데, 여기 진짜 예쁘다. By the way, it's really pretty here. 아참 is used when you just remember something. 근데 is used when you change the topic or say something out of the blue.

Our last sentence connector is 그러나. This is not very important for speaking because it's mostly used in writing. It sounds the most serious. 소는 초식동물이다 그러나 호랑이는 육식동물이다. Cows are herbivores but tigers are carnivores. 초식동물 means herbivore. 육식동물 means carnivore. 문 대통령은 최선을 다했다 그러나 계약이 성사되지 못했다. President Moon did his best but the contract was not successful. 최선을 다하다 means to do one's best. The conjunction form is ~나 or ~으나. 초식동물이나... 최선을 다했으나...
