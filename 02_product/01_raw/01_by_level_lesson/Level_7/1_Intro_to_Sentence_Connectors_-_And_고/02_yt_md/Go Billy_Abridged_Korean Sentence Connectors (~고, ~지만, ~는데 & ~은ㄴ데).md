## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the concept of sentence connectors as grammar forms used to combine two separate sentences into a single, cohesive thought. In English, this is achieved through words like "and," "but," or "though." In Korean, these connectors are attached directly to verb stems. The lesson focuses on three primary connectors: **~고** (and), **~지만** (but), and the versatile **~는데 / ~은/ㄴ데** (contrast/explaining).

The learning arc begins with basic conjugation rules for each form, followed by their specific nuances. A key theme throughout the video is the pragmatic function of these connectors—how they can be used not just to join sentences, but also to end sentences to make one's speech sound softer, more polite, or less direct.

---

### The Connector ~고 (And)
The connector **~고** is used to link two verbs or sentences with the meaning of "and." To conjugate, you simply take the verb stem by removing **다** and attach **고**. For example, **가다 (to go)** becomes **가고**, and **먹다 (to eat)** becomes **먹고**. The instructor provides the example:
> **먹고 가요.** / Eat and go.

It is important to note that **~고** is specifically for connecting verbs. To connect nouns, different particles are required. Additionally, if you wish to start a new sentence with "And," you must use the standalone conjunction **그리고 (and / and then / also)**. You cannot start a sentence with **고**.

A unique feature of **~고** is its use as a sentence ending. By ending a sentence with **~고(요)**, the speaker signals two things: first, that they are not quite finished with their thought, and second, that they want to soften the statement. This makes the sentence sound nicer and less direct than a hard statement ending in a standard polite termination.

---

### The Connector ~지만 (But)
To express "but" or "however" within a single sentence, the form **~지만** is attached to the verb stem. Like **~고**, the conjugation is straightforward: remove **다** and add **지만**. The instructor demonstrates this with a contrast of desires and obligations:
> **놀고 싶지만 슈퍼에 가야 돼요.** / I want to play, but I have to go to the store.

If the speaker wants to start a completely new sentence with "But," they must use **하지만**. The instructor compares two ways of saying the same thing:
> **저는 김치를 좋아하지만 김밥을 싫어해요.** / I like kimchi but I dislike kimbap.
> **저는 김치를 좋아해요. 하지만 김밥을 싫어해요.** / I like kimchi. But I dislike kimbap.

---

### The Versatile ~는데 and ~은/ㄴ데
This connector is more complex as its conjugation depends on the type of verb and the tense. For action verbs in the present tense, you attach **~는데** to the stem. For example, **가다** becomes **가는데** and **먹다** becomes **먹는데**. If a stem ends in **ㄹ**, it must be dropped before adding **는데**, so **살다 (to live)** becomes **사는데**.

For descriptive verbs (adjectives), the rules are different. If the stem ends in a vowel, you attach **~ㄴ데**; if it ends in a consonant, you attach **~은데**.
- **친절하다 (to be kind)** → **친절한데**
- **작다 (to be small)** → **작은데**
- **길다 (to be long)** → **긴데** (The **ㄹ** is dropped)

Special rules apply to **있다 (to exist/have)** and **없다 (to not exist/not have)**, which always take **~는데**, resulting in **있는데** and **없는데**. For descriptive verbs ending in **ㅂ**, such as **맵다 (to be spicy)**, the **ㅂ** changes to **우** before adding **ㄴ데**, resulting in **매운데**. In the past tense, all verbs simply take **~었는데/았는데** (e.g., **했다** becomes **했는데**).

---

### Functions of ~는데 / ~은/ㄴ데: Contrast and Explaining
The instructor explains that this form has two main usages. First, it is used for **Contrast**. It can mean "and" or "but" depending on the context, but its primary role is to show that two ideas are different. It is much softer than **~고** or **~지만**. While **~지만** can sometimes sound harsh or direct, **~는데** provides a friendlier, more sincere tone.

The second usage is **Explaining**. It sets the background or provides context for what the speaker is about to say, often translated as "So..." or "Well, the thing is..."
> **이 문법은 설명하기 어려운데 재밌어요.** / This grammar is hard to explain, [so/but] it's fun.
> **저는 미국 사람인데 아빠가 한국 사람이에요.** / I'm an American, and/but my dad is Korean.
> **만나고 싶은데 시간이 없어요.** / I want to meet, but I don't have time.

Ending a sentence with **~는데요** is a common way to give a soft, polite explanation or to suggest a conflicting situation without being blunt. For instance, saying **시간이 없는데요** (The thing is, I don't have time) sounds much more polite than a flat **시간이 없어요** (I don't have time).
