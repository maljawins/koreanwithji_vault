## Lesson Overview
This lesson focuses on expanding sentence-building capabilities by mastering various connectors for nouns and verbs, as well as expressing necessity and ability. The session begins with a detailed review of previous assignments, correcting particle usage with specific verbs like 도와주다 and refining the nuance of negative structures like 밖에 + 안. Ji emphasizes the importance of natural phrasing, such as choosing 고 있다 over ~는 중이다 in certain contexts and using honorific forms like 마세요 for polite commands.

The core of the lesson introduces "and" and "or" connectors. Students learn to distinguish between noun-to-noun connectors (과/와, 하고, 이랑/랑) and verb-to-verb connectors (~고, ~거나). The lesson also covers the versatile uses of 같이 and 처럼 to mean "together" or "like," and explores the "soft ending" nuance of ~고 in spoken Korean. A significant portion is dedicated to the "Any" structures using 아무 combined with (이)나 for positive "any" and 도 for negative "nothing/no one" meanings.

Furthermore, the lesson clarifies the difference between 아니고 and 말고 for negation and replacement. It concludes with the descriptive verb 필요하다 for expressing need and the various ways to express "can" and "can't" (ㄹ/을 수 있다/없다, 못, and 지 못하다). Throughout the lesson, Ji provides cultural and linguistic context, explaining how certain forms shift meaning from simple ability to hypothetical assumptions or emotional reactions.

- **On screen:** Two images: A) Chicken and Beer (치킨과 맥주) and B) Sing and dance (노래하고 춤추다). Text explains that English uses "and" for both, but Korean expresses these differently depending on what is being connected.
- **Ji's teaching:** Now, let’s learn how to say "and" in Korean. In English, we use "and" in a lot of different ways, but in Korean, we have to think about *what* we’re connecting—is it nouns or verbs?

### Slide 8 — Noun AND Noun
- **On screen:** A table lists Formal (과/와), Casual Spoken (하고), and Casual Spoken (이랑/랑). It specifies 받침 rules: 과 and 이랑 follow consonants; 와 and 랑 follow vowels. 하고 works with any noun.
- **Ji's teaching:** In Korean, there are three ways to say "and" when connecting nouns, and they’re all interchangeable. 과/와 is the most formal. You’ll often see it in writing, news articles, or presentations. Use 과 when the noun ends in a consonant (받침) and 와 when it ends in a vowel. For example: 치킨과 맥주 (Chicken and beer) or 남자와 여자 (Men and women). Now, 하고 and 이랑 are mostly used in spoken Korean. 하고 is not 받침-sensitive, like 펜하고 종이 (Pen and paper). 이랑/랑 is 받침-sensitive: 이랑 for consonants like 빵이랑 커피 (Bread and coffee), and 랑 for vowels like 너랑 나 (You and me). If you want to sound natural when speaking, go with 하고 or (이)랑.

### Slide 9 — WITH: 과/와, 하고, 이랑/랑
- **On screen:** Explains that these particles also mean "with." They are often used with adverbs 같이 and 함께 (together). 함께 is formal; 같이 is common.
- **Ji's teaching:** These particles don’t just mean "and"—they also show who you’re doing something with. You can add the adverbs 같이 or 함께, which both mean "together." Again, they are interchangeable, but 함께 is more formal. 같이 is way more common in everyday conversation. You don’t *need* to use them every time, but they help emphasize that you’re doing the action together. Examples include: 엄마랑 같이 시장에 갈 거예요 (I will go to the market together with my mom) and 친구와 함께 영화를 봤습니다 (I watched a movie together with my friend).

### Slide 10 — 같이 & 처럼 = "like"
- **On screen:** Explains that when 같이 is used without a connecting particle, it means "like" or "as if." 같이 and 처럼 are interchangeable. Examples: 천사같이 (like an angel), 배우처럼 (like an actor). A photo of actor Park Bo-gum is shown.
- **Ji's teaching:** 같이 has another meaning: "like." When it's not used with 과/와, 하고, or 이랑/랑, it expresses similarity. You can also use 처럼. They are completely interchangeable, though 같이 is a little more common in speech and 처럼 can sound clearer in writing. Examples: 너는 천사같이 착해 (You’re kind like an angel), 아이같이 웃었어요 (He/She laughed like a child), 그 사람은 배우처럼 잘생겼어요 (He’s handsome like an actor), and 기계처럼 일밖에 안 해요 (He/She only works like a machine).

### Slide 11 — Sentence Connectors & Endings
- **On screen:** Two patterns: 1) Verb stem + Grammar (direct attachment) and 2) Verb stem + 아/어/여 + Grammar (tense harmony). A table shows 하다 and 잡다 examples. It introduces ~고 (and) and ~거나 (or) as connectors that attach directly to the stem.
- **Ji's teaching:** Sentence connectors and endings are made by attaching grammar to the verb stem. There are two main patterns. Some attach directly to the stem, like 하고 or 잡고. Others require the 아/어/여 suffix, like 해서 or 잡아서, based on tense harmony rules. There’s no specific rule to tell you which is which, so you have to learn them individually. We’re starting with the easiest ones: ~고 (and) and ~거나 (or). You don't need to worry about 아/어/여 here; just stick them right onto the verb stem. They aren't 받침-sensitive either.

### Slide 12 — Verb & Verb / ~고(요)
- **On screen:** 그리고 is used at the start of sentences. ~고 is used to connect two verbs within a sentence. Examples: 밥도 먹고 술도 마셨어요 (I ate and also drank alcohol). A photo shows a group at a Korean BBQ.
- **Ji's teaching:** You already know 그리고, right? It’s used at the beginning of a sentence to add a new idea, like "And then" or "Additionally." For example: 어제는 친구를 만났어요. 그리고 오늘은 집에 있었어요. But when you want to connect two verbs in the same sentence naturally, we use the connector ~고. Just add it after the verb stem. We often pair ~고 with the particle 도 (also) to make the sentence flow better. Examples: 영화를 보고 커피를 마셨어요 (I watched a movie and drank coffee) or 책을 읽고 음악도 들었어요 (I read a book and also listened to music).

### Slide 13 — ~고 Ending (Context marker)
- **On screen:** Explains that ending a sentence with ~고 or ~고요 implies something left unsaid. It can feel soft, indirect, or polite. Example #1: 밥부터 먹고요 (Let's eat first and...). Example #2: 운전 조심히 하고 (Drive carefully and...).
- **Ji's teaching:** You can actually end a sentence with ~고 or ~고요. It might feel strange in English, but it's totally natural in Korean. It suggests there's more to be said, but it's being left out on purpose. It can suggest a follow-up or just end a conversation softly. For example, if someone asks to watch a movie and you say 밥부터 먹고요, you're saying "Let's eat first and then (watch the movie)." Or saying goodbye with 운전 조심히 하고 means "Drive carefully and (take care)." Think of it like "...and (you know what I mean)" or "...and stuff."

### Slide 14 — Past Tense Stem + 고
- **On screen:** Explains that it's more natural to conjugate only the final verb in the past tense. 밥을 먹고 커피도 마셨어요 is marked as "Much more natural" than 밥을 먹었고 커피도 마셨어요.
- **Ji's teaching:** In English, you say "I ate and drank coffee," with both in the past. In Korean, it’s more natural to conjugate only the last verb. So, 밥을 먹고 커피도 마셨어요 is the natural way. However, we *do* use the past stem + 고 when we want to emphasize that the first action was completed before a second action in a different tense. For example: 밥은 먹었고, 커피도 마실 거예요 (As for the meal, I ate it, and I’m going to drink coffee). This makes it clear the meal is finished and the coffee is for later.

### Slide 15 — How to make the Past Tense Stem
- **On screen:** A table shows the formula: Verb stem + 았/었/였. It lists regular verbs (보다, 하다, 받다, 먹다) and irregulars (ㅡ, ㅂ, ㄷ, 르, ㅎ, ㅅ, ㄹ).
- **Ji's teaching:** The past tense stem is important because many grammar forms build directly on it. To make it, take the verb stem and add 았, 었, or 였 depending on the vowel. I've included a table here with regular and irregular examples like 돕다 becoming 도와 or 모르다 becoming 몰라. Review these carefully!

### Slide 16 — Expressions Using Past Tense Stem
- **On screen:** Covers assumptions about the past (샀을 거예요 - probably bought it) and hypotheticals (했으면 했을 거예요 - if... would have done it).
- **Ji's teaching:** When you use future endings like ㄹ/을 거예요 or 겠다 with a past tense stem, the meaning changes to guesses or assumptions about the past. 샀을 거예요 means "They probably bought it." 추웠겠어요 means "It must have been cold." We also use this for hypotheticals: 시간이 더 있었으면 했을 거예요 (If I had more time, I would’ve done it). Note that for guesses, we usually use 겠지 instead of 겠어 in natural speech.

### Slide 17 — Future Tense + 고
- **On screen:** Structure: Verb Stem + 을/ㄹ 거고. Shortened from 을/ㄹ 것이고. Example: 내일은 청소하고 빨래도 할 거예요.
- **Ji's teaching:** When connecting two future actions, the full form is 을/ㄹ 것이고, but in everyday speech, we almost always shorten it to 을/ㄹ 거고. Just like with the past tense, we usually only put the future tense on the final verb. For example: 내일은 청소하고 빨래도 할 거예요 (Tomorrow, I will clean and do laundry).

### Slide 18 — Not A and... (아니고 vs 말고)
- **On screen:** 아니고 (from 아니다) is for noun negation/replacement. 말고 (from 말다) is for excluding an option and suggesting an alternative.
- **Ji's teaching:** Both 아니고 and 말고 can translate to "not A, and," but they serve different purposes. 아니고 is used when describing what something is not and replacing it with another noun, like 저는 중국 사람이 아니고 한국 사람이에요 (I’m not Chinese, I’m Korean). 말고 comes from the verb 말다 (to stop/not do). It’s used to exclude something and suggest an alternative, like "except" or "instead." 이거 말고 저거 주세요 means "Not this one, give me that one."

### Slide 19 — 말고 usages
- **On screen:** With nouns: "Not this, but something else." With verbs: Verb stem + 지 말고 (negative command + another action).
- **Ji's teaching:** You can use 말고 with both nouns and verbs. With nouns, it's for alternatives: 이 색 말고 다른 색은 없나요? (Don't you have another color besides this one?). With verbs, it attaches to the negative command form: 지 말고. It tells someone: "Don't do this, do that instead." For example: 자지 말고 빨리 준비해 (Don't sleep and get ready now).

### Slide 20 — 아니고 VS 말고 Summary
- **On screen:** A summary table comparing the two. 아니고: Noun negation (A is not B, but C). 말고: Nouns (Not A, but B instead) and Action Verbs (Don't do A but B instead).

### Slide 21 — Or 이나 / 나
- **On screen:** Used after nouns when the speaker doesn't have a strong preference. 이나 follows consonants; 나 follows vowels.
- **Ji's teaching:** Let's learn "or." For nouns, we use 이나 / 나 when there's no strong preference—either is fine. 오늘이나 내일 만날까요? (Shall we meet today or tomorrow?). It has a casual "or something" feeling. 커피나 녹차를 마셔요 (I drink coffee or green tea, or something like that).

### Slide 22 — 이나/나 without a second option
- **On screen:** Nuance 1: "or something" (커피나 마시고 싶다 - I just want to drink coffee or something). Nuance 2: "at least/just" (이거나 빨리 해 - Just hurry up and do this already).
- **Ji's teaching:** You'll often hear 이나/나 used without a second option. This is totally natural. In 커피나 마시고 싶다, it means "I just want to drink coffee or something"—it's better than nothing. In 이거나 빨리 해, the tone is a bit annoyed or urgent, meaning "At least do this!" or "If nothing else, just do this." It's a way to tell someone to stop hesitating and just focus.

### Slide 23 — Example sentences - 이나 / 나
- **On screen:** List of examples: 노래나 들을래? (Wanna listen to music or something?), 일이나 제대로 해 (At least do your job properly), etc.
- **Ji's teaching:** Here are more examples of that soft, non-picky tone. 노래나 들을래? is great for breaking an awkward silence. 일이나 제대로 해 is a bit more aggressive, like "At least do your job before complaining." 전화나 좀 해 means "At least give me a call or something."

### Slide 24 — Any 아무 ___ 이나 / 나
- **On screen:** 아무 means "any" but is never used alone. It needs 이나 / 나 to mean "anything/whatever." Table: 아무거나 (anything), 아무데나 (anywhere), 아무때나 (anytime), 아무나 (anyone).
- **Ji's teaching:** This is why 나 must be attached to 아무. In Korean, 아무 cannot stand alone. It needs 이나 or 나 to function as a positive "any." It replaces particles like 이/가 or 을/를. 아무거나 is anything, 아무데나 is anywhere, 아무때나 is anytime, and 아무나 is anyone. Remember, 아무데나 replaces 에 (direction), while 아무데서나 replaces 에서 (where an action happens).

### Slide 25 — Nobody, Nowhere, etc. (아무 ___ 도 + Negative)
- **On screen:** Structure: [아무] + [Noun] + [도: even] + [negative verb]. Translates to nothing, no one, nowhere.
- **Ji's teaching:** English has words like "nobody," but Korean uses "not even any." We use 아무 with 도 (even) and a negative verb. 여기에는 아무것도 없어요 literally means "Even anything is not here," or naturally, "There’s nothing here." 아무도 신경을 안 썼어요 means "No one cared." Always remember to use a negative verb with this structure!

### Slide 26 — Any 아무 ___ 나 + Negative = Not just any
- **On screen:** Explains that 아무나 + negative means "Not just anyone" (selective), which is different from 아무도 + negative (no one).
- **Ji's teaching:** This is a very important distinction. 저는 아무나 좋아하지 않아요 does NOT mean "I like nobody." It means "I don't like just anyone—I'm selective." 아무나 + positive means "anyone (whoever)," but 아무나 + negative means "not just anyone." This shows how vital particles are in Korean.

### Slide 27 — Verb Stem + 거나
- **On screen:** 거나 is the verb version of 이나 / 나. It works the same way for actions. No irregularities. Examples: 집에 가거나 쉬고 싶어요 (I want to go home or rest).
- **Ji's teaching:** To say "or" between actions, we use ~거나. It works exactly like 이나/나 but for verbs. It expresses a neutral choice where either option is fine. It’s very easy: just add it to the verb stem. 집에 가거나 쉬고 싶어요 means you haven't decided between going home or resting—both are valid options.

### Slide 28 — 그러거나 말거나
- **On screen:** Translated as "couldn't care less" or "Whatever!" Shows apathy or indifference.
- **Ji's teaching:** This is a very common expression. Literally, it means "whether they do that or not," but the feeling is "I couldn't care less." It’s a perfect example of how ~거나 implies you don't care which one it is. 걔가 울거나 말거나 신경 안 써 means "Whether she cries or not, I don't care. Let her cry."

### Slide 29 — 아니면, 혹은
- **On screen:** 아니면 (If not) is used when the choice matters. 혹은 is the formal version.
- **Ji's teaching:** 아니면 literally means "if not" (아니 + 면). Unlike 이나/나, we use 아니면 when the choice actually matters and you want someone to pick one. 스테이크 먹을래? 아니면 파스타 먹을래? (Do you want steak, or pasta?). 혹은 is the Sino-Korean formal version, mostly used in writing or formal requests like 이메일 혹은 문자 주세요 (Please send me an email or a text message).

### Slide 30 — 또는
- **On screen:** 또는 (또 + 는) means "or" but with a nuance of "and/or." Often used in formal writing.
- **Ji's teaching:** 또는 comes from 또 (again) and 는 (topic marker). It carries an idea of addition, meaning "and/or." It's common in official documents or instructions. 책 또는 영화를 추천해 주세요 means you could recommend a book, a movie, or both. For everyday speech, 아니면 is still the standard.

### Slide 31 — Summary: How to Say "OR"
- **On screen:** A comprehensive summary table of all "or" expressions covered: 이나/나, 거나, 아니면, 혹은, 또는.

### Slide 32 — 필요하다 = To need(?)
- **On screen:** 필요하다 is a descriptive verb meaning "to be necessary." The person in need is marked with 에게/한테 or 은/는. Shortened pronouns: 제게, 내게, 네게.
- **Ji's teaching:** In Korean, there is no action verb for "to need." Instead, we use the descriptive verb 필요하다 (to be necessary). So instead of "I need help," you say "Help is necessary to me": 저에게 도움이 필요해요. 저에게 is often shortened to 제게. A common mistake is using the object marker 을/를—you can't "necessary" something! Use the subject marker 이/가 for the thing that is needed.

### Slide 33 — 필요하다: 에게 VS 은/는
- **On screen:** A visual comparison slide with highlighted blocks (yellow and cyan) intended to reveal the differences in nuance.
- **Ji's teaching:** There’s a slight difference in focus here. 에게/한테 are directional particles. Use them to emphasize *who* the need is directed to: 학생에게 도움이 필요해요 (Help is necessary *for* the student). The topic marker 은/는 softens the sentence and shifts focus to *what* is needed: 학생은 도움이 필요해요 (As for the student, help is needed). It feels more like a casual observation.

### (Script-only Section) — Can & Can't
- **Ji's teaching:** Let's learn "can" and "can't" using Verb Stem + ㄹ/을 수 있다 or 없다. 수 is a noun meaning "ability" or "way." So 수 있다 means "there is a way/ability" and 수 없다 means "there is no way/impossible." 저는 노래를 할 수 있어요 means "I can sing." 수 없다 has a strong nuance that the situation is out of your control. 

    You'll also hear 이럴 수 있어 / 그럴 수 있어 (That can happen/understandable). 그럴 수 있어 is very common for showing empathy. For "can't," we often say 이럴 수가... (No way...) trailing off without the word 없어.

    We can add particles to 수 for more nuance:
    - 할 수가 없어! (Stronger emotion: "There's just no way!")
    - 할 수는 있어요 (Contrast: "I *can* do it, but...")
    - 할 수도 있어요 (Possibility: "I *might* be able to do it.")
    - 할 수밖에 없다 (No choice: "I have no choice but to do it.")

    Two more ways to say "can't" involve 못:
    1. 못 + Verb: Used for action verbs. 얘는 요리를 못해 (She can't cook/isn't good at it).
    2. Verb Stem + 지 못하다: More formal/polite. This one *can* be used with descriptive verbs, like 완벽하지 못해요 (It's not perfect).

    Finally, with 지 못하다, you can add particles:
    - 가지를 못해요 (Physical/emotional block).
    - 가지는 못해요 (Contrast: "I can't go, but I'll do something else").
    - 가지도 못해요 (Even: "I can't even go").

## Teaching Points
- **Noun Connectors:** 과/와 is formal and sensitive to 받침 (consonant/vowel). 하고 is the most versatile casual form. 이랑/랑 is casual and also sensitive to 받침. All three can mean "and" or "with."
- **Similarity:** 같이 and 처럼 are interchangeable for "like." When 같이 is used without a "with" particle, its meaning shifts from "together" to "like."
- **Sentence Connectors:** ~고 (and) and ~거나 (or) attach directly to the verb stem. ~고 can be used to end sentences softly, implying an omitted follow-up.
- **Past Tense Stem:** Formed by Verb Stem + 았/었/였. It is used for sequence emphasis (먹었고... 마실 거예요) and is the base for assumptions (ㄹ/을 거예요) and hypotheticals (했으면).
- **Negation/Replacement:** 아니고 negates a noun to replace it with another. 말고 excludes an option (noun or verb) to suggest an alternative ("instead of").
- **"Any" Structures:** 아무 must be paired with a particle. 아무 + (이)나 creates positive "any" (anyone, anywhere). 아무 + 도 + Negative Verb creates "no one/nothing." 아무나 + Negative Verb means "not just anyone" (selective).
- **Expressing Need:** 필요하다 is a descriptive verb. The object of need takes 이/가, never 을/를. The person needing something takes 에게/한테 (direct focus) or 은/는 (topic focus).
- **Ability:** ㄹ/을 수 있다/없다 refers to ability or possibility. 못 + Verb is a common adverbial "can't." 지 못하다 is a more formal version that can also negate descriptive verbs.
