### Overview
This lesson covers the three primary ways to express the concept of "or" in Korean: ~(이)나 for nouns, ~거나 for verbs and adjectives, and 아니면 for making distinct choices between sentences or items. While English uses the single word "or" for various contexts, Korean grammar distinguishes between situations where the speaker has no strong preference versus situations where a specific choice must be made.

The instructor guides the learner through the conjugation rules for each form, provides contextual nuances—such as why certain forms are not used in the past tense—and explains how these particles interact with other markers like subject and object markers. The lesson concludes with formal alternatives and a practical tip on how to shorten "or" questions in natural conversation.

### Noun + (이)나: "Or Something" with No Preference
The form ~(이)나 is attached to nouns to mean "or." If a noun ends in a consonant, you attach 이나; if it ends in a vowel, you attach just 나. A critical rule for beginners is that ~(이)나 replaces the subject markers (이/가) or object markers (을/를) that would otherwise be on the noun. It can, however, follow other particles like 에 (to), 에서 (at/from), or 에게 (to a person), often replacing them entirely or attaching to them (e.g., 제주도나 instead of 제주도에나).

The nuance of ~(이)나 is that the speaker does not have a strong preference between the options. It implies "this, or that, or maybe something else; it doesn't really matter." Because it involves making a choice or suggestion, it is logically not used in the past tense. You wouldn't say "I saw Star Wars or Lord of the Rings" using this form because the event has already happened and the choice is gone.

When used with two options, it suggests either is fine:
> **그럼 오늘이나 내일 만날까요?** / Well then, shall we meet today or tomorrow?
> **저는 잠이 오면 커피나 녹차를 마셔요.** / When I feel sleepy, I drink coffee or green tea (or something like that).

Interestingly, ~(이)나 can be used with only one noun to mean "or something." This is common when making a suggestion because you have nothing better to do. However, this "single option" usage is restricted to expressing what you *will* do or *intend* to do, rather than just a general want.
> **할 것도 없는데 TV나 볼까요?** / Since there's nothing to do, shall we watch TV or something?
> **제주도나 갈까?** / Shall we go to Jeju Island or somewhere?

### Verb Stem + 거나: "Or" for Actions and Descriptions
To express "or" between actions (verbs) or states (adjectives), the form ~거나 is attached directly to the verb stem. Like the noun form, it conveys that the speaker has no particular preference between the two activities. It is used for future plans or general habits, but not for completed actions in the past.

The instructor provides an example of a weekend routine where the speaker is undecided or indifferent between two options:
> **저는 주말에는 집에서 자거나 할머니 댁에 갈 거예요.** / On the weekend, I'll sleep at home or go to my grandmother's house.

It also works with descriptive verbs (adjectives) to show that multiple conditions are acceptable:
> **버스 정류장에서 조금 멀거나 오래된 집도 괜찮아요.** / Is a house that is a little far from the bus stop or an old house also okay?

---

### 아니면: Making a Definite Choice
When the choice actually matters—such as asking someone to pick between option A or option B—the most common form is 아니면. This literally comes from 아니다 (to not be) and ~면 (if), meaning "if not." While it can be used between two nouns, it is most naturally used to start a second sentence.

In Korean, it sounds more natural to split "or" questions into two separate sentences rather than cramming them into one.
> **지금 갈까? 아니면 나중에 갈까?** / Shall we go now? Or (if not) shall we go later?
> **참치가 좋아? 아니면 연어가 좋아?** / Do you like tuna? Or do you like salmon?
> **점심을 같이 먹을까? 아니면 저녁을 같이 먹을까?** / Shall we eat lunch together? Or shall we eat dinner together?

A useful conversational tip is that 아니면 is often optional in questions. You can simply ask two questions back-to-back, and the "or" relationship is implied:
> **엄마가 좋아? 아빠가 좋아?** / Do you like Mom? Do you like Dad? (Meaning: Do you like Mom or Dad?)

---

### Formal Alternatives and Additional Meanings
The instructor notes that ~(이)나 has other meanings beyond "or," such as "as many as" or "approximately," which are covered in other lessons. For the "or" meaning, there are two formal/written alternatives to 아니면:
- **혹은:** Derived from Hanja, used in very formal or fancy written texts.
- **또는:** Also means "or," used in writing but feels slightly less "fancy" than 혹은.
