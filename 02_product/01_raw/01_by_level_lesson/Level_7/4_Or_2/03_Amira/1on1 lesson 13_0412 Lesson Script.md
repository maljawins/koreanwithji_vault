**AND**

**Alright, now, let’s learn how to say “and” in Korean.**

**In English, we use “and” in a lot of different ways, but in Korean, we have to think about *what* we’re connecting — is it nouns or verbs?**

**Noun AND Noun**

In Korean, there are three ways to say “and” when connecting noun and noun — and they’re all interchangeable. There’s hardly any difference but it’s just that 과/와 .

**is the more formal than the other two. You’ll often see it in writing or formal speech — for example, in news articles or presentations.**

* **Use 과 when the noun ends in a consonant (받침)**
* **Use 와 when it ends in a vowel**

**📍 Examples:**

* **치킨과 맥주 – Chicken and beer**
* **남자와 여자 – Men and women**

**Now, 하고 and 이랑 are mostly used in spoken Korean. But 하고 is not 받침-sensitive.**

**📍 Examples:**

* **펜하고 종이 – Pen and paper**
* **의자하고 책상 – Chair and desk**

**And 이랑 / 랑 is 받침 – sensitive**

* **이랑 is for words ending in a consonant**
* **랑 after a noun ending in a vowel**

**📍 Examples:**

* **빵이랑 커피 – Bread and coffee**
* **너랑 나 – You and me**

**Now, you might be wondering — “Which one should I use?”
☑️ They all mean the same thing, but 과/와 is more formal.
So if you want to sound natural when speaking, go with 하고 or (이)랑.**

**And these particles also mean “with”**

**WITH - 과 / 와, 하고, 이랑 / 랑**

**That’s right. They don’t just mean “and” — they also show who you’re doing something with.**

**And you can add these adverbs 같이 or 함께 which mean “together”, like “together with”.**

**Again, these two words are interchangeable but 함께 is more formal than 같이.**

* **So, 같이 is way more common in everyday conversation.**

**You don’t *need* to use 같이 or 함께 every time — but they help add emphasis that you’re doing the action together with someone.**

✅ Example 1

**엄마랑 같이 시장에 갈 거예요** → I will go to the market together with my mom.

✅ Example 2

**친구와 함께 영화를 봤습니다** → I watched a movie together with my friend.

✅ Example 3

**누구랑 같이 살고 있어? → Who are you living together with?**

**Sentence Connectors & Endings**

Now, let’s talk about how sentence connectors and verb endings are formed in Korean.

You probably noticed this from our past lessons, but the key idea is that:

👉 Both sentence connectors and sentence endings are made by attaching grammar to the verb stem.

And there are 2 main patterns

1. Verb stem + Grammar

Some grammar points attach directly to the verb stem without 아/어/여. Like 하고 and 잡고

2️. Verb stem + 아/어/여 + Grammar

But for some forms, you have to include 아/어/여 suffix like 해서 and 잡아서. This is called tense harmony — based on which vowel is in the verb stem.

We’ll learn these forms later, but just know they exist.

And there’s no specific rule that tells you which forms use 아/어/여” and which ones don’t. So, you’ll just have to learn each form individually.

🔄 And we’re going to start with two of the easiest connectors:

👉 ~고 — which means “and” 👉 ~거나 — which means “or”

✅ You don’t need to worry about 아/어/여 here and stick it right onto the verb stem.
✅ And they’re not 받침 – sensitive either.

**Verb & Verb / ~고**

So, first, you already know 그리고 right? But it’s used at the beginning of a sentence, like when you're adding on a new idea like “And then” or “Additionally”

For example:

* 어제는 친구를 만났어요. 그리고 오늘은 집에 있었어요. → I met a friend yesterday. And as for today, I stayed home.

So, you can use it when you want to emphasize the new action or thought.

But when you want to connect two verbs in the same sentence naturally, we use the connector ~고 instead.

It’s really simple to use it. You just add 고 after the verb stem. That’s it

📝 Example sentences:

1. 밥도 먹고 술도 마셨어요. → I ate and also drank alcohol.
   (밥 + 먹다 = 먹고 / 술 + 마시다 = 마시고)
2. 영화를 보고 커피를 마셨어요. → I watched a movie and drank coffee.
3. 운동도 하고 공부도 했어요. → I worked out and also studied.
4. 친구도 만나고 가족도 봤어요 → I met a friend and also saw my family.
5. 책을 읽고 음악도 들었어요. → I read a book and also listened to music.

💡 Sidenote: Use 도 for “also”

By the way, as you can see, we often pair ~고 with 도. They just go really well together. It just makes your sentence flow and more natural.

**When ~고 is used without the second part**

But you can also END a sentence with **~고** or **~고요**. It feels a little strange doesn’t it? Like and… what?

It’s cause we don’t really do this in English. But it’s totally **natural** in Korean even though it’s technically an incomplete sentence. And usually, it serves **one of two purposes**:

**✅ Purpose #1: Suggesting a Follow-Up**

Let’s look at this conversation:

📌 **Example #1**
**A:** 영화나 볼래요? (*Wanna watch a movie or something?*)
**B:** 밥부터 먹고요 (*Let’s eat first and…*)

The literal meaning is: “Let’s eat first and…” And it ends there. But the implication is “Let’s first eat and THEN watch the movie.”

So, here, you could say that the second part is omitted. because **~고요** **suggests the next action**, without saying it directly.

Let’s look at another example.

📌 **Example #2**
**A:** 안녕히 계세요 (*Goodbye — stay well*)
**B:** 응, 그래. 운전 조심히 하고 (*Yeah, okay. Drive carefully and...*)

Again, **운전 조심히 하고** ends with **~고**, but the rest is left unsaid — maybe something like:
“...and take care.”

This time, **~고** is used as a way to **end the conversation softly and warmly**, leaving the message open-ended.

And you can think of it like: “…and (you know what I mean)” or “and stuff”

**Past Tense Stem + 고**

Now let’s talk about how to connect two actions in the past. In English, you say: “I ate and drank coffee.”, with both verbs in the past tense. But in Korean, it’s more natural to conjugate only the last verb into the past tense.

For example:
❌ 밥을 먹었고 커피도 마셨어요 – sounds awkward
✅ 밥을 먹고 커피도 마셨어요 – natural and fluent
→ “I ate and also drank coffee.”

So you can just leave the first verb in its present form with 고, and conjugate only the final verb.

🔹 When to Use Past Stem + 고

Then when do we use the past tense stem + 고? We use it when:

* We want to emphasize that the first action was completed in the past, and
* The second action is in a different tense (like future tense or present).

Let’s look at an example:

📍 밥은 먹었고, 커피도 마실 거예요.
→ As for the meal, I ate it, and I’m going to drink coffee.

Here, we’re making it very clear:
✅ As for the meal, I’m done with that.
✅ The coffee is going to happen later.

So in this case, we need to use the past stem (먹었고) to show that time shift.

And it’s really not that hard to make a past tense stem.

Just take the verb stem and add 았, 었, or 였 depending on the vowel.

**Expressions Using Past Tense Stem**

Now, here’s some expressions with the past tense stem.

We already learned how to use ㄹ/을 것이다 and 겠다.

But! When you use those same endings with a past tense stem, the meaning changes completely.

Now you're talking about the past — specifically, things that might have been or would have been.

You’re guessing and assuming.

📌 1. Assumption About the Past

This is when something already happened — but you don’t know the full details. You’re trying to imagine what the situation must’ve been like.

Let’s look at a few examples:

샀을 거예요 → *They probably bought it.*

Here, 샀 is the past tense stem of 사다 (to buy), and you attach 을 거예요 to guess what likely happened.

힘들었을 거예요 → *I’m pretty sure it was hard.*

And we learned that past stem with 겠다 means “Must have” remember?

추웠겠어요 → *It must have been cold.* 배고팠겠어요 → *You must’ve been hungry.*

📌 2. Hypothetical Situations

And they can also mean “would have”. These use the past tense stem to imagine things that didn’t happen. You’ll usually see them after a 면 clause, another sentence connector, which means “if”

시간이 더 있었으면 했을 거예요 → *If I had more time, I would’ve done it.*

Here, 했을 거예요 means *would have done it*.

Another example:

전화를 했으면 내가 갔겠지 → *If you had called, I probably would’ve gone.*

In Korean, when we’re making a guess or assumption like this, we usually use 겠지, not 겠어. That’s just the natural way it’s said—so I recommend thinking of 겠지 as a set expression and memorizing it as a whole.

**Future Tense + 고**

When connecting two future actions, we use: **Verb stem + 을/ㄹ 것이고 form but** you’ll almost never hear people actually say it that way in everyday speech.

Instead, they shorten it to 👉 **을/ㄹ 거고**.

Just like in the past tense, most of the time, we **only put the future tense on the final verb**.

Sort of like how you say “will” only once in English.

So 내일은 청소하고 빨래도 할 거예요.

**Not A and…**

Now, 아니고 comes from the verb 아니다 and 말고 comes from 말다 the same verb we use in negative commands. Remember 하지 마세요 Don’t do it?

And both of these might seem like they mean the same thing— "Not A, and" But they’re actually used in very different ways

**🟧 First, 아니고**

So, 아니고 is only used when you’re **describing what something is not**—and you’re **replacing it with another noun.** It’s just a noun-to-noun correction or clarification.

📌 **저는 중국 사람이 아니고 한국 사람이에요**
→ I’m not Chinese, I’m Korean.

"I **am not** Chinese" **and** I'm Korean although in English we say “but” in this case but in Korean we say 아니**고 I am not AND.**

**So, 아니고** is only used when you’re negating **a noun** and replacing it with another noun.

**✅ Now, 말고**

This one comes from the action verb **말다**, which means **to stop** or **to not do something**. We’ve seen 말다 already in negative commands like:

* **가지 마세요** (Don’t go)
* **하지 마** (Don’t do that)

Now when we use **말고**, we’re **excluding** something and suggesting an alternative. Like saying: “except” or **“instead.”**

📌 **이거 말고 저거 주세요**
→ Not this one, give me that one. This is a really common phrase when you’re shopping or ordering something.

📌 **저 말고 다른 사람한테 얘기하지 마세요**
→ Don’t tell anyone **except me** / Don’t talk to someone **other than me**

So 말고 is a bit more about **replacement** than just a neutral correction.

**말고 usages**

And you can use 말고 with both nouns and verbs. We have some more examples here.

**🔸 First — 말고 with nouns**

This is used when you want to say: **“Not this, but something else.”**

📌 **이 색 말고 다른 색은 없나요?** → Don’t you have another color besides this one?

So here, you’re pointing to a color and saying, “**Not this color** — is there another?”
It’s a really natural phrase to use when shopping or picking something out.

🧠 You’re **excluding** the first option and **looking for an alternative.**

**🔸 Second — 말고 with verbs**

When used with verbs, 말고 is attached to the **negative command form**:

**Verb stem + 지 말고**

And what this does is, it tells someone: **Don’t do THIS—do THAT instead.**

📌 **자지 말고 빨리 준비해** → Don’t sleep and get ready now.

Here, you’re saying: “Stop sleeping and go get ready.” The focus is on **replacing one action with another**.

🧠 So essentially, you’re saying “Don’t sleep, and **instead** go get ready.”

**Or 이나 / 나**

Alright, let’s learn how to say **“or”** in Korean. We’re going to start with how to say “or” when you’re connecting **nouns**.

**이나 / 나 – After Nouns**

We use this particle when the speaker **doesn’t have a strong preference**. You’re not picking one over the other. It’s more like, “**This or that, either is fine.**” or “**Something like this.**”

If the noun **ends in a 받침**, you attach **이나 and** If it **ends in a vowel**, you attach **나**

**📌 오늘이나 내일 만날까요?
→ Shall we meet today or tomorrow?**

Here, you’re not saying one is better than the other. It just means “Either day is okay with me.”
It has that casual “or” feeling.

**📌 커피나 녹차를 마셔요.
→ I drink coffee or green tea.**

**Again, it’s not a hard choice — it’s more like: “I drink coffee, or maybe green tea — or something like that.”**

**When 이나/나 is used without a second option**

Because of that soft, non-committal feeling we just talked about, you’ll often hear it used without a second option — and this is totally natural in Korean.

Now, this can be confusing at first because it sounds like “this or…” and then never finished.

But again, it doesn’t need to be finished — the meaning is already understood from the tone and context.

**Let’s look at the first example:**

📌 **커피나 마시고 싶다**
Literally: *I want to drink coffee or…?* But the natural translation is:
**“I just want to drink coffee or something.”**

Now here, you’re **not** actually choosing between coffee and another drink.

It’s more like “I’m tired, I just wanna go get some coffee or something.” You know, nothing fancy, just some coffee.

It feels casual and kind or even somewhat lazy — like, *“coffee’s fine… better than nothing.”*

✅ So the nuance is like *“Not ideal, but better than what I’m doing right now.”*

**Let’s look at the Second example:**

📌 **이거나 빨리 해**
Literally: Do this or... quickly. But the natural meaning is: “Just hurry up and do this already.”

Now, this one’s a little different. There’s no comparison happening here — it just sounds like you’re a bit annoyed or impatient, telling someone:

“Hey, whatever you’re doing — whether it’s complaining or wasting time — just get this done first.”

So in this case, **나** doesn’t mean “or” in the traditional sense. Instead, it feels closer to **“at least”** or **“just”** in English.

🧠 You’re saying:

* “If you’re going to do anything, **at least do this.**”
* “Don’t overthink — **just do this.**”

✅ It’s like saying:
**“If nothing else — this.”** Or **“At least this much.”**

This is a really common tone in Korean when you want someone to **focus**, **stop hesitating**, or **cut to the point.**

**Example sentences - 이나 / 나**

We have some more example sentences here where 이나 / 나 can be used to express that soft, non-picky tone — often meaning “or something”, or “at least…”

**노래나 들을래?**
Imagine a quiet car ride. 🎧 It’s better than awkward silence, right?

🍃 You’re gently suggesting a break — nothing fancy, just to get some fresh air.

**술이나 마실래?**
Imagine you and your friend have no fun plans.

You say: *“술이나 마실래?”*

**Any 아무 \_\_\_ 이나 / 나**

And *this* is why **"나" must be attached to 아무** when you want to say “any” in Korean.

**🎯 "아무" is Never Used On Its Own**

In English, you can say “any” by itself — like *"I’m good with any"* — and people will understand you.

But in Korean, **아무** cannot stand alone in a sentence. It needs to be paired with **이나** or **나** to actually function as a positive “any” because it’s what completes the idea of [anything or whatever]

So it replaces the usual particle that would go on the noun — like **이/가** or **을/를.**

And here’s how you say **anything, anywhere, anytime, and anyone** in Korean:

* **아무거나** – anything
* **아무데나** – anywhere *(using 에)*
* **아무데서나** – anywhere *(using 에서)*
* **아무때나** – anytime
* **아무나** – anyone

**아무데나** is used when **나 replaces 에**, which shows **direction or location**.

**아무데서나** is used when **나 replaces 에서**, which tells you **where an action takes place**.

Remember — **에** marks where something **is or goes**, and **에서** marks **where something happens**.

✅ 아무 옷**이나** 괜찮아요 = Any clothing is fine (Just wear anything)
❌ 아무 옷이 괜찮아요

✅ 아무 노래**나** 틀어= Play any song
❌ 아무 노래를 틀어

✅ 아무데**나** 갈 수 있어 = I can go anywhere
❌ 아무데에 갈 수 있어

✅ 아무데서**나** 만나자 = Let’s meet anywhere
❌ 아무데에서 만나자

So again, **아무** means “any” as an adjective — But it’s **아무 + 이나 / 나** together that complete the idea.

**Nobody, Nowhere, etc.
Any 아무 \_\_\_ 도 + Negative**

Now, English has separate words like **“nobody”** or **“nowhere.” We use “no” like an adjective.**

But Korean doesn’t. So, instead, we express those ideas using the structure **“not even any.”**

To do that, we use 아무 with 도, “even” and use a negative verb to complete the meaning.

Let’s take a look at some examples:

✅ **여기에는 아무것도 없어요**
→ Literally: “Even anything is not here”
→ Natural: *There’s nothing here.*

✅ **주말에 아무데도 가지 않았어요**
→ I didn’t go even anywhere.
→ Natural: I didn’t go anywhere on the weekend.

✅ **아무도 신경을 안 썼어요**
→ Literally: “Even anyone didn’t care” → Natural: *No one cared.*

✅ **아무때도 쉬지 못했어요.**
→ I couldn’t rest at even any time
→ Natural: I didn’t get a chance to rest at all.

🧠 So, remember:

* **도** adds the meaning of “even” **replaces particles** like **이/가**, **에**, **를**
* And it must always be used with a **negative verb**.

**Any 아무 \_\_\_ 나 + Negative = Not just any**

This one’s really important, because **it does NOT mean the same thing as 아무도 + negative.**

Let’s look at the example:

**저는 아무나 좋아하지 않아요** → It **doesn’t** mean “I like nobody.”
You’re saying: *“I don’t like just anyone—I’m selective.”*

🧠 So, if you use 아무나 with a positive sentence, it means anyone like whoever because **아무나** by itself means *“anyone”*

But when you use **아무나 + negative**, it changes the tone: → **“Not just anyone”**
→ sort of like “Not everyone can do this” / “Not everyone qualifies”

**So, this really shows how important particles are in Korean, again.**

**Verb Stem + 거나**

Now let’s talk about how to say **“or”** between **actions** — between **verbs**.

거나 is just the **verb version** of 이나 / 나 — and that is **~거나**.

It works **exactly the same way** — just with **verbs** instead of nouns.

You’re listing two **options**, and you don’t strongly prefer one over the other. It expresses a **neutral** or **soft choice**, like “I might do this, or that.”

And just like 이나/나, it’s not about choosing one over the other. It often means **either is fine** or **whichever works**.

And it’s very easy to use.

It doesn’t matter if the verb ends in a 받침 or not. And there’s no irregular changes. All you have to do is simply add 거나 after the **verb stem**.

Let’s look at some examples.

📌 **집에 가거나 쉬고 싶어요** = I want to go home or rest.
Here, we’re connecting two **possibilities** — either go home or rest. It doesn’t mean you prefer one over the other.

📌 **책을 읽거나 음악을 들어요** = I read books or listen to music.
Same idea — you’re not picking one over the other

📌 **운동을 하거나 집에 있을 거예요** = I’ll either work out or stay home.
⤷ You haven’t decided — both are possible options.

So remember:

* **이나 / 나** is “or” with **nouns**
* **거나** is “or” with **verbs**

**그러거나 말거나**

**And here’s a very common expression that uses 거나.**

**그러거나 말거나.**

**You’ll hear this all the time in Korean, and** literally, it means whether they do that or not. But the actual feeling behind this expression is:

“Couldn’t care less.” “Whatever happens, happens.” “Let them do it. I don’t care.”

This is also a **perfect example** of how **~거나** can imply: "I don’t care which one it is."

It shows lack of interests, **indifference**, or even a bit of **annoyance**.

Examples

**걔가 울거나 말거나 신경 안 써** = Whether she cries or not, I don’t care.
→ “Let her cry. I don’t care.”

**누가 그러거나 말거나 난 갈 거야** = Whether someone does that or not, I’m going.
→ “Whatever they do, I’m still going.”

You can also just say **그러거나 말거나** **by itself**.
It works as a full sentence when the rest is implied — like: “그러거나 말거나~” So, it all comes down to “HOW” you say it, really.

**아니면, 혹은**

Now let’s talk about two ways to say **“or”** when it’s one or the other.

**🔶 아니면 – “If not…”**

**아니면** literally means if Not

* **아니** = to not be
* **면** = if / when

So, **아니면** = *“if not”*

We’ll learn more about this 면 soon — it’s another connector that means “if” or “when.” But just keep in mind that 아니면 is basically if not A, then B?

And it’s used **when there’s an actual decision to make** — you want to pick one or the other.
That’s the key difference from **이나 / 나**.

**스테이크 먹을래? 아니면 파스타 먹을래?**
= Do you want steak, or pasta?

Here, you’re clearly **offering two choices** and expecting the other person to **pick one**.

And, **혹은** means the same thing as 아니면. And **혹** is a **Sino-Korean word.** So, it’s just more formal and mainly used in writing.

**이메일 혹은 문자 주세요.** = Please send me an email or a text message.

Here you have **two choices** — email or text.

They don’t want a phone call, and they’re definitely not expecting an in-person visit.

It’s direct, but polite and formal — which is why **혹은** is perfect here.

**또는**

We also have **또는**, but its nuance is different from **아니면** or **혹은**.
It feels more like an **“and slash or”** type of choice — Both options are possible, not just one or the other.

Literally, **또는** comes from **또**, which means “again” and **는**, the topic marker. So the word itself already carries the idea of addition.

Because of that, **또는** is often used in **written Korean**, **formal speech**, or **explanatory contexts**—like signs, instructions, or official documents.

Let’s look at a few examples:

📌 **책 또는 영화를 추천해 주세요.**
→ Please recommend a book and/or a movie.
You’re not limiting the choice to one. The person could recommend either or both.

📌 **토요일 또는 일요일에 만나요.**
→ Let’s meet on Saturday or Sunday.
Again, you're open to either day, or even both depending on the context.

So, if you’re writing something or trying to sound more precise or logical, **또는** is a great choice.
But for most everyday speech, **아니면** is still the go-to.

**Summary – How to Say "OR" in Korean**

Alright, this slide is here is a summary **on everything we’ve learned** about how to say **“or”** in Korean.

We’ve covered five different ways to express “or,” and each one works a little differently depending on whether you’re connecting **nouns**, **verbs**, or **sentences** — and also depending on your **tone** and **intent**.

So now, whenever you’re unsure which “or” to use, just come back to this chart.

**필요하다**

Let’s talk about how to say **“I need something”** in Korean. In English, **“to need”** is an **action verb**, right? “I need coffee.” “I need help.” But in Korean, there is **no action verb** that directly means “to need.”

Instead, we use this **descriptive verb**:
👉 **필요하다** — which literally means **“to be necessary”** or **“to be needed.”**

So instead of saying “I need help,” you’re actually saying: **“Help is necessary or help is needed.”**

Let me show you what that looks like in Korean:

📌 **저에게 도움이 필요해요.** → Literally: **To me, help is necessary.**
Here, “저에게” or 제게 means “to me”. “도움” is “help” and “필요해요” is “is necessary.”

*By the way,* “저에게” is often shortened to **“제게”.** And these are the only 3 pronouns that can be shortened when combined with 에게. Now 네게 is very very rare in conversations so just stick with 너한테.

Let’s look at another example using the topic marker this time.

📌 **저는 이 약이 필요해요.** → **As for me, this medicine is necessary.**

“저는” “As for me” 이 약이 this medicine 필요해요 is necessary.

And this is a very common mistake. 필요하다 is a descriptive verb, so you can’t use an object marker because you can’t “necessary” something.

**필요하다 – 에게 VS 은/는**

And here’s the **difference between 에게 and 은/는** when used with **필요하다.**

They carry a slightly different **tone and focus**.

**에게 / 한테 / 께** are **directional particles** that mean **“to someone.”**

So, you use these when you want to place more **emphasis on *who* the need is directed to.** It feels a little more **direct** and specific. It’s like saying “it’s necessary **for** him or her”

📍 **학생에게 도움이 필요해요.**
→ **Help is necessary for the student.**

Here, we’re emphasizing that the **student is the one who needs help.**

You can also think of it as: 👉 “It is *to the student* that help is needed.”

**은/는**

The **topic marker** adds a different kind of nuance. Instead of pointing directly at the person, it **sets the person as the topic** of the sentence. That means it slightly **softens** the sentence and shifts the focus more toward **what the person needs.**

📍 **학생은 도움이 필요해요.**
→ **As for the student, help is needed.**

This version feels a little more casual and less forceful — almost like you're simply making an observation or giving context.

**Can & Can’t**

Now let’s learn how to say **“can” and “can’t”** in Korean — using the grammar **Verb Stem + ㄹ/을 수 있다 or 없다**.

**First off — what does 수 mean?**

**수** is actually a noun, and it means **“ability,” “way,” “means,”** or **“possibility.”**
So when you say **수 있다**, you’re literally saying: 👉 “There is a way (to do it)” or I have the ability to do it.

And when you say **수 없다**, you’re saying: 👉 “There’s no way” or “It’s not possible.”

So it could be translated as [be able to] / [not be able to]

**✅ 수 있다 – Can / Be able to**

Let’s take a look at some examples:

📍 **저는 노래를 할 수 있어요.** → I can sing. [I have the ability to sing]

📍 **제 친구는 한국 요리를 할 수 있어요.** → My friend can cook Korean food. *She has the ability to do that.*

**❌ 수 없다 – Can’t / Be unable to**

Now, this one has a **stronger nuance** than a simple “I can’t because I’m busy or I’m doing something else.”

It’s more like: 👉 “It’s impossible.” 👉 “I have no way to do that.”
It implies that **the situation is out of your control** because it literally means “there is no way”,

📍 **그 날 파티에 오래 있을 수 없었어요.** → I couldn’t stay at the party for long that day.
Maybe something came up or maybe you weren’t feeling well — but the point is, **you couldn’t** — it wasn’t an option.

📍 **제 친구는 올 수 없을 거예요.** → My friend won’t be able to come.
Again, it’s not that she just doesn’t *want* to — it’s that she **can’t**, due to some condition or limitation.

So the keyword is 수 which again means “ability” or “way”:

**수 있다** = There’s a way → I *can*

**수 없다** = There isn’t a way → I *can’t* because it’s impossible or out of my hands

**이러다 / 저러다 / 그러다 + ㄹ 수 있다 (없다)**

Now, this is a super common expression. You’ll hear all the time — especially when reacting to situations, either to show **empathy** or **shock**

You already know the concept of **이** **저** and **그.** Now, when you combine these with **-러다**, we get.

* **이러다** → to act like this / or to say so [like this]
* **그러다** → to act like that / or to say so [like that]
* **저러다** → to act like that / or to say so [like that over there]

**By the way these** are **action verbs** — not to be confused with **이렇다 / 그렇다 / 저렇다**, which are **descriptive verbs**.

**And if you add -(으)ㄹ 수 있다, we get 이럴 수 있어 / 저럴 수 있어 / 그럴 수 있어**

These are used to say:

* “That can happen.” “That’s understandable.” “I can see why.” “Yeah, that makes sense.”

🧠 Especially **그럴 수 있어** is **very common** and used a lot in everyday speech when reacting to someone’s situation.

It’s basically a way to say: “I get it. That could happen. I understand where you’re coming from.”

Let’s look at an example:

**A:** 너무 늦게 일어났어. → I woke up too late.

**B:** 그럴 수 있어. → Yeah, that happens. / Understandable.

Now, as for 없다 part, You’ll often hear: 이럴 수가… / 저럴 수가… / 그럴 수가… (Without saying 없어)

These phrases are extremely common in Korean — especially in emotional or dramatic situations — and they typically end with just “수가…”, trailing off.

Technically, this expression comes from: 이럴 수가 없어 = “There’s no way this could happen”
But in real speech, we drop the “없어” because the emotion is already clear from the tone and context.

🧠 Think of it like: “No way…” “How can this be…” “This can’t be happening…”

So remember, 이럴 수가 on its own already delivers the full emotional punch. There’s no need to say 없어

**V.S + ㄹ/을 수(marker) 있다 (없다)**

Now let’s look at how we can add more meaning to the expression (으)ㄹ 수 있다 / 없다, using particles.

Sometimes, we want to emphasize how we can or can’t do something, or show a specific feeling or nuance. So, we can add particles after 수 – like 가, 는, 도, 밖에.

Let’s go over each one.

할 수가 있다 / 없다 This form just adds more emotion.

Like 할 수가 있어 sounds like “Yes, I *can* do this.”

Like in 할 수가 없어! = “There’s just no way” versus “there’s no way”

할 수는 있다 / 없다 는 here shows contrast

So, 할 수**는** 있어요 = “I *can* do it, but…” (Maybe I’m not sure I want to, or it’s not easy, you don’t know why you have do it)

할 수**는** 없어요 = “I can’t do it – *at least not this*.”

할 수도 있다 / 없다. We learned that 도 means “also” and “even”.

So, 할 수도 있어요 means “It’s also possible that I can do it.” Or it can translate to “Might” in English.

할 수도 없어요. “I *can’t even* do this.” This sounds a bit more desperate, like, “I have no way to do even this.”

할 수밖에 없다 This one’s very useful and super natural.

We learned “~밖에 없다” right?

Now, if you think of 수 as “choice” it literally means, “There isn’t any choice *but* to do this.”

So… I have no choice but to do it.

**2 more ways to say “Can’t” using 못**

There’s two more ways to say “can’t” in Korean — and these are *super common* in Korean.

**So, first we have 못 + Verb**

못 is an adverb and it’s placed right before the verb, and it works just like 안, which we’ve already seen for saying “don’t.” **But instead of “don’t,” 못 means “can’t.”**

**And it works with:**

* **Only action verbs — not descriptive verbs**
* **Pure Korean verbs like 가다 (to go), 오다 (to come), 하다 (to do)**
* **3-syllable Sino-Korean 하다 verbs, like 변하다 (to change), 취하다 (to take)**

📝 Example:

나는 못 갈 거야. “I won’t be able to go.”

Super easy to use. Just place 못 before the verb.

Now, 못 can also be used to mean someone is bad at doing something.

📝 Another example:

얘는 요리를 못해. “She can’t cook.”
(It means she doesn’t cook well — she’s not good at it.)

**2. Verb Stem + 지 못하다**

**Now, as for this other one, 지 못하다, this one also means “can’t,” and it’s a little more formal or polite than just using 못. And you CAN use this one with descriptive verb.**

**이거”는” 형용사와 같이 쓸 수 있어요.**

Also super easy to use. Just add 지 못하다 after the verb stem regardless 받침 and that’s it.

**📝 Example:**

나는 가지 못할 거야. “I won’t be able to go.”
Same meaning as 못 갈 거야, but this one has a slightly softer or more formal tone.

**📝 Another example with a descriptive verb:**

완벽하지 못해요. “It’s not perfect.”

This doesn’t just mean “it can’t be perfect,” but really: “I tried to make it perfect — but couldn’t.”
So this form implies that there was an effort.

**할 수 없다 vs 못하다 vs 하지 못하다**

So, there’s three ways to say “can’t” in Korean. They’re used differently depending on context, formality, and nuance.

🔹 First, 할 수 없다.

This word 수, means “way” or “possibility.” So literally, it means: “There is no way to do [something].” So, we use this form when something is **out of your control**.

So, you would say 갈 수 없다 when there’s no time or your car’s nor working, etc

**And this one the only form that’s 받침 sensitive.**

**🔹 못 + Verb**

This is the most common and versatile way to say “can’t” in Korean. 못 works like 안, the negation we already learned.

So, it goes directly in front of the verb and works with action verbs, pure Korean verbs and 3 syllable Sino-Korean verbs.

**This can mean:**

* **You don’t know how / You’re bad at it / Or You can’t do it because of circumstances**

**So, this form is flexible — it covers both skill and situation.**

🔹 3. Verb Stem + 지 못하다

This one is almost the same in meaning as 못 + verb, but it’s a little more formal or polished. But this one works with descriptive verbs, too.

**Verb Stem + 지 (를/는/도) 못하다**

Now, with the 못 + verb, the meaning is straightforward. 못해요 means I can’t do it. And that’s it. You can’t go deeper into exactly how you can’t do it, or how you feel about it.

But when you use **지 못하다**, you can add **particles like 를, 는, or 도**, and this gives you the ability to emphasize or contrast the action in much richer ways.

Like you can emphasize **what** you can’t do, or **how completely** you’re unable to do it.

**🔸 Expression 1: 가지 못해요. This is the standard 지 못하다 form.**

**🔸 Expression 2: 가지를 못해요**

The object particle 를 adds emphasis. You can think of this like “The car just won’t go. It physically can’t move.”

You’re focusing on the **action** of “going” as something that is **physically or emotionally blocked**.

👉 You’re emphasizing the act of going as something that’s just not possible.

**🔸 Expression 3: 가지는 못해요**

This one uses 는 implies contrast. So, this sentence means: “I can’t go… but maybe I can do something else.” Let’s say your family asks if you’re coming home this weekend. You might say:

“이번 주말에 집에 가지는 못해요…” but the sentence isn’t over.

You're hinting you’ll call, or FaceTime, or something. So, 는 softens the rejection and adds contrast.

**🔸 Expression 4: 가지도 못해요**

Now we’re adding 도 — which means “even.”

So this becomes: “I can’t even go.” It’s like saying: I have so much work I can’t even go.

Like I’m helpless. “Forget about it. I can’t even go.”

**🔸 So, 어떻게 하지도 못해요. means “I can’t even do anything.” Like I’m stuck here.**

By the way, 어떻게 means how and in Korean we say 어떻게 하다 like “do how” just like we use the word “thing” like do “something” or “anything”
