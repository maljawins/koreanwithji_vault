## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces the essential Korean grammar for expressing obligation and necessity, equivalent to "have to," "must," "need to," or "gotta" in English. The lesson centers on the structure involving the particle **야**, which carries the literal nuance of "only if." By understanding this core meaning, learners can grasp why the standard forms **~야 되다** and **~야 하다** function the way they do. The video covers basic conjugation, the difference between the two main endings, the use of the adverb **꼭** for emphasis, and various shortened or future-tense variations like **~야겠다** and **~야지**. Finally, the instructor explains how to use this structure in the past tense to express "should have."

---

### Basic Construction: ~야 되다 and ~야 하다

To create the "have to" form, the instructor explains that you first conjugate a verb into its standard **요** form but replace the **요** with **야**. For example, the verb **하다 (to do)** becomes **해야**. This is then followed by the verb **되다** or **하다**. The instructor notes that **되다** literally means "to be acceptable" or "to be okay." Therefore, saying **해야 돼요** literally means "Only if you do it, is it acceptable."

While **~야 되다** and **~야 하다** have the same meaning, the instructor highlights a slight stylistic difference. **~야 되다** is the most common version used in daily conversation. In contrast, **~야 하다** is slightly more formal and is often translated as "must," though it is perfectly fine to use it as "have to" or "need to." Examples provided on screen include:

- **해야 돼요** / I have to do it.
- **지금 나가야 돼요** / I have to leave now.
- **지금 나가야 해요** / I must leave now.

The instructor also demonstrates conjugation with other verbs: **바쁘다 (to be busy)** becomes **바빠야**, and **덥다 (to be hot)** becomes **더워야**.

---

### The Core Meaning of 야: "Only If"

The instructor emphasizes that the particle **야** is the most important part of the lesson because it can be used independently of **되다** or **하다**. Its literal meaning is "only if." When used in the middle of a sentence, it sets up a requirement for the second half of the sentence to occur. This is often paired with "can" or expressions of ability.

> **한국어를 배워야 편하게 살 수 있어요.** / Only if you learn Korean, can you live comfortably.

Other examples show how this "only if" logic applies to descriptions and habits:
> **한국 음식은 매워야 맛있어요.** / Korean food is only good if it's spicy.
> **김치를 먹어야 한국말을 잘해요.** / You speak Korean well only if you eat 김치.

---

### Emphasis and Variations: 꼭, ~야겠다, and ~야지

To emphasize a necessity, the instructor introduces the adverb **꼭**, which means "certainly" or "really." Adding this before the verb strengthens the obligation. For instance, **일을 해야 돼요** means "I have to work," while **일을 꼭 해야 돼요** means "I really have to work."

The lesson then covers several common variations of the "have to" ending:

- **~야겠다**: This combines the obligation marker with the future/supposition marker **겠다**. It functions like "will have to" or "would have to." For example, **머리를 잘라야겠다** means "I'll have to cut my hair."
- **~야지 / ~야죠**: These are shortened versions of **~야 되지** and **~야 되죠**. The instructor notes that **~야지** is very common when giving friendly commands to children, functioning like "You gotta..." or "You should..."
- **~야 될 것 같다**: This is used to soften the statement, meaning "I think I will have to..." as in **내일까지 기다려야 될 것 같아요** (I think you will have to wait until tomorrow).

---

### Past Tense: "Should Have"

To express that something "should have" happened in the past, the instructor explains that you conjugate the main verb into the past tense before adding **~야 됐다**. This literally means "Only if I had done [X], would it have been acceptable." This form is frequently followed by **~는데** to express regret or a contrasting reality.

> **했어야 돼요.** / I should have done it.
> **청소를 했어야 되는데 깜빡 잠이 들어 버렸어.** / I should have cleaned, but I just totally fell asleep.
> **연락을 먼저 했어야 했는데 그러지 못해서 미안했어.** / I should have contacted you first, but I couldn't, so I'm sorry.
