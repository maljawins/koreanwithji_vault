## Teaching Points

### Overview
This lesson focuses on the various ways to express "in order to" or "for the purpose of" in Korean. The instructor, Billy Go, explains that while several forms share a similar English translation, they carry distinct nuances, grammatical requirements, and levels of formality. The lesson is structured to move from basic, versatile forms to more advanced and specific connectors used in formal speech or to express complex intentions.

The learning arc begins with the most common forms derived from the verb 위하다, then transitions into connectors like ~(으)려고 and ~(으)러, which are essential for everyday conversation. The latter half of the lesson covers formal endings like ~고자, emphatic forms like ~게(끔) and ~도록, and specialized connectors that describe intentions that were interrupted or resulted in unexpected outcomes, such as ~(으)려다가 and ~답시고.

---

### The Verb 위하다: 위해(서) and 위한
The verb 위하다 means "to do for the sake of" or "to favor." In practice, it is used in two main forms: 위해(서) and 위한. The form 위해(서) functions like an adverb to describe an action verb, indicating that an action is done "in order to" or "for" someone/something. The "서" is optional. Conversely, 위한 functions as an adjective to describe a noun, meaning "that is for" or "intended for."

To use these with nouns, you attach the object marker (을/를) followed by 위해(서) or 위한. For example, "for a friend" becomes 친구를 위해서 or 친구를 위한. When using them with verbs, you must first turn the verb into a noun by attaching ~기 to the verb stem. Thus, "in order to eat" becomes 먹기 위해서.

> **점심을 먹기 위해서 나갔어요.** / I went out to eat lunch (in order to eat lunch).
> **이것은 엄마를 위한 선물이에요.** / This is a gift for mom (a gift that is for mom).

---

### Everyday Intentions: ~(으)려고 and ~(으)러
The form ~(으)려고 is a versatile connector meaning "intending to" or "in order to." It is used when the speaker has a specific plan or intention in mind. A key grammatical restriction is that ~(으)려고 cannot be used in commands or suggestions because it wouldn't make logical sense to command someone else's internal intention. You attach ~으려고 to stems ending in a consonant and ~려고 to those ending in a vowel.

> **차를 사려고 돈을 모으고 있어요.** / I'm saving up money to buy a car.

The form ~(으)러 also means "in order to," but it is strictly limited to use with movement verbs like 가다 (to go) and 오다 (to come). It describes the purpose of going or coming somewhere. Unlike ~(으)려고, the verb attached to ~(으)러 itself cannot be a movement verb.

> **영화 보러 갈래요?** / Do you want to go to see a movie?

---

### So That: ~게(끔) and ~도록
To express "so that" or "in a way that" a certain result occurs, Korean uses ~게 or ~도록. The form ~게 is common in everyday speech and can be emphasized by adding 끔 to form ~게끔. It is often used to describe helping someone or setting up a situation so a specific outcome is possible.

> **늦지 않게 일찍 잘 거예요.** / I'll go to sleep early so that I won't be late.

The form ~도록 is similar to ~게 but sounds more formal. It is frequently used in official settings or when giving instructions. Beyond "so that," ~도록 has a second meaning of "to the extent that" or "until," often found in idiomatic expressions like 죽도록 (to death/so much I could die).

> **바로 시작할 수 있도록 준비해 주세요.** / Please prepare so that we can start right away.
> **미치도록 보고 싶어요.** / I want to see you so much that I'm going crazy (literally: like crazy).

---

### Formal and Specialized Intentions: ~고자 and ~도록 하다
For formal writing or speeches, ~고자 is used instead of ~(으)려고. It expresses a strong desire or intention to achieve a goal. It is not used in casual conversation.

> **배우가 되고자 할리우드로 왔어요.** / I came to Hollywood desiring to become an actor.

The construction ~도록 하다 is used when making formal announcements or promises to "make sure" something happens. It is a way of stating an intention with a high degree of certainty and formality.

> **오늘 끝내도록 할게요.** / I'll make sure to finish it today.

---

### Resources and Costs: ~는 데(에)
When discussing the time or money required "in order to" do something, the form ~는 데(에) is used. It is almost always paired with verbs like 걸리다 (to take time) or 들다 (to cost money). The 에 is optional.

> **한글을 배우는 데 세 시간이 걸렸어요.** / It took three hours to learn Hangul.
> **이사하는 데에 십만 원이 들었어요.** / It cost 100,000 won to move.

---

### Failed or Unexpected Intentions: ~다는 것이 and ~답시고
Sometimes an intention leads to a different result. The form ~다는 것이 (or ~다는 게) describes a situation where the speaker intended to do one thing but ended up doing something else, usually for a longer duration or to a greater extent than planned.

> **삼십 분만 잔다는 게 세 시간 넘게 잤어요.** / I was only going to sleep for thirty minutes, but I slept over three hours.

The form ~답시고 is used to express sarcasm, disappointment, or modesty. It implies that someone (often the speaker) tried to do something "in the name of" a goal, but the result was poor or disappointing. When used with descriptive verbs, it implies that someone thinks or says something is a certain way, but the reality is disappointing.

> **영어를 공부한답시고 미국에 와서 여행만 했어요.** / I was going to study English (supposedly), but instead I came to America and only traveled.
> **가방이 예쁘답시고 샀는데 무거워서 들지를 못해요.** / I bought the bag thinking it was pretty, but it's so heavy I can't carry it.

---

### Conditions and Interruptions: ~(으)려면 and ~(으)려다가
The connector ~(으)려면 is a combination of "intending" and "if," meaning "if you intend to" or "if you want to." It sets the condition necessary to achieve a goal and is frequently followed by "must" or "need to" (아/어야 하다).

> **제 시간에 도착하려면 지금 나가야 해요.** / If you want to arrive on time, you have to leave now.

The form ~(으)려다가 indicates that an action was interrupted while the person was in the middle of intending to do it. The second action stops the first from being completed.

> **집에 가려다가 돌아왔어요.** / I was going to go home, but (in the middle of that intention) I came back.

---

### About to Do: ~(으)려던 참/차
To say you were "just about to" or "right at the time" you intended to do something, you use ~(으)려던 참. It can be followed by 에 to describe a sudden interruption or by 이다 to simply state your current state of mind. The word 차 can replace 참 for a more formal, written nuance.

> **지금 점심 먹으려던 참인데 같이 먹을래요?** / I'm just about to eat lunch now; do you want to eat together?
> **포기하고 가려던 차에 전화가 왔어요.** / Right when I was going to give up and go, I got a phone call.

---

### Suffixes for Eternity and Extent: ~토록
The suffix ~토록 is a formal or poetic version of 만큼 (as much as) or 까지 (until). It is attached directly to specific nouns to create adverbs describing a long duration or extreme extent. Common examples include 영원토록 (for all eternity) and 종일토록 (all day long).

> **오늘을 영원토록 기억할 거예요.** / I'll remember today for all eternity.
