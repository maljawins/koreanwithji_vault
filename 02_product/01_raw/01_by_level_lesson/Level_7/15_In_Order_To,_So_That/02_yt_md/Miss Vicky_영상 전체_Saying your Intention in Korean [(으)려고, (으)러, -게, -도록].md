## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor provides a comprehensive guide on how to express intention or purpose in Korean using four primary grammatical structures: `-(으)려고`, `-(으)러`, `-게`, and `-도록`. The lesson is designed to move from the most common and versatile forms to more specific structures used for commands, suggestions, or situations where the subject of the intention differs from the subject of the action.

The learning arc begins with the foundational `-(으)려고` and its close relative `-(으)러`, highlighting the specific constraints that make them non-interchangeable in many contexts. The instructor then introduces `-게` and `-도록` as essential alternatives for imperative moods and complex sentences involving multiple subjects. Throughout the video, the instructor emphasizes that while these rules may seem overwhelming to learners, native speakers rely on a "feel" for what sounds natural, which students can develop through consistent practice and exposure.

---

### The Basic Intention Marker: -(으)려고
The most common way to say "in order to" or "with the intention of" is by combining a verb stem with `-(으)려고`. If the verb stem ends in a vowel, `려고` is added; if it ends in a consonant (받침), `으려고` is used. In a sentence, the verb preceding `-(으)려고` represents the intention, while the verb following it represents the action taken to achieve that intention.

The instructor provides several examples to illustrate this structure:
> **한국어를 잘하려고 매일 공부해요.** / I study every day in order to be good at Korean.
> **일찍 일어나려고 일찍 잤어요.** / I slept early in order to wake up early.
> **운동하려고 헬스장에 갈 거예요.** / I will go to the gym in order to work out.

A vocabulary note is included for `헬스장 (gym)`, which is a combination of the Konglish word "health" and the Korean suffix `장 (place)`, literally meaning a "place for health."

---

### Movement-Specific Intention: -(으)러
The structure `-(으)러` also means "in order to," but it is much more restricted than `-(으)려고`. It can only be used when the following action verb is a "moving verb" such as `가다 (to go)`, `오다 (to come)`, or related verbs like `다니다 (to attend/go regularly)`, `돌아오다 (to return)`, and `다녀가다 (to drop by)`.

While `-(으)려고` can be used with almost any verb, including moving verbs, `-(으)러` is strictly tied to movement. For example, while you can say `운동하러 헬스장에 가요 (I go to the gym to work out)`, you cannot say `한국어를 잘하러 매일 공부해요` because `공부하다 (to study)` is not a moving verb.

The instructor highlights specific conjugation rules for `-(으)러`:
- Stem with no 받침: `-러` (e.g., `만나러`)
- Stem with 받침: `-으러` (e.g., `먹으러`)
- Irregular `ㄷ` verbs: `듣다 (to listen)` becomes `들으러`.
- Irregular `ㄹ` verbs: For verbs like `놀다 (to play/hang out)`, the `으` is omitted, resulting in `놀러` rather than `놀으러`.

---

### Friendly Invitations with 놀러 오세요
The instructor explains that the phrase `놀러 오세요` (or the casual `놀러 와`) literally means "come to play/hang out," but it functions as a common, friendly way to invite someone to visit your home, city, or country. When a Korean person says `언제 한국에 놀러 오세요`, they are giving a warm invitation to "come visit Korea someday" rather than literally asking you to come and play.

---

### Constraints on -(으)려고 and Imperative Moods
A critical distinction between `-(으)려고` and `-(으)러` involves the "imperative mood," which includes commands, invitations, and suggestions (e.g., "Please do...", "Let's..."). 

While `-(으)러` can be followed by an imperative verb (as long as it is a moving verb like `오세요`), `-(으)려고` cannot be used in imperative sentences. For example:
- Correct: `음악을 들으러 오세요.` (Please come to listen to music.)
- Incorrect: `음악을 들으려고 오세요.`

Therefore, `-(으)려고` is generally reserved for statement sentences in the present, past, or future tense where the speaker is describing their own or someone else's intentions and actions.

---

### Advanced Intention Markers: -게 and -도록
When the speaker needs to express intention in an imperative sentence that does *not* involve a moving verb, or when the subject of the intention is different from the subject of the action, `-게` and `-도록` are used. These two are largely interchangeable when expressing purpose.

**Case 1: Imperatives with non-moving verbs**
If you want to say "Study hard to get a good grade," you cannot use `-(으)러` (not a moving verb) or `-(으)려고` (imperative mood). Instead, you use `-게` or `-도록`:
> **좋은 성적을 받게 공부해.** / Study so that you get a good grade.
> **좋은 성적을 받도록 공부해.** / Study in order to get a good grade.

**Case 2: Different Subjects**
When the person who has the intention is different from the person performing the action, `-게` and `-도록` are required.
> **모두가 듣도록 크게 말해.** / Speak loudly so that everyone can hear. (Subject of hearing: everyone; Subject of speaking: you)
> **제가 이해하도록 쉽게 써주세요.** / Please write easily so that I can understand. (Subject of understanding: me; Subject of writing: you)

The instructor notes that unlike `-(으)러`, these markers do not require an extra `으` regardless of whether the stem has a 받침 (e.g., `듣도록`, `이해하게`).

---

### Usage Summary and Subjectivity
The instructor concludes by acknowledging the complexity of these rules. She provides a summary of the subjects:
- **저희 / 우리:** Both mean "we/our," but are often used to mean "my" in Korean (e.g., `우리 엄마` for "my mom"). `저희` is the humble/polite version.
- **Subject Agreement:** `-(으)려고` and `-(으)러` usually require the same subject for both the intention and the action. `-게` and `-도록` are used when the subjects differ.

The instructor encourages students not to be frustrated by the nuances, as even native speakers do not consciously think about these grammatical rules; they simply know what "sounds right" through years of usage.
