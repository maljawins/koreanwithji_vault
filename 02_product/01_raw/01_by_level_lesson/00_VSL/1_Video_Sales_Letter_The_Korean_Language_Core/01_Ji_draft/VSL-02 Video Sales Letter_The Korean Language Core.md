From my experience working with the students from all over the world 1 on 1, one of the most common tendencies I noticed is what I call **"the translation trap."**

Let me explain.

You’ve probably heard this many times. “Think in your target language”. But as a beginner, this obviously is impossible. So, what do you do?

You think of something in English, then translate that literally into Korean. Or worse, you throw the entire sentence into a translator.

If you’ve been doing this, please, I urge you to stop immediately because this will 100% hinder you from any progress.

Here’s why:

• **First**, Korean is fundamentally different from English.

It's not just English with different words. It's an entirely different system. Different sentence structure. Different way of expressing ideas. In other words, the way the language functions as a whole is completely different.

Let me give you an example.

When you eat something and you like it, in English, you say "This is good."

And "good" in Korean is "좋다." But if you use 좋다 for food, it just means it’s good *for you*—like, good for your body. Which is not what you meant to say.

The word you're looking for is "맛있다" meaning "delicious."

And this is only ONE of the examples of how a word with the same dictionary meaning has a different usage.

• **Second**, Korean is a highly contextual language.

You probably know this phrase: "사랑해"—"I love you." But technically, it’s just "love."

“나는 - I" and “너를 - you" are actually omitted. But everyone knows it means it is “I” who loves “you” from the context.

• **Third**, many Korean words don't exist in English—and vice versa. Which means, you need to phrase your sentence differently to express the same idea.

This is why the subtitles in Korean shows doesn’t make sense. And this is why you don’t understand the joke. And it’s also the reason why the translator spits out garbage all the time.

So unless you break this translating habit NOW and start working on your **Language Core**, you'll stay stuck as a beginner forever.

**Input, Process, and Output.**

■ Input: a Korean sentence.

We start with a full sentence. Not words. Not phrases. But a FULL sentence.

Why? Because a full sentence gives you **context.**

A world-renowned linguist, Dr. Stephen Krashen famously said **"We acquire language in one way, and only one way: when we understand messages."**

If I speak to you in high-level Korean right now, you’d just hear noise.

But when you have context, that noise turns into a message.

And context is what activates your brain’s ability to recognize patterns. This is what Krashen calls “Comprehensible Input.” And my goal is to make sure every sentence you see is a message you can decipher.

Don’t worry — we start with short, simple ones and build up gradually.

■ Process: STLOHV

Once we have the message, we need to break it down. And we’re going to use what I call the **S-T-P-O-H-V framework which stands for** Subject, Time, Place, Object, How, Verb, based on the universal concept of 5W1H (Who, When, Where, What, Why, How)

And this is the “standard” word order of a Korean sentence.
