**First**, you have to understand that Korean is fundamentally different from English. Not just in word order—but in the way it works as a whole.

It's not just English with different words. It's an entirely different system. Different sentence structure. Different way of expressing ideas.

In English, when you eat something you like, you say "This is good."

And "good" in Korean is "좋다."

But if you use 좋다 for food, it just means the ingredient is good *for you*—like, healthy for your body. NOT what you meant to say.

The word you're actually looking for is "맛있다"—which means "tasty."

And this is just ONE example of how a word with the same dictionary meaning has completely different usage.

Another example: "I flew to New York." Perfectly fine in English. But nobody can actually fly.

So in Korean, we phrase it differently: "비행기를 타고 뉴욕에 갔어요"—"I took a plane to New York."

비행기를 타다 literally means "to board a plane."

• **Second**, Korean is a highly contextual language. You probably know this phrase: "사랑해"—"I love you." But there's no "I" and no "you" here. Technically, it just means "love." We know it means "I love you" from context.

• **Third**, many Korean words don't exist in English—and vice versa. So even if you somehow manage to translate in your head really fast, you'll probably end up saying something awkward. That's why the translator spits out garbage all the time.

**Input, Process, and Output.**

[Show this model in the video]

■ Input: a Korean sentence.

We start with full sentences. Not words. Not phrases.

Why? Because full sentences give you **context.**

This method is called **comprehensible input**—developed by world-renowned linguist Dr. Stephen Krashen.

You need a lot of contextual input to recognize patterns.

And that’s how you stop throwing out random words and phrases and start speaking in full sentences.

Don’t worry — we start with short, simple ones and build up gradually.

■ Process: STLOHV

1) FULL Sentence Breakdown

Let me break down this sentence to show you how it's done:

“나는 지금부터 여기에서 한국어를 열심히 배울 거예요”.

2) Look up each word

From now on, promise yourself this: Never throw the entire sentence into a translator.

What you need to do instead is look up each word. One by one.

So, now, we know what each section means [I, From now on, here, Korean, Diligently, will learn]

And then after that you’re going to…

3) Identify STLOHV

This stands for Subject, Time, Location, Object, How, Verb.

This framework is based on 5W1H (Who, When, Where, What, Why, How), which is a universal concept. This is specifically designed to help you process Korean logically and systematically so you can organize your learnings by clearly identifying each element in a sentence.

This is the core framework of my system. The standard Korean sentence structure. This is essentially the bridge between “I don’t get it” and “I can make my own sentences.”

4) Guess the meaning of the sentence

Now that you know what each word means and you’ve identified the STLOHV, you can guess the meaning of the sentence — like putting together pieces of a puzzle.

5) THEN the next step is to study the grammar and understand how each word comes together to form the big picture.
