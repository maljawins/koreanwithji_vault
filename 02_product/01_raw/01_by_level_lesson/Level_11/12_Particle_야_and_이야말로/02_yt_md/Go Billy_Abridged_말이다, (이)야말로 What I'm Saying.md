### Understanding 말이다 and Restating Speech
The word 말 literally means "words" or "speech," and 이다 means "to be." In English, we often use the word "thing" to refer to speech, such as "The thing I said." However, Korean uses 말 in these instances. For example, 빌리가 한 말은 translates to "The words Billy said" or "What Billy said." When combined as 말이다, the form is used to emphasize something by restating it. It translates roughly to "What I'm saying is," "I'm talking about," or "Are you saying?" in question form.

A common idiomatic phrase using this logic is 내 말이 그 말이야, which literally means "My words are those words." This is used to mean "That's what I'm saying!" or "Exactly!" It is frequently shortened to just 내 말이! to express strong agreement, similar to saying "I know, right?" in English.

---

### Using 말이다 for Emphasis and Flow
The instructor demonstrates that 말이다 can be attached to the end of a complete, grammatically correct sentence to add a layer of emphasis or explanation. For instance, the sentence 조금만 빨랐으면 1등을 했을 텐데 (If I were just a little bit faster, I would have gotten first place) can be ended with 말이에요 to become 조금만 빨랐으면 1등을 했을 텐데 말이에요. This addition signals to the listener that the speaker is clarifying their point or expressing a "you see..." nuance.

While 말이에요 and 말입니다 typically end a sentence, the casual form 말이야 can appear in the middle of a sentence as a filler or focus marker. A famous example is the meme-like expression 나 때는 말이야 (Back in my day...), used by older generations to start a lecture about how things were harder in the past. Another example provided is 내가 처음 회사에 들어갔을 때는 말이야 (When I first started at the company, you see...), which sets the stage for an explanation about a low starting salary.

---

### Sentence-Final Conjugation: ~단 말이다
To use 말이다 at the end of a sentence more formally, it is often combined with the quoting form. This results in the shortened ending ~단 말이다 (or ~단 말이에요 / ~단 말이야). This is a contraction of ~다고 말하는/말한. It is used to summarize or reiterate a point with force. The instructor provides several examples of this usage:

- **이미 제가 다 했단 말이에요.** / I'm saying I already did it all.
- **그건 안 해도 된단 말이에요.** / What I'm saying is you don't have to do that.
- **내 옷이 더 예쁘단 말이야.** / I mean, my clothes are prettier.
- **저도 요즘 썸 타고 있단 말이에요.** / You see, I'm seeing someone these days, too.

If the preceding word is 이다 (to be) or 아니다 (to not be), the form becomes ~(이)란 말이다. The instructor emphasizes that you cannot simply attach 말이다 to a casual verb ending like 했어; you must use the plain form + ㄴ/는/단 construction to bridge the verb and the emphasis marker.

---

### Emphasizing Nouns with (이)야말로
The particle (이)야말로 is used to single out a specific noun as the most important or "the very one" among many possibilities. It attaches as 이야말로 after a consonant and 야말로 after a vowel. It translates to "exactly," "indeed," or "none other than." It confirms the noun and tells the listener to focus on it specifically.

In the example 나야말로 미안해 (I am the one who is sorry), the speaker uses 야말로 to emphasize that despite what the other person might think, they are the one who truly feels sorry. Similarly, 너야말로 means "You are the one (who should be sorry/doing that)." Other examples include:

- **그거야말로 시간 낭비예요.** / That (fighting online) is exactly a waste of time.
- **제대로 되면 그거야말로 최고의 방법이라고 생각해요.** / If it works out, I think that would indeed be the best method.

---

### Military Style: ~지 말입니다
The instructor concludes with the advanced form ~지 말입니다. This structure involves taking a verb stem and attaching 지 말입니다. While it became extremely famous through the drama "Descendants of the Sun" (태양의 후예), it is actually a bit of a stereotype. In the Korean military, soldiers are required to use very formal speech (다/나/까), but ~지 말입니다 allows them to inject a slightly more casual or expressive feeling into their speech without technically breaking the rules of formal politeness.

An example provided is 여름에는 삼계탕이 최고지 말입니다 (In the summer, samgyetang is the best, I'm telling you). However, the instructor notes that this form is not actually used very frequently in real military life and is more of a linguistic quirk associated with the "soldier" persona in media.
