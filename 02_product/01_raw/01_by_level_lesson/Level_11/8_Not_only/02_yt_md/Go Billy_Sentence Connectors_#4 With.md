### The Basic "But": ~지만 and 하지만

The most straightforward way to connect two contrasting sentences is using the sentence connector ~지만. It is attached directly to the verb stem. For example, the two sentences "I like cheese" `저는 치즈를 좋아해요` and "I don't like pizza" `피자는 안 좋아해요` can be combined into `저는 치즈를 좋아하지만 피자는 안 좋아해요`. Unlike the connector ~고 (and), which often stays in the present tense even for past events, ~지만 can and does attach to verb stems in any tense. For the past tense, you conjugate the verb to the past and remove everything after the double 시옷, resulting in forms like 했지만. For the future, it becomes 하겠지만 or 할 것이지만.

Alternatively, 하지만 can be used at the beginning of a new sentence to mean "but" or "however." It signals that the speaker is adding contrasting information to the previous statement. While useful, the instructor cautions against overusing 하지만 in short succession, as it can sound unnatural; in those cases, the sentence connector ~지만 is preferred. 그렇지만 is another standalone option that carries a slightly stronger sense of contrast, often translated as "that being said."

---

### Formal Contrast: ~(으)나 and 그러나

For formal writing and speeches, the connector ~(으)나 is used. It is much less common in daily conversation. To use it, attach ~으나 to a verb stem ending in a consonant (e.g., `먹으나`) or ~나 to a stem ending in a vowel (e.g., `하나`). Examples include `날씨는 맑으나 바람이 셉니다` (The weather is clear, but the wind is strong). The standalone version used at the start of a sentence in formal contexts is 그러나.

---

### The Versatile ~는데 Form

The ~는데 form is extremely common and can mean both "and" and "but." It creates a softer contrast than ~지만. The instructor describes the feeling of this form as "holding up your palm" to introduce a background or a contrasting point. The conjugation depends on the verb type: descriptive verbs take ~(으)ㄴ데 (e.g., `작은데`, `큰데`), while action verbs take ~는데 (e.g., `하는데`). For past tense, it is always ~었는데 (e.g., `했는데`).

Beyond contrast, ~는데 is used to set the stage or provide background information before asking a question or making a request. For example, `질문이 있는데 내일 회의가 몇 시에 시작해요?` (I have a question: what time does tomorrow's meeting start?). In these cases, it functions more like "so" or "and." Adding the polite ending 요 to make ~는데요 allows the speaker to sound extra polite and provides a natural pause in the conversation.

---

### Emphasizing Unexpected Results: ~더니 and ~던데

The connector ~더니 is used when the speaker recalls a past personal experience that led to a surprising or unexpected result. It can translate as "but," "however," or "because... and now." For example, `눈이 하나도 안 오더니 폭설이 내렸어요` (It wasn't snowing at all, and then [unexpectedly] there was a snowstorm). It can also be combined with quoting forms to create ~다더니 or ~라더니, meaning "They said [X], but [unexpectedly Y]."

Similarly, ~던데 combines the past-experience marker ~더 with the background-setting ~데. It is used to add contrast based on something the speaker personally saw or heard. For example, `광고에 많이 나오던데 안 비싸요?` (It appeared in a lot of ads [I saw this], but isn't it expensive?).

---

### Advanced and Literary Forms: ~대도, ~(으)ㅁ에도 불구하고, 건만은, and ~(으)되

For expressing "even though" or "despite," Korean uses ~대도 or the more formal ~대도 불구하고. This emphasizes that the result was surprising disregarding the previous condition. An even more formal version is ~(으)ㅁ에도 불구하고, which attaches to a nominalized verb (e.g., `했음에도 불구하고`).

건만은 (often shortened to 건만) is a literary form used to show that something is unexpected based on a previous fact, similar to "yet" or "although." It is primarily found in old-style written texts.

Finally, the form ~(으)되 has two distinct, highly formal uses. First, it can mean "I accept A, but the requirement is B," translated as "while... make sure to." For example, `달리기를 하되 가볍게 뛰세요` (Run, but run lightly). Second, in religious or historical contexts, it is used to introduce a quote from an authority figure, such as `왕께서 명하시되` (The king commanded...).

So `하다` would become `한다던데`. `잘 쉬어야 낫는다던데 걱정이네요.` They said you have to rest well in order to recover, but I'm worried. `다들 그 신발을 신는다던데 저만 없어요.` I heard everyone wears those shoes, but only I don't have them. `철수 친구라던데 저는 처음 봐요.` I heard they're 철수's friend, but it's the first time I'm seeing them. `유명한 노래라던데 들어봤어요?` I heard it's a famous song, but have you heard it?

~건만은 is used to add contrast and to show that something is unexpected. This unexpected feeling comes from something being based on something else. It works in a similar way as the form ~지만. You can translate ~건만은 as but, however, yet, or although. For example, A ~건만은 B can mean "based on A, B was something unexpected," or "A, however, what I didn't expect was B." ~건만은 is only used in old-style written texts. It's not commonly used. This can also be shortened to ~건만 in speech. To use it, attach ~건만은 directly after a verb stem in the present tense or the past tense. For example, `하다` could just become `하건만`.

`수영 연습을 매일 하건만 끝까지 가본 적이 없어요.` I practice swimming every day, but I've never gone to the other end. `틈틈이 거울을 닦건만 볼 때마다 더러워요.` I clean my mirror whenever I have spare time, but whenever I look, it's dirty. `엄마가 원하면 집도 사드리겠건만은 이사할 생각이 없대요.` If my mom wants, I would even buy her a house, but she says she isn't thinking of moving.

The particle ~만, meaning only, can also be added to the ~니다 form, so `합니다` could become `합니다만`. This has the same meaning as using the ~지만 form, but sounds extra formal. This is less common. `이런 사례도 있습니다만 아주 드뭅니다.` There are instances like this, but it's very rare. `이렇게 합니다만 추천하지 않습니다.` This is how it's done, but I don't recommend it. `여기서도 해줍니다만 전문가가 아니라서 완벽하지 않습니다.` They do it here too, but they're not experts, so it's not perfect.

The form ~(으)되 means that the speaker accepts or approves of something, but is adding a limit or requirement after. For example, A ~(으)되 B means "I accept A, but the requirement is B." More naturally, you can translate this as "while I," "I only," or "while you can do this, however, make sure to." You can also think of ~(으)되 as being similar to the form ~지만. ~(으)되 is only used in extra formal and fancy speaking or writing. It is not commonly used. It is especially not used in everyday speech. To use it, you can attach ~되 directly after a verb stem, so `하다` would become `하되`. However, if the verb stem ends with a double ㅅ or ㅂㅅ, attach ~으되, so `했다` would become `했으되`. Note that the verb `이다` becomes `이로되` after a consonant and `로되` after a vowel. `아니다` becomes `아니로되`.

`달리기를 하되 가볍게 뛰세요.` Run, but run lightly. `세 끼를 다 먹되 조금씩 먹었어요.` I ate three meals, but I only ate a little of each. `산을 갔으되 등산은 하지 않았어요.` I went to the mountain, but I did not hike. The form ~(으)되 also has a second usage when introducing a quote. This is only when ~(으)되 is used with a verb that means to say or to reply, such as `가로다` meaning `말하다`, `이르다` or `말씀하시다` among others. For example, A ~(으)되 B means "A says B." This usage is only used in old-style, formal and or religious speeches. It's not commonly used. `왕께서 명하시되 가뭄에 대비하라.` The king commanded, "Prepare for a drought." `예언자가 이르시되 자녀를 사랑으로 대하라.` The prophet said, "Treat your children with love."
