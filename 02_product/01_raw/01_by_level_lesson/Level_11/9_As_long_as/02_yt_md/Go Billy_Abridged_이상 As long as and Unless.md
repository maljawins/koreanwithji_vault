## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces the multifaceted word 이상, which most learners initially encounter as a noun meaning "above" or "more than." However, the core of this lesson focuses on its function as a grammar marker used with verbs. When attached to a verb conjugated into an adjective form, 이상 functions as a logical connector that translates to "as long as," "since," "now that," or "unless."

The underlying logic of the 이상 grammar form is to show that because a certain condition (A) exists, a specific result (B) is natural, expected, or a matter of course. The instructor breaks down the usage into three categories: its basic noun meanings, its use as a conversational closer, and its primary role as a conditional grammar structure with both positive and negative verbs.

---

### Basic Meanings: Above, Below, and "That's All"

Before diving into complex grammar, the instructor clarifies common uses of 이상 as a noun and adverb. First, he distinguishes it from the descriptive verb 이상하다 (to be strange/weird). He then introduces the pair 이상 (above/more than) and 이하 (below/less than). These terms are inclusive of the number mentioned. For example, 세 개 이상 means "three or more," and 사과 세 개 이상 먹어야 돼 means "You must eat at least three apples." Similarly, 삼십 불 이하면 더 좋아요 means "It's better if it's $30 or less."

The adverbial form 더 이상 means "any more" or "any longer" and is strictly used with negative sentences. Examples include 더 이상 물어보지 마세요 (Don't ask any more) and 철수는 더 이상 친구가 없어요 (Chul-soo doesn't have friends any more). Finally, when combined with the verb 이다, it becomes a formal way to end a report or speech. 이상입니다 or 이상이었습니다 translates to "That is all" or "I have finished reporting," often heard from news reporters or in legal contexts.

---

### The Grammar Form: [Verb Adjective Form] + 이상

The main grammar point involves attaching 이상 to a verb that has been turned into an adjective. This structure indicates that "Because of A, of course B." The conjugation rules depend on the verb type and tense:

- **Action Verbs:** Use the present tense adjective form (~는) or the past tense adjective form (~은/ㄴ). For example, 먹다 becomes 먹는 (present) or 먹은 (past). The future tense is not used with this form.
- **Descriptive Verbs:** Use the standard adjective form (~은/ㄴ). For example, 바쁘다 becomes 바쁜.

The instructor notes that the translation varies based on whether the verb is positive or negative, but the underlying "expected result" logic remains the same.

---

### Using 이상 with Negative Verbs ("Unless")

When used with a negative verb (like ~지 않은 or 아닌), the most natural English translation is often "unless" or "as long as... not." The instructor demonstrates this with several examples:

> **날아갈 수 없는 이상 제 시간에 도착할 수 없을 것 같아요.** / Unless you can fly, I don't think you're going to arrive on time.

Literally, this means "Because you cannot fly, of course, it's expected you won't arrive on time." Other examples include:
- **철수가 아닌 이상 빌리의 예문에서 사용되지 못해요.** / Unless you are Chul-soo, you cannot be used in Billy's example sentences.
- **너무 바쁘지 않은 이상 친구랑 놀아줘.** / Unless you're too busy, please play with your friend.
- **바보가 아닌 이상 이 숙제를 할 수 있어요.** / As long as you're not a fool, you can do this homework.

---

### Using 이상 with Positive Verbs ("Since" / "Now that")

With positive verbs, the meaning shifts toward "since," "now that," or "as long as." This indicates that because a certain action has already happened or a state exists, the following result is inevitable.

> **이미 시작한 이상 끝날 때까지 쉬지 못해요.** / Since it has already started, I cannot rest until it's finished.

The instructor provides further examples to illustrate this "point of no return" or "given condition" logic:
- **빌리의 책을 산 이상 매일 한국어를 공부해야 돼요.** / Now that you've bought Billy's book, you have to study Korean every day.
- **내가 이 팀에 있는 이상 우리는 꼭 1등을 해야만 해.** / As long as I am on this team, we must get first place.
