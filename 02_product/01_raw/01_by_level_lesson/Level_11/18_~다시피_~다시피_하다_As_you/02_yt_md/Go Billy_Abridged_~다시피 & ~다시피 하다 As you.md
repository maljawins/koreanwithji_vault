### Basic Usage: "As You..."
To create this form, you take an action verb stem and attach ~다시피 directly to it. For example, the verb 하다 (to do) becomes 하다시피. This form translates to "as," "like," or "in the way of" the verb. While it can theoretically be used with many verbs, it is most commonly paired with a specific set of sensory and communication verbs: 보다 (to see), 말하다 (to say), 듣다 (to hear), 읽다 (to read), and 알다 (to know).

The instructor emphasizes that this form can be used in both the present and past tense. In the present tense, you use the basic stem, such as 보다시피 (as you see) or 알다시피 (as you know). In the past tense, you attach it to the past tense stem, such as 봤다시피 (as you saw) or 들었다시피 (as you heard). Furthermore, honorifics can be integrated by using the ~(으)시다 stem. For instance, instead of the plain 알다시피, one should use 아시다시피 (as you know) when speaking politely to others. Similarly, 말하다 (to say) can become the highly polite 말씀하셨다시피 (as [someone] said).

Example sentences demonstrate these uses:
> **알다시피, 아이스크림은 우유로 만들어요.** / As you know, ice cream is made with milk.
> **보시다시피, 이 집은 이미 팔렸어요.** / As you see, this house has already been sold.
> **들었다시피, 내일은 시험이 있어요.** / As you heard, there is a test tomorrow.
> **엄마가 말했다시피, 주말엔 다 같이 청소를 할 거야.** / As mom said, we're all going to do cleaning together on the weekend.

### Idiomatic Usage: ~다시피 하다 ("Almost" or "Practically")
When the verb 하다 follows ~다시피, the meaning shifts to "almost doing" or "practically doing" something. This indicates that while the action didn't literally happen in its full sense, it was so close that it might as well have. Unlike the first usage, this construction typically uses only the present tense verb stem before ~다시피, even if the final 하다 is conjugated into the past tense.

The instructor explains the logic: saying "I did [Action B] in the way of [Action A]" implies that Action B was very similar to Action A. For example, "I did [something] in the way of studying every day" means you practically studied every day. This form is often used with adverbs like 거의 (almost/nearly) or 겨우 (barely) for added emphasis.

Example sentences for this usage:
> **작년에는 한동안 한국어를 매일 공부하다시피 했어요.** / Last year, for a while, I practically studied Korean every day.
> **요즘은 도서관에서 살다시피 하고 있어요.** / These days, I'm practically living at the library.
> **방이 너무 좁아서 앉아서 자다시피 했어요.** / Because the room was so narrow, I practically slept sitting up.

---

### ~다시피 as an Adverbial Expression
The instructor concludes by showing how ~다시피 can function as an independent adverbial phrase without being tied to a final 하다. A common idiomatic expression is 밥 먹다시피 (as if eating a meal), which is used to describe an action that happens as frequently and naturally as eating. For example, saying someone is late "as if eating a meal" means they are late very frequently.

Additionally, it can describe the manner of an action. The instructor provides the example of "collapsing into bed." By using 쓰러지다시피 (as if collapsing), the speaker describes the intensity or manner of lying down. In these cases, a hidden 하여 (the written/formal version of 해서) is often implied but dropped, turning the verb into a descriptive adverb for the following clause.

Example sentences:
> **철수는 지각을 밥 먹다시피 해요.** / Chul-su is late as often as he eats (very frequently).
> **쓰러지다시피 침대에 누웠어요.** / I lied down in bed as if I collapsed (I collapsed into bed).
