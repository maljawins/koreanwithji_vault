## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces the grammar form ~는/은 김에, which is frequently translated as "while," "since," or "now that." However, to use it accurately, one must understand the literal meaning of the noun 김, which refers to a "chance" or an "opportunity." The core concept of this structure is taking advantage of an existing situation or action (Action A) to perform a second, related action (Action B). It is not merely about two things happening simultaneously; it specifically implies that because you are already doing one thing, you are seizing that opportunity to do something else as well.

---

### Construction and Literal Meaning

The form is created by attaching a descriptive ending to an action verb stem followed by the noun 김 and the particle 에. For the present tense, the ending ~는 is used. For the past tense, the ending ~은 is attached after a consonant, and ~ㄴ is attached after a vowel. The instructor writes the following paradigm on the whiteboard:

- **Present Tense:** Action Verb Stem + ~는 김에
- **Past Tense:** Action Verb Stem + ~은/ㄴ 김에

The word 김 itself means a chance or opportunity to do something. Therefore, the literal translation of the structure is "in the opportunity of doing [Action A]." The instructor emphasizes that thinking of it as "taking the opportunity" rather than just "while" prevents learners from misusing it in contexts where no specific opportunity is being exploited.

---

### Conceptual Logic: Action A and Action B

The instructor visually diagrams the logic on the board using "A" and "B" circles. While performing Action A, you take the opportunity to do Action B. This can be viewed as doing one action "on top of" another. The relationship between the two actions is key: Action B is often something convenient to do because Action A is already happening.

Common English equivalents include:
- "While doing A, I took the opportunity to do B."
- "Since I'm doing A anyway, I might as well do B."
- "Now that I've done A, I'll do B."

---

### Usage in the Past Tense

When the first action is completed, the past tense form (~은/ㄴ 김에) is used to show that the resulting state provides the opportunity for the next action. The instructor provides an example of going to the beach:

> **바다에 간 김에 생선도 먹고 서핑도 하고 왔어요.** / Since I went to the ocean, I (took the opportunity to) ate fish and did some surfing (and came back).

In this sentence, the act of going to the beach (Action A) is finished, and being there provided the "chance" to eat fish and surf (Action B). Another example involves travel:

> **한국에 온 김에 친구 동생의 결혼식에 다녀왔어요.** / Since I came to Korea, I went to my friend's younger sibling's wedding.

Here, the "chance" created by having traveled to Korea was used to attend a wedding. A final past tense example demonstrates a suggestion based on someone's current location:

> **여기까지 온 김에 새로 오신 선생님도 한번 만나 보고 가세요.** / Since you're here anyway, meet the new teacher before you go.

The instructor notes that 새로 오다 (to newly arrive) is the natural way to describe a "new" person, such as a teacher, rather than using adjectives like 새로운 which are reserved for objects.

---

### Usage in the Present Tense

When the first action is currently in progress or is the general context of the moment, the present tense form (~는 김에) is used. The instructor demonstrates this with household chores:

> **제 방을 청소하는 김에 화장실도 청소했어요.** / While cleaning my room, I also cleaned the bathroom.

The logic here is that the "opportunity" of being in a cleaning mode or having the cleaning supplies out for the bedroom was used to finish the bathroom as well. This also works for requests:

> **설거지 하는 김에 그 옆에 쓰레기도 버려주세요.** / While you're doing the dishes, please also throw away the trash next to it.

Since the person is already at the sink or cleaning up, the speaker asks them to take that opportunity to handle the trash. Other examples include:

> **떡볶이 시키는 김에 김밥도 시키자.** / While we're ordering tteokbokki anyway, let's also order kimbap.

> **문법에 대해서 얘기하는 김에 억양에 대해서도 얘기해 볼까요?** / While we're talking about grammar, should we also take the opportunity to talk about intonation?
