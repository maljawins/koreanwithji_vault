## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor explains the usage of two versatile Korean grammar forms: 테니까 and 텐데. To master these, one must first understand their constituent parts. Both forms are built upon the element 테, which historically originates from 터, meaning a "will," "plan," or "intention" to do something. This core meaning of intent or future-oriented assumption is then combined with either 니까 (because) or 데 (contrast/background).

The lesson arc begins by reviewing the base forms 니까 and 데, then moves into the construction and nuanced meanings of 테니까 and 텐데. The instructor emphasizes that these forms are primarily used for making assumptions about the future or the past, providing a softer, more natural tone to Korean sentences compared to direct future tense markers.

---

### Foundation: Reviewing 니까 and 데

Before diving into the combined forms, the instructor reviews the base endings. The ending 니까 (or 은니까 after a consonant) means "because." For example, 바쁘다 (to be busy) becomes 바쁘니까, and 먹다 (to eat) becomes 먹으니까. A key restriction noted is that 니까 cannot be used with emotional adjectives like "happy," "sad," or "sorry" to explain a cause.

The ending 데 serves to connect two sentences by showing contrast or providing background information. It can be translated as "and," "but," or "so." Visually, the instructor describes it as presenting one sentence in each hand to show they are connected. Beyond contrast, 데 is used to make a sentence softer. Instead of a blunt "I don't have time" (시간 없어요), saying 시간이 없는데요 makes the rejection feel less harsh. It is also used for introducing a new subject or explaining a situation, functioning like "Well, you see..."

---

### Using 테니까 for Assumptions and Intent

The form 테니까 is created by taking a verb stem, attaching 을 (after a consonant) or ㄹ (after a vowel), and adding 테니까. This form combines the "assumption/will" of 테 with the "because" of 니까. It is used when the speaker is making an assumption or expressing a strong intention.

When used for assumptions, it implies "because I assume/think that..." For example, 많이 추울 테니까 조심하세요 means "Be careful because (I assume) it will be very cold." When used with the first person, it expresses the speaker's intention: 제가 할 테니까 걱정하지 마세요 means "Don't worry because I will do it." The instructor notes that in these cases, the second half of the sentence (like "don't worry") is so strongly implied that Koreans often omit it and end the sentence with 테니까요.

---

### Using 텐데 for Contrast and Softening

The form 텐데 is a combination of 테 and the contrastive/softening ending 데. Like its counterpart, it is used for assumptions about the future or past, but instead of showing a cause, it shows contrast or provides background. It can be translated as "I assume... but/and/so..."

An example of contrast is 내일은 공부해야 할 텐데 오늘 만날까요?, which translates to "I assume I'll have to study tomorrow, (so) shall we meet today?" Here, the speaker presents the background assumption (studying tomorrow) to contrast it with the suggestion (meeting today). Like the base 데 form, 텐데 makes sentences sound softer and more natural. Ending a sentence with 텐데요 adds a feeling of "I'm pretty sure that..." or "I assume that..." rather than stating a cold fact.

---

### Past Tense and Hypotheticals

Both forms can be used in the past tense by attaching 을 테니까 or 을 텐데 to a past tense verb stem (ending in 었/았/였). For example, 철수가 이미 했을 텐데 means "I assume Chul-su already did it." 

A more advanced usage involves the double past tense (었었) combined with 텐데 to create a "would have" meaning. This is frequently paired with the hypothetical "if" form 만약 ... (았/었)다면. The instructor provides the example: 만약 시간이 있었다면 갔을 텐데, meaning "If I had had time, I would have gone." This structure expresses a hypothetical assumption about a past that did not happen.

---

### Nuances and Disagreements

The instructor highlights specific pragmatic uses for these forms. While 테니까 is often used for reassuring someone ("I'll do it, so don't worry"), 텐데 is frequently used to point out a bad idea or express disagreement. By stating an assumption as background, the speaker prompts the listener to reconsider.

Examples of this "warning" nuance include:
- **비가 올 텐데** / (I assume) it's going to rain, though (Are you sure you want to go out?)
- **매울 텐데** / (I assume) it's going to be spicy, though (Are you sure you can eat it?)
- **무거울 텐데** / (I assume) it's going to be heavy, though (Are you sure you want to carry it?)

Finally, a restriction is noted: while you can use 테니까 with yourself to show intent, using 텐데 with yourself (e.g., 제가 할 텐데) can be awkward without specific context, because it literally means "I am assuming that I am going to do it," which sounds uncertain about one's own actions.
