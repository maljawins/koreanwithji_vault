### Overview
This lesson focuses on Korean sentence connectors used to express thoughts, worries, realizations, and assumptions. The instructor, Billy Go, categorizes these forms into three main functional groups: thinking/worrying, realizing something after an action or state, and making future-oriented assumptions. The lesson covers both intermediate and advanced grammar patterns, providing detailed conjugation rules and contextual nuances for each.

The learning arc begins with the concept of "thinking" through the lens of worrying about potential outcomes. It then transitions into a large section on "realizing," which explores how different connectors like `보니까` change meaning depending on whether they follow a completed action, an ongoing action, or a descriptive state. Finally, the lesson concludes with advanced connectors used for expressing disbelief and making assumptions about the future.

### Thinking and Worrying: 을까 봐(서)
The form `을까 봐(서)` is used to explain the reason for an action based on a thought or worry. It combines the `을까` form (used for wondering or suggestions) with `봐서` (from `보다`, to see/think). When used as a connector, it translates to "because I was thinking/worrying that..." and is similar in function to `것 같아서`.

To conjugate, attach `을까 봐` to verb stems ending in a consonant and `ㄹ까 봐` to stems ending in a vowel. The final `서` is optional and often omitted in speech. The instructor provides several examples of this "reason-based worrying":
> **아침에 못 일어날까 봐 걱정돼요.** / I'm getting worried that I won't be able to wake up in the morning.
> **음식이 부족할까 봐 많이 샀어요.** / I bought a lot because I was worried there wouldn't be enough food.

A shortened version, `을까`, can also be used specifically when followed immediately by verbs meaning "to worry" or "to be scared," such as `걱정이다` or `무서워요`.
> **사고가 날까 무서워요.** / I'm scared because there might be an accident.

---

### Realizing After Trying: 어/아 보니까
This form combines the "try doing" pattern (`어/아 보다`) with the "because/since" connector (`으니까`). It expresses that the speaker realized something new after trying an action for the first time. It translates as "now that I've tried, I realized..."

The conjugation involves taking an action verb, adding the appropriate `어/아` suffix, and then adding `보니까`. The final `까` can be removed for a slightly more formal tone.
> **실제로 써보니까 생각보다 더 좋아요.** / After actually trying it, it's better than I thought.
> **직접 해보니까 어때요?** / How is it after trying it for yourself?

---

### Realizing After Finishing: 고 보니까
While `어/아 보니까` focuses on the act of trying, `고 보니까` focuses on the completion of an action. It uses the `고` (and then) connector to show that a realization occurred simply after finishing something. It is similar to `고 나니까` but carries a stronger nuance of reflection or realization.
> **부모가 되고 보니까 배울 게 참 많네요.** / After becoming a parent, I realized there's really a lot to learn.
> **시간이 지나고 보니 별거 아니었어요.** / After time had passed, I realized it wasn't a big deal.

---

### Realizing While Doing: 다(가) 보니까
This pattern uses the `다가` (while) connector to indicate that a realization happened while the speaker was in the middle of a repetitive or continuous action. It translates to "after having done [A] for a while, I realized [B]."
> **계속 연습하다 보니 익숙해졌어요.** / After I kept practicing, I got used to it.
> **살다 보니까 조금만 더워도 힘들어요.** / After having lived [in a cold place], it's difficult for me even if it's only a little bit hot.

---

### Realizing Based on a State: 다 보니까
Though it looks identical to the previous form, `다 보니까` used with descriptive verbs is a separate grammar point. It uses the plain form of the adjective to mean "seeing as [A] is the case, I realized [B]." It explains a result based on an inherent quality or state.
> **가격이 너무 비싸다 보니까 아무도 안 사요.** / Seeing as the price is too expensive, nobody is buying it.
> **눈이 안 좋다 보니까 안경이 꼭 필요해요.** / Seeing as my eyes are bad, I surely need glasses.

---

### Predicting Results: 다가 보면
This form combines `다가` (while) and `보면` (if you look/see). It is used to state that if an action continues to happen, a certain result will inevitably follow. Unlike `다가 는` (which usually implies a negative result), `다가 보면` is neutral or positive.
> **살다 보면 익숙해질 거예요.** / Once you live there, you'll get used to it.
> **달리다 보면 어느 순간 달리는 게 쉬워져요.** / If you keep running, eventually running will become easy.

---

### Expressing Disbelief: 다니 and 니
The `다니` form expresses the speaker's reaction—such as surprise, happiness, or doubt—to a fact or situation. It translates to "I can't believe..." or "To think that..."
> **아직도 열두시라니 믿을 수가 없어요.** / To think it's still twelve o'clock, I can't believe it.
> **김밥이 만 원이라니 정말 비싸네요.** / To think kimbap is ten thousand won, that's really expensive.

When reacting to something someone else said (quoted information), the form becomes `니` after the quoting form. This can be shortened depending on the type of quote: `냐니` (questions), `으라니` (commands), or `자니` (suggestions).
> **제 유튜브로만 한글을 배웠다니 뿌듯하네요.** / I feel good to hear that you learned Hangeul only with my YouTube.
> **이런 날 밖에 나가자니 제정신이에요?** / I can't believe you're saying let's go outside on a day like this. Are you out of your mind?

---

### Out of the Ordinary: 치고(는)
Used after a noun, `치고(는)` indicates that something is unusual or exceptional given the category it belongs to. It translates to "for a [Noun]..." or "considering it's a [Noun]..."
> **다섯 살 치고는 말을 참 잘하네요.** / They speak really well for a five-year-old.
> **중고차 치고는 상태가 좋아 보여요.** / The condition looks good for a used car.

The verb `치다` can also be used independently in quoting forms like `다고 치다` to mean "let's assume" or "even if we say."
> **연습한다고 치고 한 번 더 해보세요.** / Say it's practicing and try it once more.

---

### Future Assumptions: 테니까 and 텐데
Both forms are used to make assumptions about the future, but they function as different types of connectors.

`을/ㄹ 테니까` expresses a reason based on an assumption or a strong intention. It is often followed by a command or suggestion.
> **설거지는 제가 할 테니, 청소기를 돌려주세요.** / I'll do the dishes (intention), so please vacuum.
> **다 알 테니까 설명하지 않을게요.** / You probably know everything (assumption), so I won't explain it.

`을/ㄹ 텐데` expresses an assumption while providing background information or contrast. It translates to "I'm sure [A], but/so [B]."
> **같이 가면 좋을 텐데 아쉽네요.** / It would be good if we go together, but it's too bad.
> **이미 바쁠 텐데 무리하지 마세요.** / I'm sure you're already busy, so don't overdo it.
