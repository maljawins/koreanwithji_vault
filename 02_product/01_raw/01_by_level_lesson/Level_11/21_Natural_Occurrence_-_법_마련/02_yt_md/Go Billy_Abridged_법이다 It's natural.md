### The Word 법 and Expressions of Unfairness
The word 법 comes from the Chinese character 法, which means "law" or "method." While it is used in grammar patterns, it can also stand alone in idiomatic expressions. The instructor demonstrates this with the phrase 이런 법이 어디 있어요?, which literally translates to "Where is this kind of law?" This is used when someone feels a situation is highly unfair or disagrees strongly with a rule. For example, if someone says 저만 들어갈 수 없다니 (To think that only I can't go in), the response 이런 법이 어디 있어요? functions like saying "Says who?" or "That's so unfair!"

---

### Expressing Natural Laws with 법이다
The primary grammar form taught is ~는 법이다. To construct this, you take a verb (action or descriptive) and change it into its present tense adjective form before adding 법이다. For example, the verb 하다 (to do) becomes 하는 법이다. This structure does not refer to legal statutes but rather to "natural laws" or universal truths. It is used to state that something is naturally or obviously a certain way because of the nature of reality.

The instructor notes several usage rules for this form. First, to make it negative, you should not change 이다 to 아니다; instead, you must make the preceding verb negative using ~지 않다. For an action verb, this results in ~지 않는 법이다, and for a descriptive verb, it becomes ~지 않은 법이다. Second, because it describes a state of fact or law, it cannot be used in commands or suggestions. Finally, while it can technically be used in the past tense, it is used in the present tense 99% of the time because it describes ongoing universal truths.

Example sentences provided on screen include:
> **사람은 쉽게 변하지 않는 법이에요.** / People don't change easily (and that's a natural law).
> **좋아하는 음식도 매일 먹으면 질리는 법이에요.** / Even if it's food you like, if you eat it every day, you're bound to get sick of it.
> **기대가 없으면 실망도 없는 법이에요.** / If there are no expectations, there is no disappointment.

The instructor also shares a proverb-like example: 윗물이 맑아야 아래 물이 맑은 법이지요, which means the "upper water" must be clear for the "lower water" to be clear, similar to the English sentiment that "a fish rots from the head down."

---

### Related Forms: 법도 하다 and 마련이다
The instructor introduces two variations. The first is ~는 법도 하다. This is used when the speaker is less confident about a universal truth and wants to express a strong possibility or likelihood instead. It translates roughly to "It is likely that..." or "It could well be that..."

The second related form is ~기 마련이다. This is constructed by taking a verb stem and adding ~기 마련이다 (or occasionally ~게 마련이다). This form is functionally equivalent to ~는 법이다 but carries a slightly less "fancy" or formal tone. It feels more like saying something is "obvious" or "bound to happen." One specific restriction for 마련이다 is that it cannot be used with "must" forms like ~야 하다 or ~야 되다; if you need to express that it is a universal fact that someone *must* do something, you must use 법이다 instead.

Examples for 마련이다 include:
> **힘든 운동도 계속하면 익숙해지기 마련이에요.** / Of course, if you keep doing difficult exercise, you'll get used to it.
> **너무 오래 참으면 언젠가 터지기 마련이에요.** / If you hold it in too long, it's obviously going to explode someday.
