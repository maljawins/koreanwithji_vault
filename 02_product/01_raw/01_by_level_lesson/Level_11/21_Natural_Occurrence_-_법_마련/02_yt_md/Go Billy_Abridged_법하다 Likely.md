## Teaching Points

### Overview

The instructor introduces an advanced but common grammar pattern used to express likelihood, assumptions, and natural outcomes. The lesson centers on the auxiliary adjective 법하다, but to understand it fully, the instructor first reviews the foundational form 법이다. By breaking down the components—the noun 법 (law/way) and the verbs 이다 (to be) or 하다 (to seem/be)—learners can see how these patterns describe the "law of the way things are." The learning arc moves from stating established facts and natural laws with 법이다 to making logical assumptions and expressing likelihood with 법하다 and its variation 법도하다.

---

### Prerequisite: The Fact-Stating 법이다

Before diving into the main topic, the instructor reviews 법이다, which is used to state that something is a natural fact or an inevitable law of nature. The word 법 comes from a Hanja root meaning "law," but in this context, it refers to the "way" or "method" things naturally occur. When combined with 이다 (to be), the expression literally translates to "it is the law that..." or "it is the way that..."

To form this, a verb is changed into a present-tense adjective form using ~는 and attached to 법이다. The instructor emphasizes that this specific form is only used with present-tense adjectives because it describes a general, unchanging truth or a natural principle. For example, using the verb 하다 (to do), the construction becomes 하는 법이다. This pattern is used to say "It is only natural that..." or "It is a fact that..."

The instructor provides two key example sentences to illustrate this "natural law" nuance:
> **사람은 쉽게 변하지 않는 법이에요.** / It's a fact that people don't change easily. (Literally: It is the law that a person does not change easily.)
> **자신을 사랑해야 남도 사랑할 수 있는 법입니다.** / It's natural that you have to love yourself in order to be able to love others.

---

### Expressing Likelihood with 법하다

The main focus of the lesson is 법하다. While 법 still means "law" or "way," the ending 하다 here functions like the 하다 found in descriptive verbs (like 피곤하다), carrying a nuance of "to seem" or "to be in such a state." Therefore, 법하다 expresses that something "seems likely to be the way things are."

Unlike the present-tense requirement of 법이다, 법하다 is used with future-tense adjectives (~을/ㄹ) or "past-future" adjectives (~었을/았을/였을). The instructor explains that the future-tense adjective 할 법하다 means something "likely will do" or "ought to be," while the past-future form 했을 법하다 means something "would have done" or "likely did."

The instructor suggests four ways to translate this naturally into English: "ought to," "likely will," "must be," or—most helpfully—"it makes sense that." This pattern is used when the speaker makes a logical assumption based on a situation. For instance, if someone lives in the same neighborhood, it makes sense that you would see them often.

Examples shown on screen include:
> **철수는 한 동네 사니 쉽게 볼 수 있을 법해요.** / It makes sense that you'd be able to see Chul-su easily because he lives in the same neighborhood.
> **잘 모르면 그렇게 생각할 법해요.** / It makes sense that if you don't really know much about it, you could think that.
> **한국 음식만 먹었으니 미국 음식이 생각날 법해요.** / It makes sense that you're going to think about American food because you've only been eating Korean food.
> **다르게 이해하는 분도 있을 법해요.** / It makes sense that there would also be people who understand it differently.

A pronunciation note is provided: because 법 ends in ㅂ and is followed by ㅎ, the sound becomes a strong aspirated ㅍ, resulting in the pronunciation [버패요].

---

### Softening the Assumption with 법도하다

The final variation introduced is 법도하다. By adding the particle 도 (also/too), the speaker acknowledges that "there is also the possibility" or "it would also make sense" for something to happen. The instructor notes that this form is slightly less confident than 법하다. It suggests that while the outcome didn't happen or is just one possibility, it would have been perfectly logical if it did.

This form is frequently used with the contrastive ending ~ㄴ데/은데 to show that while something was likely or "should have" happened, the actual result was different. The instructor demonstrates this with several scenarios:
> **눈이 올 법도 한데 비만 왔어요.** / It would make sense for it to snow, but it only rained.
> **한 번만 더 했으면 성공했을 법도 한데 아쉽네요.** / If I'd tried just once more, I likely would have succeeded, but it's too bad.
> **지나가면서 봤을 법도 한데 기억이 안 나요.** / It would make sense that I saw them while passing by, but I can't remember.

The instructor concludes by reminding students that since 법하다 and 법도하다 are descriptive verbs, they conjugate as 한데 (not 하는데) when used in the middle of a sentence to provide background or contrast.
