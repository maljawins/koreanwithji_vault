### Basic "Or" Connectors: 아니면, 또는, 혹은
The simplest way to offer a choice between multiple options is by using the word 아니면. This word is derived from the verb 아니다 (to not be) combined with the ~(으)면 (if) form, literally meaning "if not." It implies "if not that, then something else." 아니면 is versatile and can be used between nouns or between full sentences. However, it is strictly used when one of the choices will be picked; it is not used if the choice doesn't matter to the speaker.

> **김밥 아니면 김치찌개를 먹을래요?** / Do you want to eat kimbap or kimchi stew?
> **오늘 갈까요? 아니면 내일 갈까요?** / Should we go today or should we go tomorrow?

Alternatively, 또는 can be used similarly to 아니면 between nouns, but it cannot be used between sentences. 또는 is more common in writing than in speech and is less frequent in everyday conversation than 아니면. Similarly, 혹은 is used in the same way as 또는 but is typically reserved for formal and official writing.

---

### Negative Connectors: 아니고, 말고, 아니라
When expressing "not A, but B," Korean uses different forms based on the intent. 아니고 comes from 아니다 (to not be) and is used for correcting a fact or simply stating that something is not the case. 말고 comes from 말다 (to stop/not do) and is used when choosing one thing over another.

> **이건 떡이 아니고 빵이에요.** / This is bread, not rice cake. (Correcting a fact)
> **떡 말고 빵을 주세요.** / Give me bread, not rice cake. (Making a choice)

The form 아니라 also comes from 아니다 but adds more contrast than 아니고. It sounds like the speaker is explicitly correcting the listener and is often used when explaining a situation. While 아니고 simply connects information ("it is not, and..."), 아니라 emphasizes the correction ("it's not A, it's actually B").

---

### Indifferent "Or": (이)나 and 거나
The particle (이)나 is used between two nouns when the specific choice does not matter to the speaker. It carries the nuance of "or something" or "or whatever." To use it, attach 이나 to a noun ending in a consonant and 나 to a noun ending in a vowel. Unlike 아니면, 이나 can be used in regular statements and even when there is only one choice mentioned, implying "I'll just do [this] or something."

> **아이스크림이나 케이크 먹을래요?** / Do you want to eat ice cream or cake or something?
> **피자나 먹을래요.** / I want to eat pizza or something.

For connecting verbs with the same sense of indifference, the connector 거나 is used. It is attached directly to the verb stem. Unlike 이나, 거나 is generally used when there are multiple choices presented.

---

### Instead of: 대신(에) and 는 것 대신(에)
The word 대신 literally means "replace" or "exchange." To say "instead of [Noun]," you place 대신(에) after the noun. To say "instead of [Verb]ing," you attach ~는 대신(에) to the action verb stem. If the verb stem ends in ㄹ, the ㄹ is dropped (e.g., 놀다 becomes 노는 대신).

> **치킨 대신에 피자를 먹었어요.** / I ate pizza instead of chicken.
> **공부하는 대신에 영화를 보러 갔어요.** / Instead of studying, I went to watch a movie.

An alternative for verbs is using the nominalized form ~는 것 대신(에), which may sound more natural to some speakers. Additionally, 대신(에) can mean "in exchange for" or "in return for" when used after action verbs in either the present or past tense. It can even be used with descriptive verbs to mean "despite being [Adjective]," though this is less common.

---

### Besides and Except: 밖에, 외에, 빼고
To express "besides" or "except," Korean offers three main options. 밖에 is used after a noun and must be followed by the negative verb 없다 (to not exist). 외에 is more flexible and does not require a negative verb. 빼고 comes from the verb 빼다 (to remove) and is the most informal option, literally meaning "removing [Noun]."

> **라면밖에는 먹을 게 없어요.** / Besides ramen, there's nothing to eat.
> **그 외에 또 뭘 알아야 해요?** / Besides that, what else do I have to know?
> **매운 음식 빼고 다 잘 먹어요.** / Besides spicy food, I can eat everything well.
