## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the versatile word 듯, which serves as the foundation for several Korean expressions meaning "like," "as if," or "it seems." The lesson is structured to move from simple comparative markers to more complex auxiliary verbs that express a speaker's guess or assumption. By the end of the session, learners will understand the nuance between using 듯(이) for direct metaphors and using 듯하다 or 듯싶다 to express subjective thoughts.

The instructor emphasizes that while these forms are often interchangeable with more common expressions like 것 같다, they carry a more formal or "fancy" tone. The lesson requires a prerequisite understanding of verb conjugation in all tenses and the ability to transform action verbs into adjective forms (noun-modifying forms).

---

### The Comparative Marker 듯(이)
The most basic usage involves taking an action verb stem, changing it into an adjective form (using 는, ㄴ/은, or ㄹ/을), and attaching 듯 or 듯이. While 듯 is common in writing, 듯이 is more frequently heard in spoken Korean. This construction indicates that one action or state is similar to, almost the same as, or "as if" it were another. It represents the speaker's guess or subjective comparison rather than a 100% certain fact.

Visually, the instructor represents this as "A 듯 B," where B is the action being performed and A is the state it resembles. For example, to describe laughing "like crazy," one uses the past tense adjective form of 미치다 (to be crazy), resulting in:
> **너무 웃겨서 미친 듯이 웃었어요.** / I laughed like crazy because it was so funny.

Other examples demonstrate different tenses. To express being happy enough to fly, the future tense adjective form of 뛰다 (to fly/jump) is used:
> **선물을 받고 띌 듯이 기뻐했어요.** / He was so happy he could fly after he got the present.

A more poetic or descriptive example involves the expression 날개 돋치다 (to sprout wings) to describe items selling very quickly:
> **날개 돋친 듯 팔려 나갔어요.** / They sold as if they had sprouted wings (they flew off the shelves).

---

### Strong Metaphors with Verb Stem + 듯(이)
A variation of this form involves attaching 듯(이) directly to the verb stem without changing it into an adjective form first. This version is slightly stronger and more direct, often used in established metaphors and idioms to mean "exactly like" or "just as if."

The instructor highlights the common idiom 불 보듯 (as if seeing a fire), which means something is "clear as day" or "obvious."
> **불 보듯 뻔한 결과였어요.** / It was a result that was as clear as day.

Another idiomatic usage involves comparing a frequent action to the regularity of eating meals. If someone lies constantly, a Korean speaker might say:
> **제 동생은 거짓말을 밥 먹듯 해요.** / My younger sibling lies like they eat meals (constantly/habitually).

---

### Expressing Assumptions with 듯하다
When 듯 is combined with the verb 하다, it functions as an auxiliary verb meaning "it seems as if" or "I think." This is very similar to the common ~것 같다 form but sounds more formal. While 것 같다 is used for general thoughts, 듯하다 specifically implies a guess based on appearances or a certain feeling.

The instructor provides a comparison: 쉽지 않을 것 같아요 (I don't think it will be easy) versus 쉽지 않을 듯해요 (It doesn't seem like it will be easy). The latter sounds more sophisticated. Further examples include:
> **나도 왕이 된 듯하다고 생각했어요.** / I also thought that it seemed as if I had become a king.
> **많은 사람들이 마라톤에 참여할 듯해요.** / It seems as if many people will participate in the marathon.
> **바람이 저에게 힘을 내라고 얘기해 주는 듯했어요.** / It seemed as if the wind was telling me to cheer up.

---

### Subjective Wondering with 듯싶다
The final form discussed is Adjective Form + 듯싶다. The verb 싶다 adds a nuance of "wondering" or "thinking to oneself." This form is less common than 듯하다 and expresses an even weaker guess or a more hesitant assumption. It is used when the speaker is less sure and is simply "thinking maybe" something is the case.

A classic example involves the superstitious or intuitive feeling that it will rain based on physical pain:
> **무릎이 아픈 걸 보니 비가 올 듯싶어요.** / Seeing as my knees hurt, I think maybe it's going to rain.

It can also be used to express a tentative timeline or a self-reflective feeling of inadequacy:
> **내일이면 끝날 듯싶어요.** / I'm thinking maybe it'll finish by tomorrow.
> **연습을 많이 했지만 아직 부족한 듯싶어요.** / I practiced a lot, but I still think I'm not good enough.
