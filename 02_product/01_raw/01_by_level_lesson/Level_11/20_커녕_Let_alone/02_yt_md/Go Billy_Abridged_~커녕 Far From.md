## Teaching Points

### Overview
The instructor introduces the grammar form 커녕, which is used to express that something is "far from" happening or "let alone" possible. Although often categorized as an advanced grammar point, the instructor emphasizes that it is very common in daily speech and accessible even to lower-level learners. The lesson covers the basic construction with nouns, the extension to verbs, and a more formal alternative.

The core logic of 커녕 is a double denial. When a speaker uses the structure "A 커녕 B," they are not just saying "no" to A; they are stating that A is so unlikely or impossible that even B—something that is easier, smaller, or more basic than A—is also impossible. The instructor uses the visual of a downward scale to show that if the higher-level item (A) is denied, the lower-level item (B) is certainly denied as well.

---

### Basic Construction with Nouns
To use this form with a noun, you must first attach the topic marker 은 or 는 to the noun. The choice depends on whether the noun ends in a consonant or a vowel: use 은 after a consonant and 는 after a vowel. Once the topic marker is attached, 커녕 is added immediately after with no space.

The instructor demonstrates this on the whiteboard with two examples. For the word 집 (house), which ends in a consonant, the form becomes 집은커녕. For the word 학교 (school), which ends in a vowel, the form becomes 학교는커녕. This structure sets up the first part of the comparison, essentially meaning "Forget [noun]" or "Never mind [noun]."

---

### Meaning and Translation
The instructor explains that 커녕 is used to deny the noun it is attached to, while simultaneously implying that something even "less" than that noun is also being denied. If we think of the noun as 'A', the expression says "not only not A, but not even something less than A."

Common English translations for this form include "far from," "not even," "let alone," "never mind," and "forget." For example, in the sentence "I couldn't eat dinner, let alone lunch," dinner is the "higher" or more expected meal (A), and lunch is the "lower" or more basic meal (B). Using 커녕 emphasizes the extremity of the situation—the speaker was so busy that even the smaller task was impossible.

---

### Sentence Examples with Nouns
The instructor provides full sentence examples to illustrate the "A 커녕 B" logic. In the first example, the speaker is too busy to eat:
> **너무 바빠서 저녁은 커녕 점심도 못 먹었어요.** / I was so busy that, let alone dinner, I couldn't even eat lunch.

Here, dinner (저녁) is the noun attached to 커녕. The speaker is saying that since dinner was out of the question, even lunch (점심), which should have been easier to manage, was also impossible. Note that the second part of the sentence often uses the particle 도 (even/also) to reinforce the comparison.

In the second example, the speaker discusses a lack of time:
> **여행은 커녕 부모님 보러 갈 시간도 없어요.** / Forget a trip, I don't even have time to go see my parents.

A vacation (여행) is a big event requiring lots of time. The speaker is saying that they don't even have the smaller amount of time required to visit parents, so a full trip is obviously impossible.

---

### Construction with Verbs
To use 커녕 with an action, you must first turn the verb into a noun. This is done by taking the verb stem and attaching the nominalizing suffix 기. Because 기 ends in a vowel, you then always attach the topic marker 는, followed by 커녕. The resulting pattern is Verb Stem + 기는커녕.

The instructor notes that this form is very regular with no exceptions. The meaning shifts from "forget [noun]" to "far from [doing]." It describes a situation where instead of doing the expected action, the subject did something else entirely (often the opposite) or couldn't even do a simpler action.

---

### Sentence Examples with Verbs
The instructor provides three examples of the verbal construction. The first involves a door:
> **문을 잠그기는 커녕 활짝 열어놓고 나갔네요.** / Far from locking the door, they left it wide open and went out.

Instead of the expected action of locking (잠그다), the opposite happened—it was left wide open (활짝 열어놓다). The second example describes a weekend:
> **주말에 놀러가기는 커녕 아파서 잠만 잤어요.** / Far from going out to play on the weekend, I was sick and only slept.

The third example uses a comparison of quantity:
> **물고기를 많이 잡기는 커녕 한 마리도 못 잡았어요.** / Far from catching a lot of fish, I couldn't even catch one.

In this case, catching "a lot" (많이 잡다) is the A action, and catching "one" (한 마리) is the B action. Since A didn't happen, even the much smaller B was impossible.

---

### Formal Alternative: 고사하고
The instructor introduces ~고사하고 as a synonym for ~커녕. It functions with the exact same logic: Noun + 은/는 고사하고 or Verb Stem + 기는 고사하고. The primary difference is that 고사하고 requires a space before it, whereas 커녕 is attached directly to the topic marker.

Socially, 고사하고 is more formal and "fancy" sounding. It is much less common in casual conversation but appears frequently in writing or formal speeches. The instructor provides one final example using this form:
> **시험 공부는 고사하고 숙제도 못 했어요.** / Let alone studying for the test, I couldn't even do the homework.

Studying for a test is the larger task (A), and doing homework is the smaller, more immediate task (B). Using 고사하고 makes the statement sound more formal while conveying the same "A is impossible, and even B is impossible" meaning.
